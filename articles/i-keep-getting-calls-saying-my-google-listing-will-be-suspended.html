I Keep Getting Calls Saying My Google Listing Will Be Suspended — Is It Real?

The short answer: almost certainly a scam. The robocall - “final notice,” “Google will label your business permanently closed,” “press 1 to speak with a Google specialist” - is not from Google. Google does not make robocalls to businesses, claiming and verifying your listing is free and takes minutes, and no legitimate call will ever ask you to pay money or hand over your password. The federal government actually sued the people behind these calls and sent refunds to 4,467 businesses that paid. Below: what the call says, the documented case, what pressing 1 costs you, the one rule, how to check your own listing in two minutes, how real Google warnings actually arrive, and how to shrink the flood.

1. What the call says - and why it is designed to scare you

It goes something like this: a recorded voice says it is a "data service provider for Google" giving you a "final notice." If you do not act soon, Google will label your business as "permanently closed." Press 1 to speak with a "Google specialist." That exact script is documented in the Federal Trade Commission's own lawsuit filings - the wording is not an accident, it is engineered to sound like an emergency, because a panicked business owner presses 1 instead of thinking (ftc.gov, FTC press release May 2018; crawled 2026-10-10). The scam works because your Google listing is genuinely important - it is the box with your name, hours, and reviews that shows up when customers search. The callers know you do not want to lose it. That fear is the whole business model.

2. It is not Google - the three proofs

Proof one: Google does not robocall businesses. Google's own guidance is explicit: it does not use robocalls or prerecorded voice messages to contact businesses with threats, pitches, or demands for money (vimm.com summary of Google's guidance; crawled 2026-10-10). The one narrow exception is the automated call that confirms basic info like your business hours - never a threat, never a pitch, and never asking for money (see section 6). Any recorded voice threatening your listing and asking you to pay is, by definition, not Google. Proof two: verification is free and always has been. Claiming and verifying your business listing through Google takes just minutes, and it is free - "and always has been," per the FTC, which sued the people who said otherwise (ftc.gov, May 2018; crawled 2026-10-10). Anyone charging you to "claim and verify" your listing is selling you something you already own. Proof three: Google will never ask for money, your password, or a verification code on one of these calls. Google does not charge businesses to be included in Google Search or Maps, does not offer to manage your profile for you, does not offer to improve your search ranking, and will never ask for your password or verification code (vimm.com; crawled 2026-10-10). A web-agency security writeup puts Google's own help guidance in one line: a legitimate call will not ask you to sign up for a service, pay money, or give personal information (wheatonwebsiteservices.com, updated ~8 days ago; crawled 2026-10-10). And the practical rule of thumb: Google will not call about your Business Profile at all unless YOU already opened a support ticket or started the conversation. If you did not start it, it is not Google (billhartzer.com; crawled 2026-10-10). (This is the same "never arrived through a channel they use" logic as the scare mail - the domain-expiry letter - and the scare email - the hacker-recorded-you email. Different channel, same anatomy.)

3. The documented case - 4,467 businesses paid before the government stepped in

This is not a theory; it is a court record. In 2018 the FTC sued Florida-based Point Break Media and a network of 12 related companies and individuals, saying they had no relationship with Google and that their robocall threat of removal was untruthful (ftc.gov, May 2018; crawled 2026-10-10). The pitch: for a one-time fee of $300 to $700, they would "claim and verify" your listing and give you special keywords for prominent placement. Customers who paid got a follow-up pitch: $949.99 one-time plus $99.99 or $169.99 a month for guaranteed top placements. A federal court ordered the operators to turn over more than $3.3 million (ftc.gov, May 2019 judgment; crawled 2026-10-10), and in August 2020 the FTC mailed refunds totaling more than $700,000 to 4,467 small business owners - an average of $158.32 each (ftc.gov, Aug 2020; crawled 2026-10-10). The judge found, plainly: the operators had no affiliation with Google, and Google does not remove a business from search results or label it permanently closed just because the owner did not claim and verify. The scam did not end with that case - the script is still in circulation - but the case is proof of what the call always was.

4. What pressing 1 actually costs you

The call wants you off the fence and onto a phone with a telemarketer. What they want once you engage, per local-search commentary that tracks these scripts: payment for a service you do not need, access to your Google account, being added as a manager on your Business Profile, or verification details that should never leave your control (billhartzer.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Manager access is the sleeper prize - a stranger with manager access to your listing can change your hours, your phone number, or your website link, and you are now paying someone for the privilege. And do NOT do the polite thing: do not press a key to be taken off their list, and do not ask to be transferred to a removal line. That usually results in MORE unwanted calls, because pressing anything confirms your number is live and answered (sterlingsky.ca; crawled 2026-10-10). The "remove me" prompt is a trap that feeds the list, not the exit from it. (Same discipline as the inbox probes - do not engage the probe. And the access point: a stranger with manager access is an insider now, which is why the access-audit sibling - check who still has access to your property - applies to your Google profile too.)

5. The one rule - and the two-minute check that settles it

The rule is one sentence: hang up, then check your own listing through the front door. Do not use their number, their link, or their callback line - any of those routes run through them. Instead: on your phone or computer, type business.google.com into the browser yourself and sign in. If your listing were suspended or at risk, that is where you would see it - from Google, not from a robocall. Two minutes, definitive, and free. While you are there, glance at the "People and access" section of your profile settings: you should recognize every name on it. If you do not recognize someone - an old marketing company, an ex-employee - remove them. Boring admin, but it closes the door the scammers were asking you to open. (And if the number keeps calling, blocking the number helps a little - but know the numbers are usually spoofed, so the same operation will call back from a different one. Hanging up is the defense that works; blocking is a convenience.)

6. How real Google warnings actually arrive

Knowing the real channel is what makes the fake one obvious. Google's legitimate contact with business owners looks like this: automated calls that confirm basic info like business hours, and usually only AFTER you asked for something - like verifying your listing (myaifrontdesk.com, 2025; crawled 2026-10-10). A real person from Google might call as part of a support ticket YOU opened. Real warnings about your listing arrive as email to the address on your Business Profile account, and as notices inside the business.google.com dashboard - typed and clicked by you, never read to you by a robot. Three things a real Google contact will never do: threaten to delete your profile unless you pay immediately; ask for your password or any verification code by phone; ask for gift cards, wire transfers, or crypto (pagelaunch.co.uk security writeup, ~9 days ago; crawled 2026-10-10). If the contact includes any of those, it is not Google, whatever name the caller ID shows - scammers spoof caller ID as a matter of routine. (Caller-ID spoofing is the phone version of the email spoofing problem - the displayed name proves nothing. And note the direction: in the general impersonation problem strangers pose AS your business to your customers - the impersonation article - while here the scammer poses as Google to YOU.)

7. Shrinking the flood - the four things that actually help

Four moves, in leverage order. First: hang up and never press 1. Still the highest-leverage action - nothing feeds the list like an engaged number. Second: report it. Report unwanted calls to the FTC at donotcall.gov or 1-888-382-1222; file a complaint with the FCC if the calls persist; and report callers pretending to be Google through Google's own webform, with the caller's number and anything they told you (sterlingsky.ca; smartlinksolutions.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Reports are how schemes like Point Break ended up in court. Third: sign up for the National Do Not Call Registry (donotcall.gov). It does not stop criminals - they ignore it - but it cuts the legal telemarketer noise so the scam calls stand out. Fourth: tell your team. The person who answers your business line is the one the robocall reaches. One sentence for them: "If it is a recorded message about our Google listing, hang up - Google does not call us that way." The operation targets small towns, busy owners, and anyone who fears losing their reviews overnight - which is to say, it targets people who have not heard the rule yet. Now you have.

8. What to tell your web person

Send your assistant or web person this: "We are getting scam calls about our Google listing being suspended. Four asks. First, sign in at business.google.com and confirm our listing is live and in good standing - report back what you see. Second, open the People and access section and list every manager and owner on the profile - flag anyone you don't recognize for removal. Third, check that our reviews, hours, and phone number are all intact - if anything was changed, tell me what and when. Fourth, report the scam callers to Google's webform and file an FTC complaint at donotcall.gov with the numbers and what they said. Plain English, and show me the profile screen before you remove anyone." The proof is the screen: listing live, access list clean, no strangers holding manager rights.
