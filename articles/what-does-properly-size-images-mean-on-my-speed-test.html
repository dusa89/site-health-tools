What Does 'Properly Size Images' Mean on My Speed Test?

The honest answer: your website is making visitors download a full-size photo to display a thumbnail. When your speed test flags "Properly size images," it found pictures on your page whose actual file is much bigger than the space they are shown in - a 4,000-pixel photo squeezed into a 400-pixel slot, for example. The visitor's browser downloads the whole thing anyway. Below: what the line is measuring, why it shows up on so many small-business sites, and the three fixes that clear it.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - audit results differ page to page and change when your site changes. The facts below describe how the audit works in general; your own PageSpeed report is the verdict on your site.

What the line is actually measuring

Google's speed test (Lighthouse, the engine inside PageSpeed Insights) looks at every image on your page and compares two numbers: the size of the image file, and the size the image is actually rendered at on the visitor's screen - factoring in sharper phone screens too. If an image could be shrunk to save at least 4 KB (4 KiB) without changing how it looks on the page, it gets flagged. The report then lists each flagged image with two estimates: how many kilobytes you would save, and roughly how many seconds faster the page would load.

This is one of the most common lines on real speed reports - it sits near the top of almost every "most common problems" list, because the cause is so ordinary: somebody uploaded a photo straight from a phone or camera, and nobody resized it.

The 4,000-pixel photo in the 400-pixel frame

Here is what is happening in plain terms. A modern phone takes photos thousands of pixels wide. Your website shows that photo in a slot a few hundred pixels wide - a team headshot, a service image, a banner. When you upload the original straight from the phone, the visitor's browser downloads the ENTIRE original file, then shrinks it down to fit the slot on their screen. You paid for 4,000 pixels of download; the visitor saw 400 pixels of picture.

The part that surprises most owners: shrinking the image with code does not fix this. If your page's HTML or CSS says "display this at 400 pixels wide," the browser still downloads the full 4,000-pixel file first and resizes it afterward. The download - which is what makes the page slow - happens at full size no matter what the display size is. The fix has to happen to the file itself, not to how it is displayed.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Images are the heaviest thing on a typical page. Recent web-wide measurements put the median mobile page at about 2.6 MB, and images alone account for roughly 0.9 MB of that - more than JavaScript, more than fonts, more than any other single category - the heaviest thing on the page. When several images on a page are all oversized, the waste multiplies: five photos at 3 MB each is 15 MB your visitor downloads on a phone connection, most of it pixels nobody will ever see.

And it hits your biggest image hardest. The large photo at the top of your homepage - the one visitors see first - is often the single biggest file on the page, which makes it the biggest lever on the "how fast does this feel" measurement. An oversized hero image is the most expensive version of this problem, which is why our hero-image article pairs with this one.

The three fixes, in order of ease

One: resize before you upload. This is the fix that solves it permanently. Before a photo goes on your site, shrink it to roughly the size it will be displayed - a headshot shown at 300 pixels wide does not need to be 4,000 pixels wide. Free tools do this in seconds, and it is the single highest-return habit in this whole article. This pairs with our photos-slowing-you-down article, which covers the file-weight half of the problem.

Two: let the platform serve multiple sizes. WordPress automatically creates several smaller copies of every image you upload and serves each visitor the copy that fits their screen - that is the "responsive images" feature working behind the scenes. Most page builders and themes have an image-size setting per slot; if yours is set to "full size" everywhere, that is the setting undoing the platform's help. Ask your web person which size each image slot is actually using.

Three: match the format to the job. Sizing and format are two halves of the same image problem - a correctly sized JPEG still weighs more than it should next to a modern format. Our next-gen-formats decoder covers that half, and the animated-GIF piece covers the special case that eats pages alive.

The three traps

Trap one: "I already shrunk it in the editor." See above - display size and file size are different things, and the audit only cares about the file. If the original upload is still the full-resolution photo, the flag stays.

Trap two: sizing for yesterday's screens. Phone screens are sharper than they look - a 400-pixel slot on a modern phone can genuinely benefit from an 800-pixel image. The rule of thumb the pros use: serve about twice the displayed size for sharp screens, not eight times. Twice is crisp; eight times is the audit flag.

Trap three: "fixing" it by lazy-loading the hero image. Lazy-loading is for images BELOW the fold - our lazy-loading explainer covers when it helps. Lazy-loading the big image at the top of the page trades this audit flag for a different one and can make the page feel slower. Size the hero image correctly instead.

What to ask your web person

Four questions that get a straight answer: (1) "Which of my images are being served at full original size - can you show me the flagged list from my speed report?" (2) "What display size is each image slot actually using, and are the files matched to it?" (3) "Is my theme or page builder overriding the platform's automatic smaller copies with 'full size' anywhere?" (4) "After resizing, will you re-run the speed test so I can see the savings number change?"
