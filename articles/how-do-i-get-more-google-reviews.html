How do I get more Google reviews?

You did great work for a customer — the kind worth a five-star review. The customer said they would leave one. They never did. Your competitor has 214 reviews. You have 9. Same town, similar work — and yet the phone rings for them first. This guide is about closing that gap with work you can do this week, for free.

Before the storefront, before the website, before the phone call — there is the review check. BrightLocal’s Local Consumer Review Survey 2026 (1,002 US adults, published February 11, 2026) found that 97 percent of consumers read reviews before choosing a local business, and 41 percent read them every single time they browse, up from 29 percent just a year earlier. Reviews have quietly become the first thing a stranger checks, not the last. Positive reviews make 85 percent of consumers more likely to use a business; negative ones deter 77 percent. And nearly half — 49 percent — now trust the opinions of strangers online as much as a personal recommendation.

One housekeeping note on numbers you will see elsewhere: the famous “92 percent read reviews” figure dates to 2012, and the “88 percent trust them like recommendations” to 2014. The 2026 replacements are 97 percent and 49 percent (cufinder’s 2026 statistics roundup flags the old ones explicitly). Reading went up. Blind trust went down. Both moves make sense in an era of AI-generated fakes — and both are good news for a business with genuine, recent reviews.

Reviews also decide whether Google shows you at all

The same reviews that convince customers also move your ranking. Whitespark’s 2026 expert survey of local search practitioners puts review signals at about 20 percent of the weight behind the Local Pack — those three businesses with the map that capture the lion’s share of local clicks. Businesses with 50 or more Google reviews are 266 percent more likely to appear in the Local Pack than businesses with fewer than 10 (BrightLocal 2026, via searchlab.nl’s 2026 statistics roundup). A Moz study puts the cost of the other side: one negative review on the first page costs roughly 22 percent of potential customers, rising to 59 percent with three or more. And the bar for “good enough” keeps rising — 31 percent of consumers now say they will only use businesses rated 4.5 stars or higher (BrightLocal 2026), and ReviewTrackers’ 2026 data says 3.3 stars is the absolute minimum before customers pick a competitor.

There is also a new angle most owners have not considered: reviews are becoming citation fuel. BrightLocal’s 2026 survey found 42 percent of consumers now trust AI recommendations as much as traditional reviews — and AI assistants read review text when deciding who to name. Detailed reviews describing real work (“replaced our water heater same-day”) are the evidence AI quotes. Our companion guide to AI visibility explains how that game works.

The freshness rule: new reviews beat old ones

Here is the part that surprises most owners. Customers and algorithms both discount old reviews. BrightLocal 2026 found that 73 percent of consumers only pay attention to reviews from the past month, and 74 percent want reviews written within the last three months — with 32 percent wanting them from the last two weeks, up from 20 percent a year ago. A Profit Acuity analysis (July 2026, a vendor study — read it as a directional claim, not gospel) argues reviews under 30 days old carry full algorithmic weight while reviews older than six months retain only a fraction. The practical takeaway does not need the exact decay curve: a steady trickle of fresh reviews beats a tall pile of old ones, and a competitor with 30 recent reviews can outshine your 200 stale ones.

There is one more counterintuitive finding worth knowing: perfect is suspicious. Northwestern University’s Spiegel Research Center work found the sweet spot sits at 4.5 to 4.9 stars — conversion runs about 270 percent higher than a business with no reviews — while a perfect 5.0 converts about 12 percent lower than a 4.5, because customers read perfection as “too good to be true.” A few four-star reviews among the fives are not a problem. They are credibility.

Four free fixes, in order of leverage

1. Ask at the golden moment. The review is a fresh-impression transaction: ask right after the win, in person, while the customer is happy and standing in front of you. “Would you mind leaving us a Google review?” asked at job completion beats a follow-up email by orders of magnitude. Two rules from Google’s own policies: do not offer incentives for reviews (discounts, freebies, entries in drawings — all prohibited), and do not gate — never screen unhappy customers away from the review page while funneling happy ones toward it. Both can get your reviews removed.

2. Make it frictionless. Every step of effort between “yes, I’ll review” and the review box costs you reviews. Get your short Google review link from your Business Profile and make it impossible to miss: a QR code on the invoice, on the truck, on a card you hand over, and a same-day text or email with the link for customers who were not there in person. If it takes more than thirty seconds, most people will mean to do it later and never will.

3. Reply to every single review. This is the highest-return minute you can spend. ReviewTrackers’ 2026 data: businesses that respond to reviews receive 12 percent more reviews on average and carry a 0.12-star higher rating. Replies signal an active, managed business to future customers — and they are additional text attached to your name that Google and AI assistants can read. A simple thank-you with a specific detail beats a copy-paste line.

4. Build the drip, not the pile. Freshness wins, so the goal is a habit, not a campaign. A few reviews a month, every month, beats a one-time push of thirty. Put the ask in your standard close-out: finished the job, walk the customer through it, ask for the review, hand over the QR card. Two months of that cadence will show up in your newest-review dates, and those dates are exactly what customers and algorithms are reading.

The honest check you can run today

Open your Google Business Profile and read it like a customer would. How many reviews do you have versus your three nearest competitors? What is the date of your newest one — this month, or last year? Read your five most recent reviews: are they detailed, or do they just say “great”? Then search your trade and town in a private window and look at what shows up: the stars sit right in the results, before anyone clicks anything. If your competitors’ listings show stars and dates you cannot match, you now know exactly why the phone rings for them first — and you know the fix.
