How fast should my website load?

Your site opens instantly on your office wifi, so you assume it is fine. Then half your mobile visitors leave before anything loads. The truth is there is not one magic number — Google measures three things, and 2025 field data says more than half of websites fail all three on phones.

You have probably been told your website needs to load in under 3 seconds. That number comes from real research — Google and DoubleClick found in 2016 that 53 percent of mobile visitors abandon a page that takes longer than 3 seconds, and every major 2026 performance study still cites it as the benchmark. But 3 seconds is the line where customers give up, not the line Google grades you on. Google has its own scorecard, and it is more specific than any single number.

The three numbers Google actually grades you on

Core Web Vitals are Google’s official measure of real-world speed, and they have been part of the ranking signals since 2021. There are three, and your page only passes when all three are “good” at the same time:

1. Largest Contentful Paint: 2.5 seconds or less. This is how long it takes for the main content of your page — usually the big headline, hero image, or banner — to become visible. Under 2.5 seconds is good. Between 2.5 and 4 seconds needs improvement. Over 4 seconds is poor.

2. Interaction to Next Paint: 200 milliseconds or less. This is how fast the page reacts when a visitor taps a button or a menu. Under 200 milliseconds feels instant; over 500 feels broken. Note: Google replaced the older First Input Delay test with INP in March 2024, and about 43 percent of sites that passed the old test fail the new one — because INP measures every interaction across the whole visit, not just the first one. If your developer last tuned your site’s speed in 2023, the test has moved since then.

3. Cumulative Layout Shift: 0.1 or less. This measures how much the page jumps around while it loads — the thing that makes you tap the wrong button when an image pops in late. A score of 0.1 or less means the layout stays put.

And here is the part most owners miss: Google does not grade your fastest visit or your average visit. It grades the 75th percentile of real visits — meaning the slowest quarter of your actual visitors decides your score. Your fast office connection barely counts.

Why it feels fast to you and slow to your customers

You check your site on your own wifi, on a phone you bought this year, in a browser that has your site cached. That is the kindest possible version of your site. Your customer opens it on 4G in a parking lot on a three-year-old phone, first visit, nothing cached.

The gap is bigger than most owners imagine. The HTTP Archive’s 2025 Web Almanac found that only 48 percent of websites pass all three vitals on mobile — more than half fail. Their lab tests measured the median mobile page needing 7.9 seconds to fully load. Stack that against the 3-second line where 53 percent of mobile visitors leave: the typical site is more than twice over the line. And 83 percent of landing-page visits now come from phones (Unbounce, 2026) — the slow version of your site is the version almost everyone sees.

What each extra second is costing you

Speed is not about pride; it is about money, and the numbers are unusually consistent across industries. Akamai’s Online Retail Performance Report (2017) found every 100 milliseconds of added delay costs about 7 percent in conversions. Amazon’s own testing, published by former engineer Greg Linden, put every 100 milliseconds of latency at roughly 1 percent of sales. Google’s case studies on web.dev are the same story in miniature: Vodafone improved its Largest Contentful Paint by 31 percent and increased sales by 8 percent; the BBC lost an additional 10 percent of users for every extra second its site took to load; Pinterest cut perceived wait times by 40 percent and saw 15 percent more search-engine traffic and sign-ups.

You do not need Amazon’s traffic for this to hurt. A local business site with 1,000 visitors a month that converts 3 percent of them gets 30 leads. A site that slow bleeds even half a second to its competitors can lose several of those leads every month — quietly, forever, with no error message anywhere to tell you.

What to fix first, in plain English

Owners are usually told to fix everything. Do not. Fix in this order, because each one is the most common cause of its failing number.

1. The hero image. The biggest visible thing on your page is usually what decides the 2.5-second LCP grade. One uncompressed 4-megabyte photo at the top of the page can single-handedly fail it. Compress it, serve it at the size it actually displays, and this one fix wins the most common failure — our guide to why photos slow sites down walks through exactly that.

2. The scripts. Chat widgets, pop-up forms, tracking snippets, plugin bloat — everything that loads before the visitor can tap anything — is what kills the 200-millisecond interaction grade. If your site has grown five plugins in two years, that is probably your INP problem.

3. The jumping layout. Images with no dimensions set, pop-ups that drop in late, ads or banners that push content down — reserve the space up front so nothing moves, and the layout-shift grade takes care of itself.

4. Test on the phone, not the computer. Google’s free PageSpeed Insights test shows your mobile and desktop scores separately — and scores you harder on mobile on purpose, because that is where your customers are. Our companion guide on mobile speed explains why the mobile number is the one Google ranks you on.

The honest part nobody tells you

First: speed is a tiebreaker, not a jackpot. Google’s own people describe the vitals as the decider between pages that are already close — similar content, similar reputation. A fast site with weak content does not outrank a slow site with great content. Do not pay anyone who promises a ranking jump from a speed score alone.

Second: test more than once. The same page can score differently through the day as servers, networks, and ad auctions shift — a single red number is a hint, not a verdict.

Third: Google shows you two kinds of numbers — a lab test and real-user field data — and it ranks you on the real users. The field data sits right in the free PageSpeed Insights report under “field data,” collected from actual Chrome users over the previous 28 days. If you want the broader story of how much speed really affects your Google rankings, that is where the nuance lives.
