Why Does Gmail Clip My Newsletter? ('Message Clipped' Explained)

You send a newsletter you are proud of, and half your Gmail readers only see part of it. The rest hides behind a link that says [Message clipped] - View entire message. Nothing is broken on your end. Gmail simply has a size rule, and your email is over it. Here is what the rule counts, what makes emails too heavy, and the fixes that get the whole message seen.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-12 - not promises about your situation. Sending platforms differ, and your email will look different from anyone else's. The size rule is the same either way.

What you are seeing

When an email's HTML is bigger than about 102 kilobytes, Gmail shows the first 102 KB and cuts the rest. The reader gets a "[Message clipped] View entire message" link and has to click it to read the rest of the email. Many readers never click it. They see part of your message, assume that is the whole thing, and move on. Anything you put after the cut - the offer, the coupon, the "call us" button - effectively does not exist for them. (This is a display problem, not a delivery one: our promotions-tab article covers the case where the message never reaches the inbox at all.)

The 102 KB rule counts code, not photos

This is the part that surprises people. The limit measures the weight of the HTML code: your text, the HTML tags and tables, the inline CSS, the link URLs, the tracking code. It does NOT count your image files. Gmail loads images from external servers, so only the image URL - a few bytes - counts toward the limit. You can shrink your photos to thumbnails and your email will still be clipped if the code underneath is bloated. The rule of thumb several sending platforms give is to stay under 100 KB of code weight for a comfortable margin.

What makes an email heavy

The weight comes from how the email is built, not how long it looks. The usual suspects: nested tables (email layouts are built from tables, and every level adds code), inline CSS repeated on hundreds of cells, HTML comments left behind by the editor, extra whitespace and line breaks, long tracking parameters glued onto every link, and - the big one for busy owners - text pasted straight from Word or Google Docs, which drags in hidden formatting code. Paste with Ctrl+Shift+V (or Option+Shift+Cmd+V on a Mac) to paste plain text instead. Hidden content blocks that only show on desktop or only on mobile add weight too, as does the unsubscribe code and the open-rate counter. None of this is visible when you preview the email - you have to look at the code weight itself.

The testing trap

Sometimes the clipping is an illusion. Gmail groups emails with identical subject lines into one conversation thread. When you send yourself five test copies with the same subject, Gmail collapses the duplicates and clips the repeated content - and your short, fine email looks broken. Change the subject line on each test send, or turn off Conversation view in Gmail settings, before concluding your email is too heavy.

What clipping quietly costs you

Three costs that do not show up in your preview. First, the unsubscribe link usually sits in the footer - exactly the part that gets cut. A reader who wants out and cannot find the link reaches for the spam button instead, and spam complaints poison your sender reputation. (Our sudden-spam article covers what happens after complaints climb.) Second, the open-tracking pixel sits near the bottom too, so clipped emails register fewer opens than they actually get - your reports understate how many people really saw the message. (Our open-rates article covers the other reasons open counts lie.) Third, on the Gmail phone app a clipped message loses its mobile layout and renders the desktop view instead - small text, side-scrolling, the works. (Our broken-rendering article covers the other ways email layouts fall apart on customers' screens.) Contact-form replies have the same anatomy at a smaller scale - our contact-form article walks through that one.

How to slim it down

Start by measuring. Send yourself a test, open it in Gmail, use the three-dot menu and choose "Download message," then check the file size of the downloaded email. (This reads a little high because it includes headers, which is fine - it errs on the safe side.) If the code weight is over 102 KB, work the fixes in this order: cut the markup first (flatten nested tables, strip HTML comments, delete styles that do not change how anything looks); consolidate repeated inline CSS instead of pasting the same declaration on 200 cells; shorten your URLs and tracking parameters; drop images you do not need (fewer images means fewer tags, even though the image files themselves do not count); and move long content behind a "Read more" link to your site instead of pasting the whole article in. Also watch for accented and special characters (the copyright symbol, é, and friends): they can inflate the size through character encoding, and using HTML entities for them keeps the weight down. (One more thing worth checking while you are in here: whether your newsletter should come from your business email address at all - the wrong sending tool makes every other fix harder.)

The bottom line

Gmail's 102 KB limit is not a suggestion and it applies no matter which platform sends your email - there is no setting that turns it off. But it is a code-weight limit, not a content limit: a long, rich newsletter written in clean code sails under it, while a short one pasted from Word can trip it. Measure your weight, cut the markup first, and put your offer and your unsubscribe link high enough that a clipped reader still sees them. Your newsletter should not need a second click to do its job.
