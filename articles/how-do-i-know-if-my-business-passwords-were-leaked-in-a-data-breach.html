How Do I Know If My Business Passwords Were Leaked in a Data Breach?

The honest answer: you can check for free, in about two minutes - and you almost certainly should. A free lookup run by security researcher Troy Hunt lets you type in your business email addresses and see exactly which known breaches they appear in. Below: how the check works and why it is safe to use, why a password you set in 2014 can still open doors for attackers today, the free breach checks already sitting on your phone and browser, and the fix order - change it here, change it everywhere you reused it, turn on the second lock.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your site.

The verdict first: yes - check, don't guess

Here is the tool: Have I Been Pwned (haveibeenpwned.com), a free breach lookup created by Australian security researcher Troy Hunt and running since 2013. You type in one email address - no sign-in, no payment - and it tells you which known breaches that address appeared in and what kind of data each one exposed: names, phone numbers, passwords, and so on. The scale is hard to picture: the homepage currently lists more than 17 billion pwned accounts across 1,000+ breached websites - and a count of its public list found 108 breaches added between January 1 and October 10, 2026 alone, covering about 539.8 million accounts, with roughly 20 of them including passwords. That pace is the verdict: checking is not paranoia, it is maintenance. Run every business email address through it - current ones, old ones, and shared ones like info@ that people forgot existed.

How the check works - and why it is safe to use

Three honest mechanics, because the natural question is "wait, am I handing my password to a stranger?"

The email check never shows your actual data. It only reports which breaches your address appeared in and what types of data each one exposed. Nobody sees your password or your address contents - that is exactly the right amount of information to decide what to do next.

The password check never sends your password. The site hashes the password on your own device before checking it, so the real password never leaves your device.

Some breaches are owner-only. Breaches involving sensitive data are hidden from public search and shown only to the verified owner of the address - so verify your addresses to see the complete picture, and switch on the free "Notify me" option, which emails you when your address appears in a future breach. That is your ongoing watch, free forever.

Why an old leak still endangers your business today

The attack has a name: credential stuffing. An attacker takes a leaked email-and-password pair and has software try it on every other site - banks, email, shopping, your website admin - on the bet that you reused it. One of the visible signs is password-reset emails you never asked for - someone has your address and is interested. A breach at some forum you joined years ago can come back to bite your email or bank account today, especially with reuse. The industry numbers say this is the main way in: Verizon's 2025 breach report, covering 22,000+ incidents and 12,195 confirmed breaches across 139 countries, found stolen credentials were still the most common first step into a breach - 22% - and that 88% of basic web-application break-ins used stolen credentials.

There is also a second supply line most people miss. In February 2026, Hunt loaded 244 million new passwords and 284 million email addresses harvested by infostealer malware - malicious software that steals the passwords saved in your browser and computer - pulled from 1.5 terabytes of stealer logs shared on Telegram. Which means even a password you never typed into a breached website can end up on one of these lists. One honest shift in the same reporting: for most people this year, the bigger risk from a breach is not even someone having your password - it is a scammer having your real name, phone number and email, which makes a fake message look convincing. That is the same breach data feeding the phishing your staff keeps clicking - our phishing article explains the other half.

The three traps

Trap one: "the check said clean, so we are safe." The lookup only covers breaches whose data has been found and loaded. A clean result is good news, not a guarantee - keep the Notify me alert on, because the next breach is coming.

Trap two: checking your personal email and skipping the business ones. Check every address tied to the business: current staff addresses, old addresses still linked to bank, shopping and social accounts, shared logins, and the old assistant's address nobody deactivated. The forgotten addresses are where the leaks hide - our article on old developer access covers the account side of the same problem.

Trap three: typing your real current password into a strange "check my password" website. Use only checks that hash locally (the one above does) or the built-in browser checks below. And never pay any site a fee to "file" or "recover" for you - legitimate checks are free.

The free checks already sitting on your devices

The email lookup covers addresses. For the passwords themselves, you probably already own the checker:

Chrome or Android: go to passwords.google.com, choose Go to Password Checkup, then Check passwords. It flags any saved password that has appeared in a breach, plus weak and reused ones. You can also switch on Chrome's setting to warn you when a saved password shows up in a breach.

iPhone, iPad or Mac: open the Passwords app and tap Security - it lists passwords that are weak, reused, or have appeared in data leaks. Switch on "Detect Compromised Passwords" for alerts about new leaks.

Already using a password manager: look for a section called Watchtower, Security, or Health - most run the same check. Mozilla Monitor is the named alternative with ongoing monitoring.

What to tell your web/IT person

Send your assistant, web person, or IT contact this: “Check whether our business credentials have leaked. Four asks. First, run every business email address - current, old, and shared - through haveibeenpwned.com and switch on the free Notify me alert on each one. Second, run the built-in breach report on everyone's devices: Google's Password Checkup for the Chrome users, the iPhone Passwords Security tab for the Apple users, or the password manager's security report. Third, work the results in this order: change the exposed password on the breached account first, then change it everywhere else it was reused; turn on two-factor authentication on the important accounts - our two-factor article explains why; log out of all active sessions; and check those accounts for anything odd. Start with email, because a hacked email account can take over the website too - and do not skip the shared logins; shared passwords widen every leak. Fourth, report back to me: which addresses appeared in which breaches, which passwords you changed, and which accounts now have two-factor on.” The proof is the follow-up check coming back clean - and the Notify me alerts staying quiet.
