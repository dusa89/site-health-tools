How Do I Stop Spam From Flooding My Business Email?

Crypto pitches, fake invoices, "SEO experts" you've never heard of — arriving at 3am to the email address your customers use. Nearly half of all email on earth is spam, and a business address is a magnet for it. The good news: most of the flood is controlled by four habits, not four purchases. Here is what actually works, what quietly makes it worse, and how to stop feeding the harvesters.

Every other article in our email section is about your mail going to spam — deliverability, blacklists, the promotions tab. If that's your problem, start with why your emails suddenly go to spam. This guide is the reverse direction: spam coming into your business inbox. Different problem, different fix. If you open your work email to 40 junk messages every morning, read on.

The short answer: report spam instead of deleting it, never click "unsubscribe" on mail you didn't ask for, build a couple of filters, and stop publishing your address where harvesters live. The four rules below cut most of the flood for free. What they cannot fix: spam you are unknowingly inviting — published addresses, catch-all inboxes, and signups you forgot about.

Rule 1: report spam, don't just delete it

Deleting a spam message removes it from your sight. Reporting it removes it from everyone's sight. When you click "Report spam" (or "Mark as junk"), your provider's filter learns — Gmail, Outlook, and every serious provider feed user reports into their global filters. The North Carolina Department of Justice's anti-spam guidance says exactly this: report it, usually via the built-in report button, and many providers simplify the whole job to that one click.

This matters because of scale: nearly 48% of the world's ~392.5 billion daily emails are spam — about 188 billion junk messages a day (cleverly.co, 2026). Nobody's filter catches all of it by default; the filters improve precisely because users report what slips through. Deleting quietly is polite. Reporting is what actually shrinks the flood.

Rule 2: never "unsubscribe" from spam you didn't sign up for

This is the counterintuitive one, and the one most people get backwards. A legitimate newsletter — the store you bought from, the list you joined — has a real unsubscribe link that works. A spammer's "unsubscribe" link is a trap. Clicking it does one thing reliably: it confirms your address is live, monitored, and owned by a real person. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) advises that if a message looks like phishing, you should not reply or click any link — including the unsubscribe link. The North Carolina DOJ says the same thing in plainer words: if you respond to a real spammer, they learn the account is active and you get even more spam.

The rule of thumb: unsubscribe only from senders you recognize and chose. Anything else gets "Report spam" or "Block sender." Blocking sends future mail from that address straight to spam, and unlike unsubscribing, it never tells the sender you're there.

Rule 3: turn on filters (the two-minute setup)

Every mailbox provider lets you build rules that act on mail before you see it. The highest-value filter for a business inbox: route mail from unknown addresses containing the spam words your business keeps getting ("crypto," "SEO audit," "funding") into a quarantine folder or straight to spam. The DOJ's guidance names filters as one of the core tools, and both Gmail and Outlook let you create one from the message's menu (the three dots or "More" menu → "Create filter") in under a minute.

A second filter worth building: invoice and payment language. Fake invoices and "payment failed" alerts are among the most common phishing lures — email was the contact method in 25% of fraud reports where a contact method was identified (FTC Consumer Sentinel Network Data Book 2024). A filter that flags mail containing "invoice attached" or "wire transfer" from addresses outside your known vendors puts every fake bill in one place for a 10-second daily review.

Rule 4: stop feeding the harvesters (your address is public in more places than you think)

Spam starts with your address being known. Spammers harvest addresses from exactly the places businesses publish them:

  • Your website's contact page. An "info@yourdomain.com" in plain text is harvested within days of a site going live. The fix is a contact form, not a published address — forms can't be scraped the way text can. (This also quietly fixes the contact-form spam problem our security guide covers.)
  • Catch-all inboxes. If your mail is set to accept everything@yourdomain.com, every typo and every dictionary-guess address lands in your inbox. Turn off the catch-all; use a handful of real addresses instead.
  • Role addresses you share everywhere. The same "info@" printed on your truck, your invoices, and your Google Business Profile goes into every list your business ever touches. Use separate addresses for signups and trials — when one starts getting spam, you know exactly who leaked it, and you can retire it without touching your real contact address.
  • Signups you forgot. Be careful what you sign up for, as the DOJ puts it: every "create an account" form is a future mailing list. Once a quarter, search your inbox for "unsubscribe" from senders you recognize and actually unsubscribe from the dead ones.

The one kind nothing technical stops

Filters, reporting, and address hygiene kill bulk spam — the mass-sent, spray-and-pray kind. They do not kill targeted mail: the email that names you, your business, and your vendor, and asks for a payment or a password. That is not a spam problem; it is a fraud problem, and the defense is procedural, not technical: verify by a second channel. If "your vendor" emails new bank details, you call the vendor at a number you already had — never from the email. No filter replaces that habit, and no amount of spam reduction does either.

The team rule card (copy-paste)

If anyone else touches this inbox, send them these four lines:

"(1) Report spam — don't just delete it. (2) Never unsubscribe from mail we didn't sign up for — block or report it instead. (3) Don't reply to junk, ever; replies tell spammers we're alive. (4) Anything about invoices, payments, or passwords gets verified by phone before we act on it."

The bottom line

Inbound spam is mostly a habits problem: report instead of delete, unsubscribe only from the legitimate, filter the patterns, and stop publishing the address that gets harvested. That handles the flood. What it doesn't handle is the email that knows your name — and that one is defeated by a phone call, not a filter.

Numbers cited: ~48% spam of 392.5B daily emails via cleverly.co (2026); email as contact method in 25% of fraud reports via FTC Consumer Sentinel Network Data Book 2024 (via totaldefense.com); CISA "don't click unsubscribe on phishing" guidance (via totaldefense.com, 2026); report/filter/unsubscribe-from-legitimate-only guidance via North Carolina Dept. of Justice (standing guidance). Figures describe industry measurements, not guarantees for your inbox — your own report-and-filter discipline determines your result.
