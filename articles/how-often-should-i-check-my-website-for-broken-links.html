How Often Should I Check My Website for Broken Links?

Your website is rotting slowly, and nobody sends you a memo about it. Pages you link to get moved, renamed, or deleted. Pages you renamed leave dead ends behind. Seasonal pages point at offers that expired months ago. Nobody notices one dead link. They notice the fifth — and by then they've decided your business is sloppy. The fix is not a project. It's a schedule: 10 minutes, 4 times a year, plus an immediate check after 3 specific triggers.

If you're wondering why links keep dying in the first place, read our plain-English guide to why links break first — this guide is the other half: the maintenance schedule that keeps them from piling up. And if you want to see what you're dealing with right now, our guide to finding the dead links already on your site walks through the cost angle.

The short answer: check quarterly at minimum. Check monthly if you add or change content regularly. And check immediately — the same day — after any redesign, migration, or big content change. Why links die on a schedule

Two different clocks are running. The first is your clock: every time you rename a page, delete an old service listing, or redesign the site, you create dead ends — links from your other pages, your Google Business Profile, and other people's sites that pointed at the old address. The second is everyone else's clock: the sites you link out to — suppliers, review platforms, news articles, directories — move, delete, and redesign on their own schedules. You changed nothing. The internet moved under you anyway. That's why "I just built this site last year, it should be fine" is the sentence we hear most, right before the check finds 11 dead links.

The 3 triggers that demand an immediate check

Don't wait for the quarter on these — check the same day:

  • 1. Any redesign, migration, or platform switch. Moving from one website builder to another, or even a theme change, is the single biggest link-killer there is. Page addresses change silently, and every old address — on your site, on Google, on other people's sites — now leads nowhere. Check before you announce the new site, not after.
  • 2. Renaming or deleting any page. That "Services" page you renamed to "What We Do"? Every link to the old name just broke. If the page is gone for good, the old address should redirect to the replacement — one redirect catches every stale link at once, including the ones on sites you'll never find. (Your web person can set this up in minutes; ask for a "301 redirect from the old URL to the new one.")
  • 3. Seasonal or event content expiring. Holiday promotions, event announcements, limited-time offers — pages with a built-in expiry date. Their links die the fastest because the target dies on purpose. When the event ends, either redirect the page or remove the links pointing at it.

The quarterly 10-minute routine

Put it on the calendar next to your quarterly tax estimate — same discipline, smaller bill:

  • Minute 0–1: run the check. Our free broken-link check scans your site's links and lists the dead ones. About 10 seconds, no signup.
  • Minute 1–10: decide each link's fate. Every broken link gets one of 3 verdicts, and the decision takes seconds once you know the rule: fix it if the page moved and you know the new address (update the link); redirect it if other sites or Google still point at an old address of yours (the old URL should send visitors to the right place); remove it if the target is gone for good and nothing replaces it. A link to a dead supplier page helps no one — delete it.

That's the whole routine. Most quarters you'll find 0 to 3. The quarter after a redesign, you'll find more — which is exactly why the trigger list above exists.

What about links on other sites pointing at you?

You can't fix someone else's website. What you can do is make their stale links harmless: whenever you rename or delete one of your pages, set up a redirect from the old address to the closest live page. One redirect quietly rescues every outdated link on Google, on directories, and on other people's sites — including the ones you'll never know about. This is the highest-leverage 5 minutes in website maintenance, and almost nobody does it.

The bottom line

Broken links are not a disaster; they're decay. Decay is beaten by a schedule, not by a heroic cleanup once every 3 years. Quarterly checks, immediate checks after the 3 triggers, and a redirect every time you rename a page — that's the whole system, about an hour a year. Your customers will never notice you doing it. They'll only notice if you don't.

No statistics are cited in this guide: link-rot rates vary wildly by site type and no honest industry-wide figure exists for small-business sites.
