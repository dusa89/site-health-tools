Why Am I Getting Password-Reset Emails I Didn't Ask For?

You didn't forget your password. You didn't click "forgot password." But there it is in your inbox: "Reset your password." Your stomach drops, because the obvious reading is that someone is inside your account right now. Here is the calmer truth: receiving a password-reset email does not mean you were hacked. Almost anyone who knows your email address can trigger one. But it does mean someone — or something — has your address and is interested in your account, and it deserves five focused minutes, not panic and not the delete button. Here is exactly what to do, in order.

Start with the fact that reframes everything: a password-reset email is not proof that someone broke in. On nearly every major service, the "forgot password" form asks for nothing but an email address or username — both things that are often public. Type in someone's address, and the service dutifully sends them a reset email. The email proves one thing only: somebody entered your address into a password-reset form. That somebody might be an attacker testing whether your account is worth attacking. It might be automated software working through a list of leaked addresses. It might be a stranger who shares a similar address and fat-fingered yours. And sometimes the email itself is the attack — a fake reset email designed to scare you into clicking a link that steals your login.

So the email is a signal, not a verdict. Your job is to read the signal correctly, and the first step is the one most people get backwards.

Step zero: do not click the link in the email

This feels wrong — the email is telling you to secure your account, and your instinct is to act fast. Resist it. There are two reasons. First, the email might be fake. Phishing emails dressed up as password resets are one of the oldest tricks running: the panic the message creates is the weapon, and the link is where the damage happens — a lookalike login page that harvests whatever you type. Second, even when the email is genuine, clicking a link in a moment of panic trains exactly the reflex attackers count on. The safe habit, every security guide agrees on, is boring: open the account through its official app, or type the website address into your browser yourself — never through the email. Then look at what is actually going on inside the account.

The 5-minute check, in order

Once you are inside the account directly (not via the email), work through this list. It takes about five minutes and it answers the only question that matters: is someone in here with me?

1. Check recent activity. Every major email provider and most business services have a "recent activity" or "security" page showing sign-ins, devices, and locations. Look for anything you do not recognize — a device you have never owned, a city you have never visited, a 3 a.m. sign-in. One unfamiliar session is the signal to act; a clean activity log on an account you use daily is strong reassurance.

2. Sign out everything else. If anything looked even slightly off, sign out of all devices and sessions. A password change only governs new logins — it says nothing about a session an attacker already has open. Kicking every session forces everyone, including anyone who should not be there, to log in again with the new password.

3. Check your recovery email and phone number. Open the account's recovery settings and confirm the backup email and phone number listed are yours — ones you entered and can access. This is the step people skip, and it is the one attackers love: whoever controls the recovery address controls the account the next time it gets "lost." If a number or address you do not recognize is sitting there, remove it immediately.

4. Check your email forwarding rules. This is the sneaky one. An attacker who got in briefly can set a rule that silently forwards a copy of your incoming mail — including every future password-reset email — to their own address. Here is the part that surprises everyone: changing your password does not remove a forwarding rule. Neither does signing out of devices, and neither does turning on two-factor authentication. A rule is a setting, not a session. Open your mail settings, look at every forwarding rule and filter, and delete anything you did not create. This single check is on the FTC's hacked-email checklist for exactly this reason.

5. Change to a password you have never used anywhere else. Long beats clever — a passphrase of several ordinary words is both stronger and easier to remember than a short tangle of symbols. And "never used anywhere else" is the load-bearing part, for the reason in the next section.

How did they get your address? Check the breach lists

The most common way a stranger ends up with your email address is also the most boring: it leaked. Breaches spill email addresses by the millions, and those lists circulate for years. You can check yours in about ten seconds at haveibeenpwned.com — a free lookup run by security researcher Troy Hunt since 2013. Type in your email address and it tells you which known data breaches include it, and what kind of data was exposed. You can also sign up there for a free alert if your address shows up in a future breach.

If your address is in there — and for an address you have used for years, the honest odds are that it is — assume the password you used on that breached site is burned. Now comes the part that turns a single old breach into reset emails on your other accounts: credential stuffing. Attackers take leaked email-and-password pairs and replay them automatically against login pages everywhere — your bank, your hosting, your domain registrar, your social accounts. It works for one reason: people reuse passwords. A single reused password turns one forgotten forum breach from five years ago into a working key for your business email today. Researchers recently reported a circulating compilation of nearly 2 billion email addresses assembled for exactly this kind of replay — your address does not need to be important to be in the pile; it just needs to exist.

This is also why the reset emails sometimes arrive in clusters across several services in the same week. It is not a coordinated conspiracy against you. It is automation: your address is on a list, the list is being worked through, and every site on it sends you its reset email as the bots poke at it.

Why this matters more for a business owner

For most people, email is email. For you, your email account is the master key to the business. Think about what resets through your inbox: your bank, your hosting account, your domain registrar, your website's admin login, your payment processor. Almost every "forgot password" flow on the internet ends at an email inbox — which means whoever controls your inbox can, patiently, collect the keys to everything else. That is the same "one doorway" story as a stolen phone taking over a business, except there is no stolen phone here — just an address on a list and a reset form anyone can fill in. An unexpected reset email on your business email address is not a curiosity. It is a knock on the master-key door, and it gets the five-minute check, every time.

Lock the door they found

Once the five-minute check is clean (or cleaned up), make the knock useless next time:

Turn on two-factor authentication on your email first, then on the accounts that matter most. A password can be replayed; a code from your phone cannot be guessed from a breach list. The full walkthrough is in the guide on turning on two-factor for your business email — it is the single highest-leverage security move a business owner can make.

Retire every reused password. Anywhere you used the same password as a breached account, change it to something unique. Start with the accounts that can hurt you: email, bank, hosting, domain registrar, website admin. A password manager makes this painless; even a notebook system you actually follow beats reuse.

Audit who else has a door in. Reset emails are the outsider threat. The insider version is the developer, assistant, or agency who still has your passwords from a project two years ago — worth the same five minutes in the guide on whether your old developer still has access.

When to worry, and when to shrug

Not every reset email is an emergency, and learning the difference saves you from crying wolf at yourself. Shrug: a single reset email for an account you barely use, activity log clean, no other signs — probably a mistyped address or a bot poking a list and moving on. Delete it (via the spam button if it looked phishy) and move on. Worry: reset emails across several accounts in a short window, an unfamiliar session or a changed recovery address in your five-minute check, or a forwarding rule you did not create — that is someone actively working your accounts, and the full lockdown above happens today, not this weekend.

And remember the mirror image: while strangers are poking at your accounts, automated bots are poking at your website's login page the same way — the guide on why your website keeps getting login attempts covers that side of the fence. Both problems have the same root cause (your address and your site are both on lists now) and the same cure (unique passwords plus two-factor, everywhere it counts).
