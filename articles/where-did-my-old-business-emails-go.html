Where Did My Old Business Emails Go?

Nothing makes a business owner panic like an inbox with holes in it. The 2023 invoices are on the office computer but not on your phone. Or worse: they are nowhere at all. Before you assume you were hacked, know this: the most common cause is not an attack. It is a setting. A mail setting from the 1980s that most business email accounts still carry around, quietly downloading your mail onto one device and deleting the original. It has a name, POP, and once you know it exists, the fix takes about ten minutes.

This article describes how email programs handle your mail in general. Your own account settings are the verdict on your mail, not this page. Nothing here moves, deletes, or changes your email - the fixes below are settings you or your web person change on your devices.

Your email program has two ways of handling mail

Every email program, Outlook, Apple Mail, Thunderbird, your phone, fetches your mail using one of two methods. They sound technical. They are not.

POP works like a courier. It drives to the post office, picks up all your letters, delivers them to one house, and the post office throws its copies away. Your mail now exists in exactly one place: that device. Microsoft's own support pages put it plainly: by default, when a POP3 account retrieves new messages, the messages are deleted from the mail server and saved on the computer you are using. Because the message is no longer on the server, you cannot see it when you connect from another computer.

IMAP works like visiting the post office. You walk in, read your letters, maybe file some into folders, and leave everything there. Your phone, your computer, and webmail all show the same thing, because the mail lives on the server and every device just looks at it. Delete something on your phone and it is gone everywhere, which is the trade-off, but nothing ever vanishes because you opened it on the wrong device.

That is the whole mystery. If your old emails live on one device and nowhere else, or they were there last year and are gone now, you are almost certainly on POP with the default setting: download, then delete the original.

The telltale signs you are on POP

Sign 1: the emails are on the office computer but not on your phone. The computer checked the mail first, downloaded everything, and deleted the server copies. Your phone arrived at an empty post office. This is the classic one, and it is almost never anything else.

Sign 2: webmail shows nothing old. Log into your email in a browser. If years of mail are missing there but present in Outlook on one computer, that mail was pulled off the server and now lives only inside that computer's local mail file.

Sign 3: mail you sent from your phone never appears in your computer's sent folder. POP only downloads the inbox. It does not sync sent mail, drafts, or folders. Each device keeps its own private sent history, which is why "I know I sent it" turns into an argument with yourself.

Sign 4: it started after someone "set up" your email. A web host's one-click setup, an old Outlook configuration, a nephew who "knows computers." POP was the default for decades, so any setup done more than a few years ago, or done quickly, probably picked it.

Why so many businesses are still on POP

Nobody chooses POP on purpose anymore. It survives by inertia. The account was configured in 2016 and nobody has touched it since. The web host's setup guide defaulted to it. An old tutorial said to use it. And here is the part nobody tells you: it works perfectly until the day you get a second device. One computer, checking one inbox, never notices anything wrong. The problems begin the week you add a phone, a laptop, or an assistant, and by then years of mail may already be trapped on the original machine.

Our duplicates article covers the mirror-image symptom, getting every email twice, which comes from rules and groups rather than from POP. If your problem is mail that is missing rather than mail that is doubled, you are in the right place.

The fix, in order

Fix 1: flip the "leave a copy" switch. POP has a setting that stops the deleting: in Outlook it lives under Account Settings, then More Settings, then the Advanced tab, labeled "Leave a copy of messages on the server." In Thunderbird it is under Account Settings, then Server Settings, labeled "Leave Messages on the server." Check it, and from now on your mail stays on the server after download. This stops the bleeding. It does not fix the deeper problem, which is that POP still does not sync anything, but your mail will stop disappearing.

Fix 2, the real one: switch to IMAP. Most modern providers default to IMAP now, because it is the method built for multiple devices. Switching usually means removing the account from your email program and adding it again, choosing IMAP this time. Your address, your password, and your mail on the server all stay the same. One honest warning: mail that POP already pulled off the server does not come back. It lives in the old computer's local mail file. Copy or export that file before you change anything, which is also why our email backup article exists. And if your Gmail address is involved, note that Gmail keeps POP turned off by default: it must be switched on manually under Settings, then Forwarding and POP/IMAP, before any outside program can use it at all.

Fix 3: check the Gmail fetcher variant. If you have Gmail pulling in your business mail through its "check mail from other accounts" feature, that feature uses POP too, and its "leave a copy of retrieved messages on the server" box must be checked or Gmail quietly hijacks everything and the original account goes empty. Our Gmail-forwarding article walks through that exact trap.

A practical note for the re-setup: if your email program suddenly refuses your password during all of this, especially with a Gmail address, it may be the 2022 change, not a wrong password. Since May 30, 2022, Google no longer lets mail apps sign in with your regular Gmail password; with 2-step verification on, you generate a special 16-character App Password from your Google account's security page and use that in Outlook or Apple Mail instead.

Three other reasons old emails vanish

Your mailbox is full. Email accounts have a storage limit. When it fills up, new mail starts bouncing back to senders, and some setups start dropping the oldest mail to make room. If customers say their emails to you are bouncing, our bounce-back article covers it, but check your storage meter first: a full mailbox explains both missing old mail and missing new mail.

Archive is not delete, but it looks like it. On phones especially, a swipe archives instead of deleting, and archived mail leaves the inbox without going to trash. It is still there, in All Mail or the Archive folder. Before diagnosing anything exotic, search All Mail for a missing message.

A retention policy is quietly tidying up. Some business setups auto-delete mail older than 30, 90, or 365 days. If mail vanishes on a schedule rather than randomly, ask whoever manages the account whether a retention rule is running. This one is set by policy, not by accident, so the fix is a decision, not a setting.

What to ask your web person

Four questions that get a straight answer: (1) "Am I on POP or IMAP on each of my devices?" (2) "Is 'leave a copy on the server' turned on, and if I switch to IMAP, what happens to the mail already sitting on the office computer?" (3) "How do I get the old mail off that one computer and somewhere safe before we change anything?" - this is the backup question, and the answer should involve exporting or copying the local mail file, not hoping for the best. (4) "If we move providers later, how do we do it without losing anything?" - our provider-switch article covers the safe sequence, and if your phone is part of the mess, our phone setup article covers the device side.
