Can Your Customers Actually Tap the Buttons on Your Website?

Open your site on your phone and try to tap the menu with your thumb. If you have to slow down, zoom in, or re-tap — your customers are doing the same thing, except they do not re-tap. They leave. There is a world standard for how big a tappable button has to be, and a 5-minute test that tells you whether your site passes it.

One clarification before we start: this is not about screen readers (covered in our guide to blind customers using your site), keyboard-only navigation (our no-mouse contact-form guide), or text you can read (our readability guide). This guide is about the physical size of the things people tap — buttons, links, icons, phone numbers — and whether a human thumb can hit them on the first try.

The short answer: if your buttons and links are smaller than about 24 pixels square, a meaningful share of your customers cannot tap them reliably — and they will not tell you. They will tap, miss, and assume your site is broken, or they are. The world standard (WCAG 2.2) sets 24 pixels as the minimum; Apple aims for 44, Google for 48. The fix is usually spacing and padding, not a redesign.

Why so many sites fail this

1. Your site was designed on a desktop. Most business sites are still built on a big monitor with a precise mouse cursor, then shrunk onto a phone screen. What felt fine at 27 inches becomes a fingertip-sized target on a phone — a menu link that was easy to click is now a bullseye you have to land with your thumb.

2. Icon-only buttons are tiny by default. The little X that closes a pop-up, the hamburger menu, the row of social icons in the footer, the search magnifying glass — these are often drawn 16 to 20 pixels wide. That is below the standard even before you consider that the finger doing the tapping is wider than the icon.

3. Links get crammed together. Footer link lists, pagination numbers, "prev/next" arrows, the list of services in a sidebar — when tappable things sit shoulder to shoulder, the customer has to thread the needle. They tap "Pricing" and land on "Testimonials." The standard actually allows smaller targets if there is enough space between them — but crammed rows fail both the size test and the spacing test.

The 5-minute thumb test

You do not need software for this. Your thumb is the instrument.

1. Open your site on your phone. Not the desktop preview — your actual phone, the one your customers are using.

2. Use only your thumb. Scroll through the whole site tapping every button, link, menu item, phone number, and social icon with your thumb — the way a customer holding their phone in one hand does it.

3. Count the hesitations. Every time you have to slow down, steady your hand, zoom in, or tap twice — that is a failure. Not a complaint, a lost customer: they do not report the miss, they just assume the site is difficult and go to the competitor whose "Call now" button you can hit from across the room.

4. Hit the usual suspects. Footer social icons, pagination numbers, the X on any pop-up, star ratings, the mobile menu links, and any row of small links packed together. These are where sites fail most.

Who this costs you (it is not who you think)

The people who miss small targets are not careless — they are your highest-value visitors. Older customers with less precise hands. Anyone with arthritis, a tremor, or a hand injury. The parent holding a kid in one arm and tapping with the free thumb. The person on a bumpy bus. A customer whose reading glasses are at home.

These are also the customers least likely to fight a fiddly site. A 25-year-old might zoom in and try again. A 68-year-old looking for a plumber taps the phone number, misses, and calls the next listing on Google — no error message, no complaint, no second chance. The miss looks like "no traffic" in your analytics, not like a tap-target problem. You will never see the customers this cost you; they never got far enough to be counted.

The numbers behind the standard

In October 2023, the W3C published WCAG 2.2, the current version of the world’s web accessibility standard. It added a rule (2.5.8, "Target Size (Minimum)") that says interactive targets should be at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels — roughly a quarter of an inch on most screens — or have enough space around them that a near-miss still lands on the right target. The enhanced level (2.5.5) asks for 44 by 44 pixels.

The phone makers agree and go further: Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines call for a 44-point minimum touch target, and Google’s Material Design for Android calls for 48 density-independent pixels. When the people who built the phones and the people who write the web standard both land in the same neighborhood, "my buttons are fine at 16 pixels" is not a position anymore — it is a wish.

The important detail: the icon can stay small while the tap area grows. A tiny social-media glyph can keep its delicate look while the invisible tap zone around it reaches the standard — padding, not pixels. Your web person knows the setting. "Make the tap target 44 pixels" is a sentence you can say to any developer and be understood.

The fix ladder (cheapest first)

1. Spread the cramped rows apart. This is free. Footer links, social icons, pagination — give them breathing room. The standard explicitly rewards spacing: a small target with generous space around it passes where a small target jammed against its neighbor fails. One line of CSS, biggest win.

2. Grow the invisible tap zone. The icons do not need to get bigger — the tappable area around them does. Ask your web person to add padding to every icon-only button (the X, the menu, the social icons, the search glass) until the tap area reaches 44 pixels. The site looks identical; the thumbs stop missing.

3. Enlarge the real offenders. Anything that failed the thumb test twice — the "Call now" button, the mobile menu links, the form submit button — make visibly bigger too. These are revenue buttons; they deserve to be the easiest things on the page to hit.

4. Re-test after every redesign. Tap targets are the first casualty of a "cleaner, more minimal" redesign — smaller icons, tighter rows, more delicate everything. Run the 5-minute thumb test the week after any redesign, before customers run it for you.

What not to do

Don’t assume "they can just zoom." Pinch-zooming to hit a button is an admission the button was wrong — and zooming breaks the layout (our pinch-and-zoom guide covers why that is its own problem). If your answer to a small button is "customers will zoom," you have described the failure, not solved it.

Don’t buy a widget for this. No overlay widget enlarges your tap targets — they add a toolbar that promises to fix accessibility while the tiny buttons stay tiny (our widget guide covers why). This is a spacing-and-padding job, not a subscription.

Don’t treat it as cosmetic. "The buttons look fine" is a design opinion; "24 pixels" is a published standard with the W3C’s name on it, and it is the accessibility level (AA) that courts and contracts actually reference. When a customer’s lawyer asks whether your site meets the standard, the answer is a number, not an opinion. Our lawsuit guide walks through what that conversation looks like.

The bottom line

Your customers tap with thumbs, not mouse cursors — and the people with the least precise hands are often the ones most ready to buy. The standard says 24 pixels minimum, the phone makers say 44 to 48, and the fix is usually spacing and invisible padding rather than a redesign. Five minutes with your own thumb, once, tells you where you stand. The customers who miss never complain — they just leave, and they never show up in your analytics as the tap-target failure they were.
