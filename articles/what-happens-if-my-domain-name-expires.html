What happens if I let my domain name expire — can someone steal my website?

Miss your domain renewal and your website does not just go dark — your business name goes up for sale to strangers, and a professional industry grabs 50,000+ expired domains a day. Your email dies with it. Here are the three stages, the vultures, the fake-letter scam, and the five-minute fix.

Every business has bills it can pay late. The power company sends a warning. The landlord gives you a grace period. Your domain name — the address your customers type, and the name on every business email you send — is the one bill with no real second chance. Miss it, and your website does not just go dark. Your name goes up for sale to strangers, and there is a professional industry waiting to buy it.

What actually happens, in three stages

Stage 1: the grace period. After the expiry date passes, most registrars give you roughly 30 days to renew at the normal price. Your site may still work during part of this window. This is the easy rescue — if you notice.

Stage 2: redemption. Miss the grace window and the domain enters a redemption period of roughly another 30 days. You can still get it back, but now at a premium — a redemption fee that commonly runs in the tens to low hundreds of dollars on top of the renewal. (Exact lengths and fees vary by registrar and registry; these are the standard ICANN-shaped numbers for .com-style names.)

Stage 3: pending delete. About five more days, and then the domain is released to the public. Anyone on earth can register it — and usually, someone is already waiting.

The vultures are already waiting

There is a whole industry built on expired domains, called dropcatching: the moment a name drops, automated systems grab it. Security company Infoblox reported observing more than 50,000 dropcatched domains per day among generic endings like .com, .org, and .net in the first half of 2026 (reported by IT Brew). A separate report covered by SC Media in August 2026 put the figure at roughly 65,000 expired domains re-registered every day — nearly one in five new domain registrations — and described one criminal operation, nicknamed Sable Squirrel, that spent over $7 million acquiring more than 10,000 expired domains to run illegal streaming sites and malware operations.

Why pay for old names? Because your expired domain is worth more than a fresh one. It carries years of trust: old links from other sites, old customers who still type it in, and a reputation with email and security systems that a brand-new domain does not have. Criminals love that — a scam run from a ten-year-old domain sails past filters that would stop a name registered yesterday. Dropcatchers are not reading your site and deciding it is special. Their software grabs everything that looks valuable and sorts it later.

What the new owner gets with your name

Your customers. For a while, people keep typing your address and calling the number on the old flyers. They land on whatever the new owner put up — a parked page full of ads, a competitor’s site, or a scam wearing your name. Every one of those visitors was your customer five minutes ago.

Your email — and this one hurts immediately. Business email is tied to the domain, not the website. The moment the domain lapses, every address at it stops working: quotes do not send, invoices bounce, customers’ replies vanish into the void. And if a stranger registers the name, they can start receiving mail sent to your old addresses — order confirmations, password resets, customer details. Change your domain contact email the moment anything looks wrong.

Your reputation. Years of Google trust, reviews pointing at your address, links from the chamber of commerce — all of it now points at a stranger. Getting the name back after release is not a support ticket; it is a negotiation with whoever grabbed it, and their opening price is not the $15 renewal you skipped.

The other trap: the official-looking renewal letter

Here is the cruel twin of the expiry problem. Scammers mail and email official-looking “Domain Name Expiration Notice” letters that use your real domain name and your real expiration date — public information — and ask for, say, $265 for five years, with a payment form and a deadline. A 2026 write-up of one such operation (“Domain Name Services”) notes the fine print admits you are under no obligation and the payment actually moves your domain to a different registrar.

This is not new, and the enforcers have acted before: the FTC shut down a Canadian operation in 2010 for sending fake domain invoices to thousands of U.S. small businesses, and Australia’s consumer watchdog won $1.95 million in fines in 2018 against two companies that sent roughly 300,000 fake renewal notices and collected about $2.3 million. The rule that beats every version of this: never pay a renewal notice until you independently confirm it came from the registrar that actually manages your domain. If you are not sure which company that is, ask your web person before you pay anyone anything.

The five-minute protection plan

1. Turn on auto-renew, on a card that will not expire. Link renewals to a business card with a distant expiry date and alerts turned on — not a personal card you replace every two years. Most lapses are a dead card, not a decision.

2. Keep the contact email current. Renewal warnings go to the address on file from when you registered — sometimes a decade old. Log into your registrar once and check it. This is also the address a stranger would use to take over the account, so use one you actually control.

3. Lock the domain. Every registrar offers a transfer lock (often called registrar lock). It costs nothing and stops the name from being moved to another registrar without your approval. Turn it on.

4. Put a 60-day reminder on your calendar. Auto-renew plus a calendar nudge two months before expiry is belt and suspenders. If the card fails, you find out with time to spare — not in stage 2.

5. Know your actual registrar. Write down which company holds your domain, today, somewhere you will find it. Half the panic calls start with “I think GoDaddy? Or the guy who built the site?”

Your domain costs about fifteen dollars a year to keep. Losing it costs your website, your email, your customers’ trust, and a ransom negotiation. It is the cheapest insurance your business owns — treat it like it.
