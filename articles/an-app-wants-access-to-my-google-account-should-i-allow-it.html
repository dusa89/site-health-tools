An App Wants Access to My Google Account - Should I Allow It?

You clicked a link - to connect your scheduler, your invoicing app, a 'sign in with Google' button - and Google shows a screen listing what the app wants: read your email, see your contacts, manage your calendar. Your finger hovers over Allow. That button is not a login. It is you handing that app a key to your Google account - a key that can keep working even if you later change your password. Here is what the screen is really asking, the 30-second check to run before you ever click Allow, and the attack the FBI issued an alert about in September 2026 that uses this exact screen.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-13 - not promises about your situation. App companies and attack campaigns change fast; the mechanics below stay the same either way.

What that "Allow" button actually does

When you click Allow, the app never gets your password. Instead, Google hands it a pass - a token that says "this app may do exactly these things in this account." The pass is scoped (only the permissions listed on the screen), it expires, and you can revoke it at any time from your Google account's list of authorized apps. That is the good news, and it is also the entire reason this works: you never typed your password into the app, so there is no password to steal. (Our browser-passwords article covers where your passwords should live; this pass is a different mechanism entirely - no password changes hands.) The permission screen that lists what the app wants is your one clear look at the deal. Most people treat it as an annoying speed bump on the way to using the app. Treat it as the contract it is.

The 30-second check before you click Allow

Read the screen the way you would read a contract someone slid across the table. First: who is asking? The screen names the app and the company behind it. If you got here from a text message or an email about a shared file or an event invitation - especially from someone you don't know - stop and verify the sender independently before touching the button. Second: match the permissions to the job. A scheduling app asking to see your calendar makes sense. A PDF viewer asking to read your email and send messages as you does not - that mismatch is the classic red flag. Third: watch for the "unverified app" warning. Google shows a screen saying it hasn't verified the app; developers are told to click through that warning only for their own test apps. If a stranger's app shows you that warning, it is a stop sign, not a speed bump - walk away. And remember: verification on a screen lowers the risk but is not a guarantee. It confirms a real company stands behind the app. Even then, compare what it asks for with what it does.

The attack the FBI warned about

On September 1, 2026, the FBI issued a public alert about "OAuth consent phishing" - and it targets exactly this screen. Here is how it works, per the alert: criminals impersonate government officials, media figures, or event coordinators and message you with a link disguised as a file-sharing service or an event invitation. You click, and you land on a permission screen that is real - a legitimate Google or Microsoft screen, not a fake login page. (Our password-reset emails article covers the fake-login kind of phishing; this is the other kind, and it is harder to spot because the screen is genuine.) If you click Allow, the criminal's app gets the permissions listed - reading and sending your email, reaching your files and other sensitive data - without ever learning your password. Your multi-factor authentication does not stop it, because you are the one signing in. (Our two-factor article explains what 2FA does stop - this isn't it.) And here is the part that surprises most people: changing your password afterward does not kick the attacker out. The access runs on the token you approved, not on your password, so the token stays valid after a reset. The only fix is to revoke the app's access directly in your account's security settings.

The 5-minute cleanup worth doing tonight

You don't have to wait for an attack to make this list smaller. Open your Google Account, go to Security, and review the third-party apps connected to your account. You will likely find apps you connected years ago and forgot - a tool you tried once, a sign-in you used on a site you never visit. Remove anything you don't recognize or no longer use. Each removal takes seconds, and every app you remove is one fewer key floating around. The warning signs that you already approved something bad: unexpected requests to authorize a new app, permissions that never matched what the app does, unfamiliar apps in that connected-apps list, or emails going out from your account that you didn't write. If you spot any of those, remove the app first and ask questions second. (This is also the safer pattern to use with your web people instead of handing over passwords - our developer-passwords article explains why.) And one more thing to know: your email is the master key to everything else. (Our email-takeover article and stolen-phone article cover the other two ways someone gets into your accounts.)

The bottom line

Allow is safe when the app is one you chose, the permissions match the job, and the app is verified by a company you trust. It is dangerous when you arrived there from an unexpected message, the permissions are broader than the job needs, or the app is unverified. Build the habit now: read the permission list like a contract, and once a quarter, open your Google Account's third-party app list and prune it. Five minutes of pruning beats every defense in this article, because an app you removed cannot be abused.
