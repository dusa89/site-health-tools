Can Someone Send Email That Looks Like It's From My Business?

The short answer: yes — and your account can be completely clean when it happens. Email was built in 1982 on a handshake deal: the sending computer declares who the message is from, and everyone down the line takes its word for it. Nobody checks the ID. So a criminal on the other side of the world can send mail that says it's from your business, to your customers, and your own mailbox will never show it. Here is how they do it, what it costs you, and the three records that take your name off their costume rack.

1. Nothing on your computer has to break first

This is not a hacked account. The hacked-account version is a different problem — there, mail actually leaves YOUR mailbox. Here, your mailbox, your password, your computer are all fine. The criminal never logged into anything of yours. They simply wrote your name on an email they sent from their own computer — because the email system never asks for proof.

When email was designed in 1982, the internet was a small room full of people who trusted each other. The protocol that moves email (SMTP) takes the sender's word for the "From" address — your email program doesn't validate what you type, and the servers that carry the message don't check it either. What the recipient sees in their inbox is just whatever the sender wrote in the From line. Unless someone digs into the message's raw underlying data, the forgery is invisible (smtpedia.com, 2026; Wikipedia).

There are actually two "senders" on every email, and they don't have to match. The hidden routing envelope carries the real return path (where bounces go). The visible message header carries the From name the recipient sees. By default, nothing checks that either one is legitimate. The one piece of hard evidence is the trail of "Received" lines in the raw headers — the actual computer that sent the mail. Normal people never look at those. Criminals count on it (Wikipedia).

2. The three costumes they wear your name in

Costume one: the exact forgery. The From line says you@yourbusiness.com. The hidden envelope says something else entirely. The customer sees your business name, in your voice, and has no reason to doubt it. This is the version the authentication records in section 4 are built to stop.

Costume two: the friendly-name trick. Every email has a display name (the big readable part: "Shawn — Acme Plumbing") and a real address behind it (the small gray text underneath). Criminals set the display name to YOU and put a throwaway address behind it. Most customers read the big text and never look at the small text. They can also set a separate "Reply-To" address, so any reply to the forged mail lands in the criminal's inbox instead of yours (spoofing mechanics guide).

Costume three: the lookalike domain — and the honest limitation. The mail comes from acmep1umbing.com (that's a 1, not an l) or yourbusiness.co instead of .com. At a glance in a busy inbox, nobody notices. Here is the part most guides skip: your DMARC record cannot stop this one, because the forgery doesn't use your domain at all — it uses a cousin. The defense is different: register the two or three closest lookalikes of your own domain yourself (about $12 a year each), and teach your customers to look at the actual address, not the display name.

3. Why it costs you even though your systems are fine

The invoice scam is the money version. A customer gets an email that looks like you: "we've updated our banking details — please send the invoice payment here." They wire it. It is gone. The FBI's 2025 internet crime report put business email compromise at $3,046,598,558 in losses from 24,768 complaints — the second-costliest cybercrime category in the country after investment fraud, at roughly $123,000 lost per reported incident (FBI IC3 2025 report, via rexxfield.com and nationalcybersecurity.com). The mirror version — where the scam wears your vendor's name and you're the target — is covered separately. Either direction ends at someone's money leaving.

The bounce storm lands on YOU. Forged mail that doesn't reach anyone bounces back — to the real return address: you. Owners describe waking up to hundreds of "delivery failed" messages for mail they never sent. Forged mail also draws angry replies from strangers and poisoned spam complaints against your domain. All of that sinks your domain's reputation — which is exactly why your REAL invoices start landing in spam afterward. The forgery doesn't just fool your customers; it trains the world's inboxes to distrust your name.

The big inboxes have moved against this — and that is good news for you. Since February 2024, Gmail and Yahoo have required anyone sending 5,000+ emails a day to their inboxes to authenticate with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, and keep spam complaints under 0.3%. Microsoft applied the same rules to Outlook.com in May 2025 — non-compliant bulk mail now gets a hard rejection, not a spam-folder delivery. Even small senders are now expected to have at least SPF or DKIM set up (autospf.com, 2026; warmy.io, 2026). The world's biggest inboxes have decided unverified mail is suspicious mail. Publishing your records puts you on the trusted side of that line.

4. The three records that lock your name

Three short text records in your domain's DNS are the entire defense. SPF says which mail servers are allowed to send as you. DKIM stamps each message with a signature only your server can make. DMARC ties them together — and crucially, DMARC is the one that tells receiving inboxes what to do with mail that fails the other two. No DMARC, no instruction — the inbox has to guess.

DMARC has three settings, and the setting is the whole game. p=none means "just watch and tell me" — forged mail still gets delivered; it only reports. p=quarantine means "send failures to spam." p=reject means "throw forged mail away." The safe rollout is none first (so you can watch the reports and make sure your OWN legitimate mail passes), then quarantine, then reject. But know what most businesses do: stop at none, forever. A 2026 survey of Australian domains found 92.3% had a DMARC record, yet 53.3% of those still didn't enforce a reject policy (PowerDMARC, via spoofguard README, 2026). A DMARC record that says "just watch" doesn't block a single forgery.

5. What to tell your web person (the exact words)

Send them this: "Someone can send email that looks like it's from our business. Five asks: first, check that SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are all published for our domain — if any are missing, add them. Second, set our DMARC reporting address so we get reports on who is sending mail as us. Third, after two to four clean weeks of reports, move the DMARC policy up: none, then quarantine, then reject — don't stay on 'just watch' forever. Fourth, buy the two or three closest lookalike domains of our name and point them at our site. Fifth, add a line to our invoices and quotes: 'we will never email new bank details — if you get one, call us before you pay.'" Five asks, one afternoon, and your name stops being a free costume.
