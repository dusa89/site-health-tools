Is Lazy-Loading My Hero Image Slowing Down My Website?

The short answer: lazy-loading is for photos people scroll to. Your hero image — the big photo at the top of your homepage — should load first, not last. Lazy-loading tells the browser to hold a photo back until it's needed. That's brilliant for the twenty photos down the page. But your hero is the first screen, and it's usually the biggest thing Google times when it grades your speed. Hold the hero back and you've held back your grade. If you're new to the idea, the lazy-loading explainer covers how the trick works; this article is about the one place you must never use it.

1. The scene: the advice that backfired

Somebody — a speed plugin, a blog post, a well-meaning nephew — told you lazy-loading makes websites faster. So you turned it on for everything. Every photo on the site now carries the little loading="lazy" stamp, including the big hero photo at the top of your homepage. Then you ran a speed test and Google still flagged your largest photo as slow. The advice wasn't wrong. It was incomplete: lazy-loading is a waiting line, and somebody put your most important photo at the back of it.

2. The 30-second version of how loading works

Every photo on your page loads in one of two ways. Eager means "download this right now" — it's also what the browser does when you say nothing at all. Lazy means "hold this back until the visitor is about to see it." For the photos halfway down the page, lazy is the right call: why spend a visitor's data on a photo they may never scroll to? (The full mechanics are in the lazy-loading explainer.) The trouble starts when the "hold it back" stamp lands on the photo that's already on screen the moment the page opens.

3. Why the hero photo is special: it's what Google grades

Google's speed grade for your page hinges on one moment: when the biggest thing in the first screen finishes appearing. That metric is called Largest Contentful Paint, and the target is under 2.5 seconds (what that target means, in plain English). On most homepages the biggest thing in the first screen is the hero photo. So the hero photo isn't just decoration — it's the exam. Lazy-loading the hero is like telling the star runner to wait at the starting line until the crowd looks at him. The race is already being timed.

4. The hidden mechanic: your hero skips the browser's fast lane

Here's the part nobody tells owners. As your page arrives, the browser runs a second, super-fast scan of the raw page and starts downloading the important images immediately — before the page has even finished arriving. But a lazy-loaded image is skipped by that fast scan. For the browser to even decide whether a lazy image is on screen, it first has to download and read all of your page's styling instructions and lay the page out. Only then does the hero photo's download begin. In plain terms: your entire stylesheet becomes a waiting room your biggest photo must sit through. One stamp — loading="lazy" — quietly moved your hero to the back of every line.

5. The WordPress twist: your site may have done this to itself

If your site runs on WordPress, check this even if you never touched a setting. In August 2020, WordPress started stamping loading="lazy" on every image automatically — including heroes. The people who build WordPress then discovered the stamp on the hero was slowing down the very grade it was meant to help, so in January 2022 they refined it: the first content image on the page no longer gets the stamp. But that's only the first image in your page content — a hero built by your theme, a slider, or a page builder can still carry the stamp today. Google's own speed tool will actually call this out by name with a warning that the largest photo was lazily loaded. WordPress tested the fix across 50 popular themes: skipping the stamp on the first image made the graded moment about 7% faster in the middle of the pack (their test: 2,020 milliseconds down to 1,877), and up to 33% faster in the best cases. Those are their lab numbers on themes — not a promise for your site — but they show how real the penalty is.

6. The 30-second check: look at your own hero

Open your homepage in Chrome. Right-click the big photo at the top and choose Inspect. A panel opens with one line of code highlighted. Look for the words loading="lazy" in that line. If they're there, you found it — your most important photo is waiting in line. (If you see loading="eager" or nothing at all, your hero is fine and your slowness lives elsewhere — oversized photo files and homepage sliders are the next places to look.)

7. The fixes, cheapest first

First, don't nuke the whole thing. Lazy-loading is still doing good work on the nineteen photos below the fold — it saves your visitors' mobile data. WordPress's own team tested turning it off everywhere and found the refined version (lazy everywhere except the hero) beats both extremes. The fix is a scalpel, not a sledgehammer.

The no-code fix: most speed plugins (and many themes) have a setting to exclude specific images from lazy-loading — usually called "exclude from lazy load" with a box where you paste the hero photo's file name. Add your hero there, clear the site cache, and re-test.

The tell-your-web-person fix: the hero image tag should say loading="eager" instead of loading="lazy", and it can carry fetchpriority="high" — that's you telling the browser "this photo is the main event, download it first." One step further is a preload hint in the page header for the hero file, which puts it at the very front of the line. While they're in there, make sure the hero has its width and height set — a hero with no reserved space makes the page jump as it loads (why that jump happens).

8. What to tell your web person (the exact words)

Send them this: "My homepage hero image is lazy-loaded and I think it's hurting our speed grade. Two asks: first, remove the lazy-loading from the hero image only — set it to eager and add fetchpriority high — and leave lazy-loading on everything below the fold. Second, check whether the theme or a plugin is stamping lazy-load onto the hero automatically, and whether the hero has its width and height set so the page doesn't jump. Then re-run the speed test and tell me what the largest-photo timing did. No jargon in the answer." One stamp removed, and the star runner starts on time.
