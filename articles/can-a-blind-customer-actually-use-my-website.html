Can a blind customer actually use my website?

A screen reader never sees your design — it reads the code underneath and speaks it out loud. Here are the five things in that code that decide whether a blind customer can use your site, and why none of them change how it looks.

Picture your customer browsing your website with the screen off. They are not guessing — they are listening, because they are blind, and the software reading your site out loud is called a screen reader. Now ask the uncomfortable question: could they actually use your website?

Here is what most business owners get wrong about this: a screen reader does not look at your site. It reads the code underneath — the headings, the labels, the order of things — and turns that into a spoken summary. Your site does not fail blind customers because it looks bad. It fails them because the code tells a different story than the pixels do.

1. Your images are invisible without alt text

A sighted visitor sees your product photo, your team picture, the before-and-after shots. A screen reader hears only the image’s alt text — the short description you (or your web developer) wrote in the code. If there is none, the reader announces the file name instead: “image, IMG_3421_final_FINAL dot jaypeg.” Your product is invisible, your team is invisible, your portfolio is invisible. Decorative graphics should be marked so they are skipped quietly; every image that carries meaning needs a real description.

2. Headings are the table of contents

Blind users don’t read a page top to bottom — they jump from heading to heading, the way a sighted reader scans. That only works if the code has real headings, in order: one main heading, then sections, then subsections. Plenty of small-business sites cheat here: the “headings” are just bold, oversized text that looks like a heading but is tagged as a plain paragraph. The screen reader sails right past it. Skipping from heading level 1 to heading level 5 has the same effect — the reader’s outline becomes nonsense.

3. Form fields must say what they are

This is where the money breaks. Your contact form, your quote-request form, your booking form — a sighted visitor sees “Name” next to the box. If the label exists only visually and isn’t wired into the code, the screen reader announces “edit, blank” and your blind customer has to guess what goes in each box. Name? Email? Phone? They are filling out your lead form blindfolded. Plenty of businesses never hear about this — the prospect just gives up and calls the competitor whose form announced itself properly.

4. “Click here” is meaningless out loud

One of the testers’ favorite complaints: a page with forty links, every one of them announced as “click here.” Sighted readers see the sentence around the link and know where it goes. A blind customer pulling up a list of your links hears “click here” forty times and learns nothing. Links should describe their destination: “view our service-area map,” not “click here.” This costs you nothing and changes nothing about how the site looks.

5. The keyboard has to reach everything

Many blind users — and plenty of sighted ones with limited mobility — navigate with the keyboard alone, pressing Tab to move through the page. The focus has to move in the same order a sighted eye follows, and it has to be visible. If your dropdown menu only opens on mouse hover, if your pop-up can’t be closed without a mouse, if Tab jumps from the header to the footer and skips the whole middle of the page — those customers are locked out of whatever sits behind the lock.

The honest part

You may have seen ads for accessibility widgets — a small toolbar that promises to make any site compliant in one click. Here’s the tester’s rule of thumb, stated as judgment: your prettiest page is probably your worst one, and no toolbar can fix what the underlying code gets wrong. Automated widgets don’t add the alt text you never wrote, don’t label the form fields you never labeled, and don’t fix the heading order — those are code problems, and they take a person looking at the actual code.
