Should I Pay If Hackers Lock My Website?

You open your laptop and your files will not open — the website files, the customer list, the invoices, all of it. In their place is a message: your data has been encrypted, pay this amount (usually in cryptocurrency) by the deadline, or lose it forever. This is ransomware, and the question it is designed to make you ask — under pressure, on a timer — is "should I just pay?" The blackmail email from the sextortion article is a bluff you can delete; this one is real, and the answer is still no. Here is why, in plain English — and the first-hour playbook that gets your business back without paying.

1. What just happened to your files

Ransomware scrambles your files so nothing opens, then sells you the unscramble. It is not a warning or a prank — the files genuinely are locked. The ransom note usually adds two more screws: a countdown ("the price doubles in 48 hours, the key is destroyed in 7 days") and a second threat ("we also copied your customer data, and if you don't pay we publish it"). The FBI calls that second threat double extortion — encrypt your systems AND steal your data (the August 2026 FBI/CISA/HHS advisory on the Medusa ransomware group describes exactly this model, verified Oct 2026). The timer is a pressure tactic, not a fact about what your data is worth.

You are not too small to be on their list. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report counted 3,611 ransomware complaints with over $32 million in reported payments (April 2026, verified Oct 2026) — and that figure is only the payments people reported; it excludes lost business, wages, equipment, and cleanup costs. Sixty-three new ransomware variants appeared in 2025, and 14 of the 16 U.S. critical infrastructure sectors were hit. Hospitals, schools, city governments, small manufacturers — the FBI's Cyber Division chief put it plainly in 2026: "I can't think of anything that's off limits to them." A locked bakery laptop pays just as well as a locked hospital server to the people running this.

2. The answer: do not pay. Here is why, in plain terms

The FBI's official position: "The FBI does not support paying a ransom in response to a ransomware attack. Paying a ransom doesn't guarantee you or your organization will get any data back. ... We have seen cases where organizations never got the decryption key after having paid the ransom." (fbi.gov, verified Oct 2026). These are criminals holding a gun to your files. There is no customer-service desk, no refund policy, and no reason for them to hand over a working key after the money lands. Some victims pay and get a key that does not work. Some pay and get nothing at all. The money is gone either way — along with the data, if there is no backup.

Reason two: paying marks you as a payer. The groups run this as a business, and a business that pays once goes on the "will pay" list. The same August 2026 federal advisory documented a case where a second attacker demanded an additional payment after the first ransom was paid — triple extortion. Paying does not buy safety; it buys your name on a list.

Reason three: paying does not un-leak your data. In the double-extortion model, they copied your files before scrambling them. Paying for the unscramble key does nothing about the copy they kept. The "we'll delete it if you pay" promise comes from the same people who broke into your systems — it is worth exactly as much as their word, which is the thing that put you here. The general hack-response article covers the forensics side; this article is the payment decision, and the decision is no.

3. The first-hour playbook (do this before anything else)

Minute one: disconnect, don't power off. Unplug the network cable or turn off Wi-Fi — not the power button. A shutdown can wipe the evidence investigators need to find out how they got in (FBI-linked 2026 SMB guidance, verified Oct 2026). One infected machine can spread to every other device on the same network; cutting the network cord stops the spread without destroying the trail.

Minute five: call your IT person, not the hackers. Do not negotiate with the ransom note. Do not install the "decryption tool" they link you. Call whoever maintains your website or computers — and if that is the developer or IT helper from the responsibility article — they are the one who decides the recovery order. Doing this alone, under pressure, is how backups get overwritten and logs get lost.

Minute thirty: report it at ic3.gov. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center is where ransomware gets reported. Reporting helps investigators track the group behind the attack — and sometimes a free decryption tool already exists for your exact ransomware variant, which the FBI can surface (verified Oct 2026).

Then: restore from your backup — the one you have actually tested. A backup nobody has test-restored in the last year is a hope, not a plan. If you have a clean, tested copy — like the one the backup article walks you through making — wiping and restoring is how the business comes back without paying a cent. (Do not count on your hosting company's copy: their backup is for their disasters, not yours.)

4. The mistakes owners make under the timer

Mistake one: paying fast to beat the countdown. The timer exists to stop you thinking. The three reasons in section 2 are true at hour 1 and hour 100 — the price doubling does not make the key any more reliable.

Mistake two: hiding it. Owners who quietly pay and tell nobody — not their IT person, not the FBI — end up with the worst of both worlds: money gone, data still in criminal hands, and the hole that let them in still open. The forensics article exists because "they got in" is a question you want answered, not assumed away.

Mistake three: wiping everything immediately. Wiping destroys the evidence of how they entered — which is the evidence that prevents the next attack. Disconnect, preserve, call the professional, restore from backup. In that order.

5. The questions owners always ask next

"What if it's my customers' data that's locked — do I have to tell them?" That is a legal question, not a technical one, and the honest answer is "ask a lawyer" — many states have breach-notification rules with real deadlines. What is certain: paying does not remove that obligation. The ransom note is not a legal settlement.

"My web person says wipe and restore — but my site was fine yesterday?" Yes, wipe and restore. "Fine yesterday" is exactly when ransomware strikes — the encryption happens in minutes, long after weeks of silent access. A clean restore from before the infection is the fix; the scrambled copy is evidence, not an asset.

"Is there any case where paying makes sense?" The FBI's guidance is a flat no, and the cases where companies pay are the ones with no tested backup and days of downtime staring them down — which is the most expensive possible way to discover you had no backup. The cheap version of this lesson is making the copy now.
