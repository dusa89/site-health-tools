I Set Up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC - Why Does the Checker Still Say Fail?

You spent an afternoon in your DNS settings doing exactly what the guides said. SPF pasted. DKIM pasted. DMARC pasted. And the checker still shows red. Before you rip it all out and start over, know this: the problem is almost never that you set it up wrong. It is one of six small mismatches between what you published and what the checker is reading - and five of them take under a minute to confirm. Run them in order before you touch a single record.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-12 - not promises about your situation. Every mail setup is arranged a little differently, and your answer will be one of the six below, not all of them. (Our DMARC explainer covers what the records themselves mean, if you need the primer first.)

1. You have TWO SPF records - of the six problems, this is the one that shows up most often

Start here, because it is the fastest check with the biggest payoff: one lookup of your DNS answers it in seconds. Your registrar's DNS panel lets you add a new TXT record without warning you that one already exists. So now you have the record your web host gave you during setup and the record your mail provider gave you - both sitting there, both individually correct, and together completely broken.

The rule is strict and old: a domain may publish exactly ONE TXT record starting with v=spf1 on a given name. More than one, and the email standard (RFC 7208) says the receiver must throw out both of them - a permanent error. Your mail does not get half-authenticated. It gets treated as unauthenticated. One check of your DNS for "how many lines start with v=spf1" settles it: the answer must be one.

The fix is a merge, not a delete. Every sender goes inside the single record, like this: one v=spf1, both includes side by side, one ending - v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net ~all (swap in your own providers). Then remove the second record completely. And know there is a ceiling too: more than 10 DNS lookups inside the one record is the same kind of permanent error, so every include: is a cost you should be able to justify.

2. The DKIM key is published under the wrong name

A DKIM key is not just a value - it is a value at an address. The public key must live at {selector}._domainkey.yourdomain.com, and that selector name must match the name written in the s= field of the signature stamp on your outgoing mail. If the record says google._domainkey but your mail is stamped with a different selector name, the verifier looks at the wrong address, finds nothing, and calls it a fail. The value is fine. The name is wrong.

The Microsoft 365 version of this trip is especially common. Their DKIM setup is not a TXT record at all - it is two CNAME records, selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey, pointing into Microsoft's own key namespace - and the signing toggle in the Defender portal has to be switched on before a single message gets stamped. Records sitting in your DNS without the toggle on is one of the most common "I did everything and it still fails" situations in business email: the infrastructure is built and the engine is off.

3. You checked the wrong name

The checker looked at example.com. Your invoices go out through invoicing.example.com, or your newsletter tool sends from news.example.com. A subdomain is a different name with its own records - fix the apex domain all you like, the subdomain's mail still fails. Check the exact name your mail actually sends from, and if your newsletter tool gave you its own setup page, its own records live on its own sending name. (Our subdomain article walks through how that setup is supposed to look.)

4. DNS has not finished spreading yet

DNS is not one computer - it is a network of copies, and a new or changed record takes time to reach all of them. Checkers do not all ask the same copy, which is why one tool can show green and another red for the same domain on the same afternoon. Allow up to a day or two for everything to settle, then test again. This is the reason the diagnostic order at the end of this article puts patience before edits: most "it fixed itself overnight" stories are just DNS finishing its job.

5. The DMARC "fail" may be exactly what you staged

Not every red mark is broken. A DMARC record with p=none means "watch and report, do not block anything" - and that is the correct first stage, on purpose, for almost every business. It is how you collect the reports that tell you who sends as your domain before you start rejecting anything. A checker that flags it as a failure is showing you your plan, not a broken record. Escalating to quarantine and then reject is a deliberate second and third step, after the reports come back clean - not something to do because a tool showed red.

6. The DMARC report mailbox does not exist

Your DMARC record says rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com - send the reports here. If that mailbox was never created, the reports bounce, and some checkers flag the setup as incomplete. Create the address (or point the record at a monitoring service's address), and - this is the part everyone skips - actually read the first few reports. They are the only honest inventory of every service sending mail as your business, and they are what tell you when it is safe to move from monitoring to enforcement.

The ten-minute diagnostic order

Run these before changing anything:

1. Count the SPF records. However many lines start with v=spf1 on the name you send from - the answer must be exactly one. Merge, do not add.

2. Match the DKIM selector. The name in the record must match the name in the s= field of your outgoing mail's signature. Microsoft shops: confirm the two CNAMEs and the signing toggle.

3. Check the name you actually send from. If your mail leaves from a subdomain or a tool's sending domain, that is the name to verify.

4. Wait a full day. If you changed records in the last 24-48 hours, your "failure" may be a copy of DNS that has not caught up. Test again in the morning before touching anything. (Our DNS-move article covers the cousin of this - what breaks when your whole DNS changes homes.)

5. Only then escalate DMARC. Deliberately: reports first, quarantine second, reject last. (And if your worry is really "will my quotes reach the inbox," the trust check is the reputation side of the story; staying unauthenticated is what eventually gets you blacklisted.)
