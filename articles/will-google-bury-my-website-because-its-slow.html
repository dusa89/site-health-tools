Will Google Bury My Website Because It's Slow?

Somebody told you a slow website gets punished by Google - maybe a web person selling you a speed package. The truth is more useful than the scare: Google does grade your speed, it does count toward where you rank, and it is not the burial people describe. Here is what is actually measured, how much it moves the needle, and the cost of slowness that has nothing to do with Google at all.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-13 - not promises about your situation. Nobody outside Google can tell you exactly how much speed moves any single ranking, and anyone who promises you a specific jump is selling something.

The short answer

No - Google does not bury slow websites. There is no penalty box for slowness, no "slow site" filter that deletes you from the results. What exists is quieter: since 2021, how your site feels to use has been one of Google's official ranking signals, measured by three specific numbers called the Core Web Vitals. A slow site gives up a small edge on every search it appears in - and, more importantly, it bleeds visitors who click and then leave. The second cost is usually bigger than the first.

The three things Google actually grades

Google introduced the Core Web Vitals in May 2020 as its official measure of real-world page experience - not lab tests, but what actual Chrome visitors experience on your pages. There are three, and your page only passes when all three are good at the same time:

1. How fast the main content appears: 2.5 seconds or less. Google calls this Largest Contentful Paint - the moment your headline, hero image, or main banner becomes visible. Under 2.5 seconds is good, 2.5 to 4 needs work, over 4 is poor. (Our how-fast guide walks through all three numbers in detail.)

2. How fast the page answers a tap or click: 200 milliseconds or less. This is Interaction to Next Paint - the delay between a visitor tapping a button and the page responding. Google replaced the old version of this metric with the stricter one in March 2024, so if your web person is still talking about the old measurement, their report is out of date.

3. Whether the page stays still while it loads: a shift score under 0.1. Cumulative Layout Shift measures the jump-around - text sliding down as an image loads late, a button moving just as someone taps it. Under 0.1 is good.

Google judges these at the 75th percentile of real visits - meaning three out of four of your actual visitors need a good experience, not just the average one.

When speed became a ranking factor

The vitals became official ranking signals with Google's Page Experience Update, which rolled out through the summer of 2021. Two later changes matter for you: in March 2024 Google swapped in the stricter responsiveness metric described above, and since July 2024 Google judges all of this on the mobile version of your site by default - the desktop version no longer sets the grade. If your site is fast on your office computer but slow on phones, Google is grading the phone version. (Our mobile-speed article explains why that gap is so common.)

How much it really moves your ranking

Here is the honest part the scare stories skip: page experience is one signal among many, and relevance wins. If your page is the best answer to what someone searched, it can outrank a faster page with a weaker answer. Google's own people have described page experience as the kind of signal that matters most when two pages are otherwise close - the tiebreaker, not the executioner.

That does not make it meaningless. In local searches - "plumber near me," "dentist open Saturday" - the pages competing for the top spots are often very close in relevance, and that is exactly where a tiebreaker bites. A slow site is not buried; it is quietly outranked, one close call at a time, by competitors whose sites feel better to use.

The bigger cost has nothing to do with Google

Forget rankings for a moment. Google's own published research found that more than half of mobile visitors abandon a page that takes longer than 3 seconds to load - a 2017 benchmark still quoted in speed guides today. Every one of those visitors clicked, waited, and left without calling. They never complain, they never come back, and no ranking in the world fixes a page that drives its own traffic away. (Our one-extra-second article puts the business math on this.)
