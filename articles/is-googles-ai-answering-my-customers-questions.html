Is Google's AI answering my customers' questions so they never visit my website?

A February 2026 study of 300,000 keywords found the #1 result's clicks fell 58% when an AI answer appeared above it. Here is what that means for a small business — the real leaks, the honest caveat, and the fix in leverage order.

Picture this: a homeowner searches “how much does it cost to repipe a house?” Google’s AI answers right there on the results page — average price, factors that move the number, done. The homeowner got the answer and never clicked a single website. Yours included. That is not a hypothetical: a large share of searches now ends exactly this way, and 2026 studies have put hard numbers on what the AI answer costs the websites below it.

The numbers, with sources and dates

An Ahrefs study published February 2026 analyzed 300,000 keywords and compared Search Console data from before AI Overviews existed (December 2023) with December 2025. Where an AI Overview appeared, the click-through rate of the #1 ranking page fell 58% — up sharply from a 34.5% drop Ahrefs measured in April 2025. In absolute terms, position-one CTR fell from 7.6% to 1.6%. That is a 79% collapse in the clicks that used to arrive reliably each month.

Pew Research Center tracked 68,879 real searches from 900 U.S. adults (July 2025). When an AI summary appeared, users clicked a traditional result only 8% of the time, versus 15% without one. Worse for publishers: just 1% clicked a source cited inside the AI answer, and 26% of users stopped searching entirely after reading the summary. A Seer Interactive analysis of 25.1 million impressions found a 61% organic CTR crash when an AI Overview appeared. And a randomized trial by University of Pennsylvania and Northeastern researchers (released August 2026, ~1,000 Chrome users) found that removing the AI Overview nearly doubled outbound clicks — inbound clicks rose from 0.37 to 0.62 per search.

The honest caveat: this is mostly an informational problem

Before you panic, here is what the fine print says. These studies measure informational queries — questions, how-tos, definitions — where a paragraph of text can satisfy the searcher. Those are the queries an AI Overview can summarize cleanly, and they take the heaviest damage. Local service pages, pricing pages, and anything tied to a booking or a phone call hold up much better, because those queries still require a click to actually hire someone or contact you. Nobody books a plumber from inside a summary box. Your “get a quote” traffic is far less exposed than a publisher’s article traffic — a correlation worth naming honestly.

That said, there are two real leaks for a small business. First, your advice content gets zero-clicked. That “how to reset your water heater” page or seasonal tips post that used to bring neighbors to your site now answers its question inside Google, and the visitor never sees your name. Second, your expertise feeds the machine for free. If your site publishes the clearest pricing explanation in town, the AI summary may be built from your words — with 1% of users bothering to click the citation. You taught the customer; a competitor got the call.

The fix, in leverage order

First, check what the AI actually says about you. Ask ChatGPT and Google the question a customer would ask — “who is the best [your trade] in [your town]” and “how much does [your main service] cost?” — and read the answers cold. Our free AI-visibility check shows you the verbatim answer, whether you were named, and which competitors were. Free, ten seconds, and it removes the guessing.

Second, make yourself citable. The AI Overview cites sources it can verify quickly: businesses with a claimed, complete Google Business Profile, a stack of recent reviews, and a website that states services, service area, and prices in plain text — not buried in images or vague “contact us” pages. Our guide on Google Business Profiles and AI answers walks through the profile side; the website side is just as mechanical.

Third, publish what the AI cannot summarize away. General advice (“how to clean gutters”) is summary-bait. Local proof is not: real before-and-after photos of jobs in named neighborhoods, prices with your town’s name on them, service-area pages that say exactly where you go, an FAQ that answers the awkward questions (“why are you more expensive than the guy on the flyer?”). A summary can quote “repipes run $4,000–$12,000” all day; it cannot quote your garage in Henderson and your 5.0-star streak.

Fourth, keep the stuff that converts. The click economy changed; the phone-call economy did not. Reviews keep growing in weight — the August 2026 Spearleaf study found review depth separating recommended businesses (226 vs. 164 reviews) — and every new review is both a trust signal and fresh text the AI can quote.
