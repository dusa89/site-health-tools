Does My Old Developer Still Have Access to My Website?

The website works, the invoice is paid, and you parted on decent terms. Then one evening it hits you: somewhere out there is a list of logins you never got back. This is not a betrayal story. Nobody stole anything. It is just what happens when a handoff ends without a checklist — the keys stay in the other guy's pocket, and nobody notices until something goes wrong.

The short answer: assume yes until you check. Most web projects end with the developer holding logins to half a dozen accounts in your name — and most owners never ask for them back, because asking feels awkward and the site works fine either way. The fix is not a confrontation. It is a checklist, done once, in an afternoon.

The keys he probably still holds

A typical build hands a developer access to far more than the website itself. Walk through these and mark the ones you are not sure about:

  • Your website's admin account. In WordPress this is the Users list — anyone listed as Administrator can change anything. Old agencies often leave a spare admin account "just in case." If your developer's name is on that list, so is his key.
  • The hosting control panel. This is the account at your web host — the place where the whole site can be copied, deleted, or pointed at a different server. Whoever can log in here can undo the website in minutes.
  • FTP / SFTP credentials. These are file-transfer logins that bypass the website's admin screen entirely and reach the raw files. They were handed out to upload the site and are almost never revoked afterward.
  • The domain registrar. This is the account where your domain name lives — GoDaddy, Cloudflare, Google Domains, wherever it was bought. Whoever controls the registrar controls the name itself: they can point it anywhere, let it expire, or lock you out of renewal. Of everything on this list, this is the one that matters most.
  • Business email admin. If the developer set up your email, he may be the administrator on the account — which means he can reset anyone's password, including yours.
  • Google properties. Google Analytics, Search Console, and your Google Business Profile each have their own user lists. Analytics tells you who visits; Search Console controls how Google sees the site; the Business Profile is what customers find on the map. All three can have stale owners from years ago.
  • Social media pages. Facebook pages, Instagram business accounts, LinkedIn company pages — each with roles that survive long after the working relationship.
  • The payment processor. Stripe, PayPal, and Square accounts have team-member lists too. A former developer with processor access can see your money movement.
  • Email marketing and other services. The newsletter account, the review widget, the booking calendar — every service with a login is a key somebody might still hold.

None of this requires malice to be a problem. An old login becomes a problem when it is phished, when a shared password leaks somewhere else, or when a well-meaning ex-helper "fixes" something he was never asked to touch.

How to take them back, in order

Do the two that can end your business first:

  • 1. The registrar. Log in, confirm the account is in your name with your email and your payment method, and remove anyone else. Then turn on two-factor authentication. This is the one account that should have exactly one owner: you.
  • 2. The hosting panel. Same treatment — your name, your email, your password, two-factor on. Change any shared passwords while you are in there.

Then work through the rest:

  • 3. The website admin list. Log into your website as an administrator, open the Users list, and look at everyone with full admin rights. Anyone who no longer works with you gets removed — or demoted to a lower role if they still help occasionally. If he stays on call for emergencies, an editor-level account is plenty; full admin is not a standing privilege.
  • 4. Rotate the shared passwords. Every password the developer ever knew — hosting, FTP, email, services — gets changed to something new that only you know. A password is only yours if nobody else has ever seen it.
  • 5. Rotate API keys and disconnect old apps. If the site connects to other services (payment processors, email tools, calendars), check each service's list of connected apps and API keys, and revoke anything you do not recognize. This is the quiet one people miss.
  • 6. Check the email forwarding rules. This is the sneakiest item on the list: an inbox rule that silently forwards a copy of every incoming email to an outside address. It takes thirty seconds to check and it is the classic leftover. Look in your email settings for forwarding addresses and filter rules you did not create.
  • 7. Turn on two-factor everywhere. Registrar, hosting, email, payment processor, social accounts. Two-factor turns a stolen password from a break-in into a dead end.
  • 8. Write it down. One sheet — physical or digital — listing every account, who has access, and when it was last reviewed. The reason this problem recurs is that access is invisible; the list makes it visible.

This is the companion piece to the question of whether to hand passwords over in the first place. And if the handoff conversation feels uncomfortable, remember who owns security — it was always you, and reclaiming the keys is part of the job.

The conversation, if you still talk

The easiest version of this starts while everyone is still friendly: "Send me a list of every account and login you have for us, so I can file it away." Most developers will comply happily — they do not want the keys either. The list is a liability for them too: if something breaks a year later, the ex-developer with standing access is the first suspect. Framing it as housekeeping, not suspicion, gets the job done without burning a bridge you might need later.

The bottom line

Assume your former developer still has access to everything until you have checked. Start with the domain registrar and the hosting panel — the two accounts that can end the business — then work through the website's admin list, the shared passwords, the API keys, the email forwarding rules, and two-factor everywhere. It is one afternoon, it is pure housekeeping, and it is the cheapest security upgrade your website will ever get.
