How Do I Know If My Website Has Already Been Hacked?

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: a hacked small-business website usually looks perfectly fine to its owner. Modern attackers don’t deface your homepage — that would get them caught. They quietly borrow what you’ve spent years building: your Google reputation, your visitors, your server. The site loads, the phone rings, and meanwhile strangers are cashing in on your domain. The good news is that most infections leave fingerprints, and you can check for them yourself in about ten minutes, with tools that cost nothing. This is the smoke detector. The fire extinguisher — what to do when you find something — is the gets-hacked article.

1. Ask Google what it sees: the site: search

Type site:yourdomain.com into Google (your actual domain, no spaces). This shows you every page Google has indexed under your name. Scroll past the first page. You’re looking for pages you never made: pharmaceutical products, gambling, Japanese or Chinese characters in the titles, gibberish URLs. If you see them, you’ve likely got the most patient hack there is — SEO spam. Attackers plant hundreds of hidden pages inside your site that exist only to rank in Google and send the visitor somewhere else. Your real site keeps working normally on top of them; you keep paying the hosting bill.

The classic version has a name: the Japanese keyword hack. Thousands of fake pages get injected into the site, show up in Google results with Japanese text, quietly damage your rankings, and redirect visitors to fraudulent stores. Google sometimes labels the results “This site may be hacked,” but attackers use cloaking to hide from Google’s own checks — so the manual site: search is the reliable version.

How common is this? Sucuri’s researchers found SEO spam on roughly one in three infected sites they scanned in the first half of 2024 — mostly hidden content, keyword spam, and gambling pages. And it’s a long game: in one documented case, the spam folder was created fifteen months after the attacker’s first login — a folder named css, so it looked like a styling folder nobody opens, holding 323 files: more than 300 pharmaceutical pages dressed in the site’s own header and footer, with their own sitemap so Google would find them faster. Fifteen months of quiet profit, off somebody else’s domain.

2. Check the user list — the smoking gun

In your WordPress dashboard, go to Users. Anyone you don’t recognize is a red flag; an administrator account you didn’t create is close to proof. Attackers add their own admin user so they can stroll back in whenever they like — it’s standard procedure, not an accident. Delete the stranger. Then change every password that matters: WordPress admins, hosting account, FTP. A changed password on a door the attacker still has a key to is theater.

3. Test the redirect — from your phone, not your laptop

Some hacks only redirect certain visitors — mobile users, or Google’s own crawler — a trick called cloaking. Your laptop shows a perfect site; a customer on her phone gets bounced to a pharmacy. Security scanners have whole signature families for this: malicious redirect rules hiding in the site’s configuration that fire only for mobile visitors or only for search-engine crawlers. So don’t test like the owner. Test like a customer: open your site on your phone — or better, ask someone else to, on their phone, on their data plan. If it jumps somewhere you didn’t build, that’s the redirect hack, and it’s been invisible to you precisely because it was designed to be (the redirect deep dive and the phone-only variant cover each half of this).

4. Read your traffic and your host’s mail

A sudden, unexplained drop in visitors can mean Google flagged or demoted you behind your back. Google Search Console — free, built for site owners — has a Security issues report that shows exactly this: hacked-content labels, sample URLs, the works. Check it. And read email from your host instead of skimming it: hosts suspend accounts for malware and phishing, and they warn about excessive outgoing mail. A notice that your site is sending spam email you never wrote — or “undeliverable” bounces for messages you never sent — is a detection, not a false alarm. Same goes for customers telling you their browser called your site dangerous (that warning is Google’s flag, and it follows your domain across every browser).

5. Look for the graffiti: ads and content you didn’t put there

Banners, popups, strange links injected into your pages — that’s someone selling ad space on your site without asking you. It’s one of the oldest monetizations of a hacked site, and owners miss it for months because they don’t browse their own site like a stranger would (the injected-ads deep dive). While you’re in there, note anything else unfamiliar: plugins you didn’t install, files with recent modification dates you can’t explain. You don’t need to understand what the files do — “I didn’t put that there” is the entire diagnostic.

What to do with what you find — and the one thing not to do

Do not just delete the weird page, the strange user, or the odd plugin and call it done. That is the number-one way people get re-hacked. The attacker got in through a door — an outdated plugin, a weak password, a backdoor file planted separately from the visible mess — and deleting the visible mess leaves the door wide open. Cleanup writeups are blunt about this: miss the backdoor or leave the vulnerability unpatched, and the attacker’s automated systems simply re-infect the site within days or weeks. Finding the entry point matters more than deleting the evidence.

So the sequence is: confirm what you found, contain it, then follow the full response playbook — scan, remove, patch the door, change the passwords, request a review from Google if you were flagged. That playbook is the gets-hacked article; this one was the smoke detector, that one is the fire extinguisher. After the cleanup: update everything, remove plugins and themes you don’t use, keep a backup from before the infection so a cleanup never becomes a rebuild from scratch, and run a real malware scan — the free ones compare your site’s files against the official originals and flag anything that doesn’t match.

One last honesty note: no checklist proves a negative. A clean site: search doesn’t prove you’re clean — it proves you’re not showing the loudest symptom. Plenty of infections stay silent, which is why the reliable answer is a real scan rather than a symptom hunt. Run the checks above tonight; they’re free. Then let a scanner do the quiet work every week after.
