Why does my SSL certificate keep expiring?

The padlock was there on Tuesday. Wednesday morning a customer calls: your website says “not secure.” You fixed this exact problem three months ago. Here is why it keeps coming back — and why the industry is making the cycle shorter, not longer.

The certificate is not a one-time purchase. It is a subscription that has to renew itself — and free certificates, the kind most small-business hosts install, only last 90 days. That means your site has to renew successfully four times a year, every year, with nobody watching. Miss one renewal and the padlock becomes a warning overnight. (If the warning is already up on your site, start with why your site says “not secure” to find out which of the three causes is yours.)

Why the renewal you set up keeps breaking

Something changed and the renewal check stopped working. Automatic renewal works by the certificate provider proving, each time, that you still control the domain — a tiny validation file served from your website, or a record in your DNS settings. Move hosts, point the domain through a new security service, change DNS providers, or let a firewall start blocking that validation file, and the check fails. Nobody emails you “renewal is broken.” You just find out at expiry — usually from a customer.

The warning emails go to an inbox nobody reads. Expiry notices go to whatever address is on file — often the web designer’s old address, or an admin@ mailbox nobody opens. The one email that mattered died in a dead inbox.

Someone did it by hand and forgot. If your certificate was installed manually, there is no auto-anything. It is a calendar event someone has to remember four times a year, forever. People remember it twice, then life happens.

The industry is making certificates shorter, not longer

This is not your host being difficult. In April 2025, the CA/Browser Forum — the industry body that sets the rules for certificate authorities — approved a schedule to shorten public certificates: a maximum of 200 days for certificates issued from March 2026, 100 days from March 2027, and 47 days from March 2029. Apple, Google, Microsoft, and Mozilla all voted in favor.

The reasoning is straightforward: a stolen or mis-issued certificate is dangerous only while it is valid, and browsers have never been good at revoking bad ones. Expiry is the real kill switch — so the industry is making everything expire sooner.

The honest upshot for a business owner: hand-renewing is dying. Certificates that renew themselves — with somebody watching to make sure the automation keeps working — are the only strategy that survives this schedule. If your renewal already breaks at 90 days, it will not get better on its own.
