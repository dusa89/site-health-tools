Why does Gmail show a question mark next to my business name?

That gray ? is Gmail's own trust warning — Google's words: your message "isn't authenticated," so Gmail "doesn't know if the message is coming from the person who appears to be sending it." Here is why your emails carry it, what it costs, and the two-level fix: the free one that removes it, and the logo one (BIMI) that comes after.

Picture it from your customer's side. Your quote lands in their Gmail. Before they read a single word, their eye goes to the little circle next to your business name — where most companies have a logo or at least a photo. Yours has a gray question mark. They have not read your price, your promise, or your name. They have already been told, by Gmail itself, to be careful with you.

What the question mark actually means

Google says it plainly on its own support page: “If you see a question mark next to the sender’s name, the message isn’t authenticated. When an email isn’t authenticated, that means Gmail doesn’t know if the message is coming from the person who appears to be sending it.” And Gmail's advice to your customer is just as direct: “be careful about replying or downloading any attachments.”

That is the whole story. The question mark is not a bug, and it is not Gmail insulting your logo. It is a trust warning. Google also notes that unauthenticated messages “aren’t necessarily spam” — legitimate businesses trip this all the time, often without knowing. But from your customer's seat, the difference between “not authenticated” and “dangerous” is exactly zero. The warning does the damage either way.

Why it shows up on YOUR emails

Authentication is the part of email most owners have never heard of — the three checks (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) that tell Gmail your message really came from your domain. The question mark appears when those checks are missing or broken, and the usual culprits are boringly common:

You never set it up. Most small-business email was configured once, years ago, by whoever built the website. If nobody ever added the authentication records to your domain, every email you have ever sent has carried the question mark. You never saw it, because Gmail does not show it to the sender.

A service sends mail “as” you. Quotes, invoices, and booking confirmations often go out through a third-party tool — QuickBooks, your CRM, your booking app — stamping your business name on messages your own domain never authorized. Same gray circle. (This is the close cousin of the “via” label next to your name — both are Gmail narrating the gap between who claims to be sending and who actually is.)

Something broke it. You changed web hosts, moved email providers, or added a new tool, and the authentication records pointed at the old setup. Authentication is invisible until it stops working — then the question marks appear overnight on mail that used to look fine.

What it is quietly costing you

Trust is the currency of every email a business sends, and the question mark spends it at the worst possible moments — the quote that decides the job, the invoice that decides whether you get paid on time. A DigiCert survey of 5,000 consumers in 2025 found that 49% would choose between competing brands based on whether a verified logo appeared in the inbox — and separate industry studies have measured meaningfully higher open rates once the logo is there. (DigiCert and the deliverability vendors who publish these figures all sell certificates and tooling, so read the exact percentages as directional — but the direction is unanimous: the inbox avatar is a trust signal, and a warning icon is the opposite of one.) Your competitor's email arrives with their logo. Yours arrives with a warning. That is not a cosmetic difference. That is the first impression.

The two-level fix (level 1 is free)

Level 1: authenticate — this kills the question mark, and it is free. The question mark disappears the moment your email properly passes SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. No certificate, no trademark, no logo file — just the DNS records that prove your mail is really yours. Our free email deliverability check shows exactly which of the three you are missing and hands you the records to fix it. If you read our DMARC guide, this is the part where DMARC stops being abstract: it is the check that turns the question mark off.

Level 2: get your actual logo in the inbox (BIMI) — the badge, not the fix. Once authentication is solid, the standard called BIMI (Brand Indicators for Message Identification) lets your logo replace the avatar circle in Gmail, Yahoo, and Apple Mail. The requirements are specific, and they are a ladder:

First, DMARC must be at enforcement — a policy of quarantine or reject, applied to all your mail. Google states outright that a monitoring-only policy (p=none) does not qualify; this is the gate almost everyone fails. Second, your logo has to be a special SVG Tiny PS file — square, solid background, under 32 KB, hosted at a public web address. Third, you need a certificate binding the logo to your domain: the traditional Verified Mark Certificate requires a registered trademark (Google notes trademark registration typically takes 6 to 12 months), and published third-party pricing sits around USD $1,000–1,500 a year — rates move, so get a current quote rather than budgeting from an article. Since about 2024–2025 there is also the Common Mark Certificate, which needs no trademark — just proof your logo has been publicly displayed on your domain for at least 12 months. Then you publish one BIMI DNS record tying it all together.

Here is the honest ordering, straight from people who sell this stuff: get to DMARC enforcement first, chase the logo second. Enforcement is the work that actually improves deliverability — fewer spam-folder trips, no more question marks. The logo is the badge you earn for having done it. If Gmail is rejecting your mail outright, the logo is irrelevant until the rejections stop.

The reframe to carry with you

The question mark feels like an insult. Read it instead as a favor: Gmail is showing you, in public, exactly where your trust is leaking — something most business problems never do. Every fix on the ladder makes every email you send after it work harder: the quote, the invoice, the follow-up, all of them arriving with proof they are really from you instead of a warning that they might not be.
