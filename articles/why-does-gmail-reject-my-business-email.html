Why does Gmail reject my business email?

Your quote went to a Gmail customer and came back with a cold error code — or worse, it never came back at all, and you only found out when the customer called someone else. It is almost never your message. It is your domain’s missing paperwork — and since late 2025, Gmail rejects it instead of delivering it.

This one usually breaks the same way: a customer you have emailed for years suddenly stops answering. You check — your last three quotes went to their Gmail, and Gmail said no. Nothing on your end changed. That is exactly the signature of what changed on their end.

What changed — in plain dates

In February 2024, Google and Yahoo started requiring senders to authenticate their email: at minimum, SPF or DKIM set up correctly, for everyone sending to Gmail or Yahoo addresses. Anyone sending 5,000 or more emails a day to Gmail faces the full rulebook — SPF and DKIM both passing, a DMARC record published, the From address matching one of them, the spam-complaint rate kept under 0.3%, and one-click unsubscribe on marketing mail. Microsoft joined in May 2025 with the same 5,000-a-day threshold for Outlook, Hotmail, and Live addresses. And as of November 2025, Gmail hardened enforcement further: non-compliant bulk mail is now permanently rejected at the door — 550 errors — instead of temporarily deferred. (Sources: PowerDMARC’s 2026 bulk-sender rules guide; Greg Pabijan-Morawski’s September 2026 deliverability guide; Warmy’s 2026 SPF, DKIM and DMARC guide.)

But I don’t send 5,000 emails a day

Probably not — and the full bulk rulebook may never name you. But here is the part owners miss: the enforcement wave landed on everyone. Gmail treats unauthenticated domains the same way at every volume — your mail is more likely to be refused or spam-foldered, and the direction of travel is stricter, not looser. And there is a sticky clause worth knowing: once a domain crosses the 5,000-a-day line, Google applies the bulk requirements permanently — even if you send less later. If you ever run a launch, a holiday push, or a big announcement list, get the records right before you send, not after.

The three stamps, in one paragraph

The rules name three records you may have met in our other guides: SPF, the public list of servers allowed to send as you; DKIM, the signature your server adds to each message; and DMARC, your written instruction for what inbox providers should do with fakes of your domain. Our first email guide explains all three in plain English, and the DMARC guide covers the policy that stops impostors. If your mail is being rejected, these three records are the first things to check.

What the error codes actually mean

If a bounced email shows one of these, the failure is authentication — not your subject line: 550-5.7.26 means Gmail found a DMARC alignment failure (a code that started appearing with the November 2025 escalation); 421-4.7.32 means SPF or DKIM alignment failed; 550-5.7.1 means an SPF hard fail; and Outlook’s signature is 550 5.7.515 — “Access denied, sending domain does not meet the required authentication level.” Any of these is your cue to check the records, not rewrite the message.

The spam-rate rule nobody watches

The 0.3% complaint ceiling deserves its own warning. Send a newsletter to 300 people and one of them marks it spam — you have already crossed the line. Keep lists to people who asked to hear from you, make unsubscribing genuinely one click, and watch your complaint rate in Google Postmaster Tools before Gmail has to tell you about it. A purchased list of “local businesses” is the fastest way to fail this check.
