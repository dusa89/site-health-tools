Why do my photos slow down my website?

You uploaded the new photos — the crew, the shop, the finished jobs — and now the site drags. Nobody touched the code, nothing was redesigned. It is the photos. And this is the most fixable speed problem there is.

There is a version of this story every web person has heard a dozen times. An owner calls and says the website used to be fine, and now it loads like it is wading through mud. Nothing changed except one thing: new photos went up. The gallery, the team page, the before-and-afters. The complaint sounds backwards — how can pictures slow a website? — but pictures are usually the biggest thing on the page, and by a lot.

Pictures are the heaviest thing on most pages

The HTTP Archive’s 2025 Web Almanac measured the median mobile home page at 2,362 KB — more than triple the 845 KB of a decade earlier, a 202.8% increase. Of that median page, 911 KB is images — roughly 4 out of every 10 bytes, and the Almanac’s roundups describe images as around half of a typical site’s total weight. More bytes than the JavaScript. More than everything else on the page put together, some days. This is not a code problem. It is a freight problem: your page is carrying a truckload of pictures down a phone connection.

The camera-file trap

Here is how it usually happens. The photos come straight from a phone or a camera — sharp, beautiful, print-quality — and get uploaded exactly as they arrived. A phone photo can be several megabytes. A hero banner image can be 3 to 8 MB. Audits of real business sites routinely find hero images in that range that should be under 200 KB, product photos at 1 to 2 MB that should be under 100 KB. One 8 MB hero image can turn a one-second page into an eleven-second page all by itself.

Think of it this way: your phone takes a photo detailed enough to print on a poster, and your website shows it in a slot the size of a postcard. All the extra detail is weight the customer never sees but still downloads. The fix is not taking worse photos — it is uploading them at the size the page actually displays.

The format problem: your JPEG is from 1992

The most common photo format on the web, JPEG, dates to 1992. Google’s answer was WebP: in a trial of about a million web images, Google measured savings of around 39% at no perceptible quality loss, and its published figures say lossy WebP runs 25 to 34% smaller than comparable JPEGs at the same quality (lossless WebP is about 26% smaller than PNG). A newer format, AVIF, goes smaller still but is not supported everywhere yet. One honest caveat: the savings are averages — some images save a lot, some a little, and it is worth checking the actual files rather than trusting a headline number.

Many site builders (Squarespace, Wix) and WordPress plugins convert images automatically now — which is worth verifying, not assuming. If your site is still serving every photo as the original JPEG or PNG, you are leaving the easiest bytes on the table.

The loading-order problem: everything first, hero last

The third problem is not the size of the photos but the order they load. Browsers load page resources roughly in the order they appear, so photos far down the page — the gallery, the footer, the testimonials row — often start downloading before the big photo at the top has finished. Google’s speed scores (Core Web Vitals) measure how fast the largest thing on the screen appears — the LCP element — and on many business sites that element is the hero photo. A slow hero is a bad score; a bad score is a bad ranking signal.

The fix has two halves that sound contradictory but are not: the hero photo should load first, and every photo below the fold should wait until the customer scrolls near it. That second half is called lazy loading, and most site builders offer it as a checkbox. The hero should never be lazy-loaded — that is the one image you want front of the line.
