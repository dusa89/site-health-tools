Can Someone Steal My Domain Name?

Someone stole a small business’s domain name last year and the business didn’t find out for three days — because the thieves didn’t touch the website. They took the domain itself: one unlock, one transfer code, one registrar move, and suddenly the email stopped working, the website showed someone else’s page, and the real owner had to fight for weeks to get it back. Your domain was paid for, renewed, and safe — and none of that stopped the theft. Here is how it actually happens, and the five steps that make it fail.

One clarification before we start: this is not about forgetting to renew your domain (that is our guide on what happens when a domain name expires). An expired domain is your fault and you get it back by paying. A stolen domain is taken while it is fully paid for — and the person who takes it holds all the cards until you get it back. Different problem, different defenses. This guide is the theft side.

The short answer: yes — a domain is stolen through the registrar account, not through the website. Whoever can log into your domain registrar (GoDaddy, Namecheap, Google Domains, whoever you registered with) can unlock your domain, ask for the transfer code, and move it to a registrar they control. Your website password, your hosting, your WordPress login — none of that matters to the thief. The registrar login is the whole game.

How a domain theft actually works

It is embarrassingly ordinary. Here are the four moves:

1. They get your registrar login. Usually through your email: reset the registrar password, intercept the reset email, log in. Or they buy credentials from a data breach. Or the weakest version — the domain was registered by your old developer in their account, and a dispute (or their own compromise) turns into your loss. (That one has its own guide: does your old developer still have access?)

2. They unlock the domain. Domains ship with a transfer lock on — the registrar-level status clientTransferProhibited, which blocks any transfer to another registrar. The thief turns it off with one click, because they are logged in as you.

3. They take the AuthInfo code. The transfer code — also called the EPP or authorization code — is the secret that proves the requester is the owner. Your registrar must give it to the registered name holder (ICANN’s own transfer policy says so, and says the holder can demand it and the lock’s removal within 5 calendar days if there’s no self-service button). The thief grabs it in seconds.

4. They move the domain out — and it auto-completes. At the new registrar they start the transfer, paste the code, and if nobody objects, the transfer completes on its own after about 5–7 days. The moment the domain lands elsewhere, they point your domain’s DNS at their own servers. Your website shows their page. Your business email on that domain stops reaching you — password resets for everything else, invoices, customer replies — gone with it.

That last part is why a stolen domain is worse than a hacked website: a hacked site can be restored from a backup; a stolen domain takes your email, your Google Business Profile verification, and your customers’ trust in every address that mentions it.

The 5-step lockdown (in this order)

Step 1 — Today: two-factor authentication on the registrar account. This is the single highest-leverage move on this page. The attack runs on a password-only registrar login; an authenticator app on your phone kills the password-reset path stone dead. Do it before you finish this article. Every registrar offers it — it’s usually under account settings or security.

Step 2 — Today: check the transfer lock is ON. In your registrar’s control panel, confirm the domain shows locked — the status is clientTransferProhibited in technical terms. It costs nothing and it is the latch on the door: with it on, nobody can start an inter-registrar transfer. (You’ll turn it off yourself if you ever move registrars on purpose — that’s normal.)

Step 3 — This week: own the one email that rules everything. The registrant contact email — the address on the domain’s registration — is where transfer confirmations and password resets go. If that address is your old Gmail, your developer’s inbox, or an address at a provider you left, the theft path runs through an inbox you don’t control. Point it at an email you actively own and check, and lock that email with two-factor too.

Step 4 — This week: WHOIS privacy. Public WHOIS shows the world your name, email, and phone — the exact details a social-engineering attacker needs to impersonate you to a registrar’s support line. Privacy protection (free at most registrars now) replaces your details with the registrar’s — fewer targets painted on you.

Step 5 — When the domain matters enough: registry lock. The registrar lock in step 2 stops outsiders; it does nothing once someone is logged in as you (a security handbook from OWASP says this explicitly — the account-owned case). Registry lock sits one level above the registrar: changes then need an out-of-band verification — a phone call or signed request the registry verifies independently — before anything moves. It’s a paid, manual service (Verisign Registry Lock, and registrar services like CSC and MarkMonitor offer it). For a domain your whole business runs on, it’s the deadbolt behind the latch.

Two more worth a line: clientUpdateProhibited blocks nameserver changes (so an attacker can’t just repoint your DNS without a transfer), and clientDeleteProhibited blocks accidental deletion. Ask your registrar if they can set them — some do by default with the transfer lock.

What not to do

Don’t hand the AuthInfo/EPP code around by email. That code is the key to the whole domain — the same policy that says you can demand it also assumes you’ll treat it like a password. The new transfer rules coming through ICANN (approved at ICANN82 in March 2025) turn the code into a short-lived, request-only TAC that expires after about 14 days by default — generate it when you need it, use it, and never forward it around.

Don’t leave the domain in someone else’s account. If your developer, agency, or ex-partner holds the domain in their registrar account, you don’t own your domain — you rent it from your relationship with them. Move it into your own account. It takes a week and it’s the move that matters most after 2FA.

Don’t rely on the lock alone if the email is weak. A transfer lock you can turn off by answering a security question on a five-year-old email account isn’t a lock. Step 1 (2FA on the registrar) plus step 3 (own the contact email) is what makes step 2 real.

Don’t assume “my registrar is too small to be targeted.” Attackers don’t target registrars — they target owners. The small business whose email and site both run on one domain is a better target than a big company with a security team, because the ransom works faster.

If it already happened

Speed matters more than anything — transfers can complete in under a week if nobody objects. Call your registrar (the one you lost the domain from) immediately, not by email: report the domain as stolen, ask them to freeze any pending transfer, and document everything. File ICANN’s registrar complaint process and the transfer dispute channel if the registrar won’t act. Keep your registration receipts, renewal invoices, and WHOIS history screenshots — proof of prior ownership is your case. For clear theft (not a billing dispute), a police report establishes the paper trail. Honest note: recovery is a process measured in days to weeks, and it starts with the registrar you bought the domain from — that’s why every step above is about never needing this section.

The bottom line

Your domain is stolen through one login: the registrar’s. Two-factor on that account, the transfer lock on, the contact email in your hands, privacy on the WHOIS record, and a registry lock for the domain the business lives on — five steps, under an hour, and the theft fails at step one instead of completing at step four. An expired domain costs you a renewal fee. A stolen domain costs you your email, your site, and your name on the internet — do the lockdown.
