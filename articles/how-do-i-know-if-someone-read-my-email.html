How Do I Know If Someone Read My Email?

You sent the quote three days ago. Nothing back. So you do the thing every business owner has done a hundred times: you stare at the sent folder and wonder — did they read it, or did it vanish into the void? Email has two systems that claim to answer that question, read receipts and tracking pixels, and both are weaker than anyone selling you on them will admit. Here is how each one actually works, why the “opened” column in your reports is mostly noise, and what genuinely tells you a customer saw your email.

First, the distinction this guide is built on: this is not a deliverability question. If the email never arrived at all, that is a different problem (we have a whole guide on quotes customers swear they never got). This guide starts after arrival. The email landed. Did a human open it? The honest answer is messier than the tools want you to believe, and it changed permanently in 2021.

The short answer: read receipts only work when both sides agree to them — and your free Gmail account cannot send them at all. Tracking pixels, the invisible dots marketers embed in emails, can no longer be trusted because Apple started firing them automatically for everyone. What actually tells you someone read your email is behavior: replies, clicks, and the follow-up you were going to send anyway.

Read receipts: the system that asks permission

Read receipts are the polite system. When you send an email, you attach a request: “please tell me when you open this.” The recipient’s email program shows them a prompt, and they can say yes or no. That is the whole honest story — and it is why receipts are rarer than owners expect.

Gmail’s version comes with fine print. It exists only for Google Workspace — the paid business version of Gmail. If you send from a free @gmail.com account, the option does not exist for you at all (one more reason the free Gmail address is a bad business move). Even on Workspace, your admin has to turn the feature on first — it is off by default. You request it per email from the desktop website’s compose window, and the Gmail phone app cannot initiate a request. And the other side still gets the prompt: they can decline, and many corporate setups are configured to decline automatically. So the absence of a receipt proves nothing — it means they said no, their system blocked it, or they never saw the prompt.

Outlook’s version is the same shape. The recipient can decline the receipt, their IT department can set the whole company to decline, and plenty of people hit “no” out of habit. Treat read receipts as a tool for internal paperwork — invoices, deadlines, anything where the other side already agreed to play along — not as a customer-mind-reading device.

Tracking pixels: the system that spies

Tracking pixels are the other system, and they work without asking. A tracking pixel is a tiny invisible image embedded in the email, tied to the recipient’s address. When the recipient’s email program loads the image, the sender’s server records an “open.” Notice what “opened” actually means here: the image loaded. It does not mean a human read a single word, lingered on the price, or even glanced at the screen.

For years that was close enough. Then three forces broke it, and the biggest one came from Apple.

1 — Apple started opening everyone’s email. In September 2021, Apple rolled out Mail Privacy Protection with iOS 15 (later iPadOS and the Mac). When the feature is on — and Apple prompts every Apple Mail user to turn it on — Apple pre-loads all remote content in the email, including tracking pixels, through its own servers before the message even reaches the person. The pixel fires. The email shows as “opened.” The person may never open it, may delete it unread tomorrow, may have never heard of you. A huge share of the “opens” in any email report today are Apple’s servers doing their job, not customers doing theirs. Apple also masks the recipient’s IP address in the process, so the time-and-location detail marketers used to get is gone with it.

2 — Security scanners open everything too. Corporate spam and malware filters routinely “open” every incoming message to scan it before delivery. Each scan fires the pixel. If you email offices — property managers, clinics, contractors’ back offices — some of your opens are software doing security, not people doing business.

3 — Image blocking goes the other way. Many email programs block images by default. If the reader never taps “load images,” the pixel never fires — and your report says “unopened” for an email the customer read twice and forwarded to their spouse. False negatives, sitting right next to the false positives.

Add it up: the “opened” column is now noise in both directions. Inflated by Apple and scanners, deflated by image blocking, and never once proof that anyone read anything. The email industry’s dirty secret since 2021 is that open rate stopped being a measurement and became a mood.

What actually tells you someone read it

Replies. Obvious and still king. A reply is the only open that cannot be faked.

Clicks. If your quote or proposal lives behind a link — a quote PDF on your site, a proposal page, a scheduling link — the click is the honest signal. Someone had to be engaged enough to press something. Clicks survived the privacy changes far better than opens did.

The follow-up you were going to send anyway. The oldest tracking system in business: “Hi, just checking you got a chance to look at the quote.” Day 3, day 7. No pixel required, and it moves the sale forward whether or not they opened the email. If you catch yourself refreshing the sent folder instead of following up, the folder is the problem — not the customer.

What not to do

Don’t buy “100% accurate open tracking” claims. No product on earth can beat Apple’s pre-loading — the pixel fires before the human exists in the picture. Any vendor promising perfect opens is selling you 2020. Walk away.

Don’t read silence as rejection. Silence after a quote can mean the pixel never fired, the receipt was declined, the email went to the promotions tab, or — most likely — the customer is busy and your follow-up is what actually closes it. The tracking tools give you none of this, so stop letting them write the story.

Don’t paste tracking pixels into one-to-one customer emails. The pixels are newsletter technology. In a personal quote or a reply to a real person, they add nothing — some filters flag them, and if a customer ever learns you were silently tracking their opens, the relationship gets weird. Receipts for invoices, follow-ups for quotes, pixels for the newsletter — and even there, read the numbers as direction, not truth.

The bottom line

Read receipts need the other side’s cooperation, and your free Gmail account cannot even send them. Tracking pixels used to be close enough to the truth, and since September 2021 they have not been. What tells you a customer read your email is the same as it ever was: they replied, they clicked, or you followed up and they told you. Spend the energy you were spending on the sent folder on the follow-up instead — it is the only tracking system that has never lied.
