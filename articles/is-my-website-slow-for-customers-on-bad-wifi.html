Is My Website Slow for Customers on Bad Wifi?

Here is a complaint owners hear and cannot explain: a customer says the website took forever, but it opens instantly every time you check it. Nobody is lying. You are testing on good wifi with a fast connection. They were on a weak signal in a parking lot, a crowded coffee shop, or a rural road with one bar. Your website did not change between those two moments. The pipe it had to squeeze through did. And this is one of the most common reasons a perfectly fine website loses perfectly good customers.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - connections, devices, and pages differ. The figures below describe the web in general; the free speed check at the bottom tells you what your own homepage weighs, which is the number that matters for you.

Your page has a weight, and the connection has a size

Think of your website as a delivery truck and the visitor's internet connection as the road. Your office wifi is a six-lane highway: even a heavy truck flies down it. A customer on a weak signal is a one-lane dirt road. The truck is the same. The road is not.

Websites have gotten heavy. The median page on the mobile web now weighs about 2.6 megabytes, and about 3 megabytes on desktop (HTTP Archive's August 2026 crawl, checked this week). Images are the biggest single chunk - roughly 911 kilobytes of the median mobile page - with JavaScript next at about 632 kilobytes. That is the average page. Half the web is heavier. And here is the part owners miss: on a fast connection, weight barely registers. On a slow one, it is the whole story.

The 5-seconds-per-megabyte rule

Do the math with me, because this is the entire article in one number. Google's own speed test grades your mobile page on a simulated slow connection: 1.6 megabits per second, which Lighthouse's own documentation describes as roughly the bottom quarter of 4G and the top quarter of 3G connections. That is not a torture test. That is a plausible real customer.

Now the arithmetic (ours, from the published figures): one megabyte is 8 megabits. At 1.6 megabits per second, each megabyte of your page takes about 5 seconds just to download, before the phone does a lick of work with it. The median 2.6-megabyte page needs roughly 13 seconds of pure downloading on that connection. Thirteen seconds before anything is even processed. Google's long-standing research found that the chance a visitor leaves climbs 32% when load time goes from 1 to 3 seconds, and 90% from 1 to 5 seconds. At 13 seconds, the customer is long gone - they never saw your headline, your photos, or your phone number.

And downloading is not the only tax. The median mobile page makes about 70 separate requests - images, scripts, stylesheets, fonts - and each round trip on a slow connection costs a fraction of a second that adds up, especially when requests have to wait in line behind each other. Heavy pages do not just load slowly on bad connections. They compound.

Why you never see it yourself

You check your website on office wifi or home fiber, on a phone you bought recently, probably with the page already cached from last time. That is the best-case scenario stacked three deep. Your customers check it on hotel wifi shared with 40 rooms, on a phone from 2021, on a road trip with one bar of signal, on a prepaid plan where every megabyte also costs them real money. (Our fast-for-me article covers the geography version of this gap - the server being far away. This is the pipe version: the server can be next door and a thin connection still punishes a heavy page.)

Notice the mismatch this creates: Google judges your mobile page on that simulated slow connection, and your real visitors on slow connections live the same experience every day. The median mobile page takes 7.9 seconds to fully load in lab tests (versus 4.7 on desktop), and mobile now carries about 59% of web traffic. More than half your visitors are on the device-and-connection combo that suffers most. (Our slow-on-mobile article covers the phone-as-a-weaker-device half of that story - this is the connection half.)

The fixes that cost nothing

1. Find out what your page weighs. The free speed check below reports your homepage's total weight in megabytes. Apply the rule of thumb: multiply by 5 to get the rough download time on a slow connection. If your homepage is 4 megabytes, that is about 20 seconds for a customer on a weak signal. Now you know the size of the problem in plain numbers.

2. Kill the heaviest single file first. One autoplay background video can outweigh everything else on the page combined. Replace it with a still image and a play button: customers who want the video still get it, and everyone else stops paying for it. (Our photos article covers the most common heavy-file fix of all - and our next-gen formats decoder explains the one format setting that shrinks images without visibly changing them.)

3. Turn on compression. Your server can "zip" your pages before sending them, the same way a zip file shrinks a document. Text files - the code and styling - shrink dramatically. Most caching plugins and decent hosts do this with one toggle. It is free, invisible, and it directly cuts the megabytes a slow connection has to carry.

4. Stop sending desktop photos to phones. A shocking number of business websites send the same giant image to every device. A phone screen never needs the 2400-pixel-wide version. Modern website builders can serve a smaller file to smaller screens automatically - if yours does not, this is a ten-minute job for your web person, not a rebuild.

5. Delete the trackers and widgets you never look at. Every analytics script, chat widget, and social feed is another download on a thin pipe - and another of those 70 requests. Audit them the way you would audit subscriptions: if nobody has opened the dashboard in six months, it is dead weight your slowest customers are paying to download.

6. Lazy-load what is below the fold. Images further down the page do not need to load before the customer sees the top. Lazy loading means the page fetches them only as the customer scrolls. On a slow connection, this is the difference between "the top of the page appears in 3 seconds" and "nothing appears for 13."

One honest caveat: caching helps your repeat visitors - our caching article explains how. But a first-time customer on a slow connection still has to download every byte once. There is no shortcut around a page that is simply too heavy. (And if the site feels slow even on good wifi, that is a different problem - our feels-slow article walks through the psychology of waiting versus real delays.)

The bottom line

You cannot control your customers' connections. You can control your page's weight. Every megabyte you cut is about 5 seconds handed back to the customer on the worst connection - the customer in the parking lot, on the road, on the prepaid plan, the one your competitor's lighter site just served in 3 seconds flat. Weight is the one speed factor that is entirely in your hands, costs nothing to fix, and helps every single visitor, fast connection or slow.
