Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 - Which Should My Business Email Live On?

The short answer: stop comparing the price, and start comparing the people. Since July 2026, when Microsoft raised its small-business prices, the two suites cost the same at every tier - $7, $14, or $22 per person per month on the annual plan, Google or Microsoft. So the decision is not which is cheaper. It is three questions: where does your team actually do its work - in a browser tab or in desktop Office apps; what does the rest of your software expect; and do you want the AI assistant included or sold separately? Answer those and the winner is obvious. Below: what each tier buys, the three questions, the switching-cost reality nobody mentions, and the exact script for your web person.

1. The price question - settled in July 2026

For years, Google Workspace was the budget option. That ended on July 1, 2026, when Microsoft raised its small-business prices: Business Basic went from $6 to $7 per person per month, Business Standard from $12.50 to $14, and Business Premium held at $22. (If you already had Microsoft 365, the new rate applies at your next renewal, not immediately.) The result: both lineups now read $7 / $14 / $22 per person per month on an annual plan. So if anyone tells you one is "the cheap one," they are reading old pricing. Two money details still differ. First, Google Workspace can be bought month to month - roughly $8.40 / $16.80 / $26.40 for the three tiers, so a few dollars more than the annual rate for the freedom to cancel. Microsoft's small-business plans mostly want the annual commitment for the headline price. Second, both companies run intro discounts that expire - whatever number the salesperson quotes you, ask what it becomes in year two. (If paying per person per month for a suite sounds steep compared to the free email that came with your hosting, read the hosting-email article - that is the real comparison, and when each is right.)

2. What the money actually buys at each tier

At $7 a month, you get business email on your own domain from both - Gmail vs Outlook. The packages around it differ: Google's Starter gives 30 GB of pooled storage per person plus Drive, Docs, Sheets, and Meet video calls. Microsoft's Basic gives web and mobile versions of the Office apps only - no desktop Word or Excel - plus 1 TB of OneDrive storage and Teams. At $14, the split sharpens: Google's Standard adds 2 TB of pooled storage, meeting recording, shared drives, and Gemini AI features built in. Microsoft's Standard adds the full desktop Office apps - real Excel on your computer, not in a browser tab. At $22: Google's Plus brings 5 TB pooled storage, Vault, and stronger security controls; Microsoft's Premium adds the whole security and device-management stack - Entra ID, Intune, Defender for Business. The honest tier guide: a team of two or three who live in a browser is usually fine at $7 on Google; a business whose office runs on desktop Excel or Outlook is really buying the $14 Microsoft plan; the $22 tier is for businesses with employees to manage, devices to lock down, or compliance to prove. (Pooled storage is Google's quiet advantage at the high end - one person's unused gigabytes help cover another's. Microsoft gives each person their own 1 TB.)

3. Question one - where does your team actually work?

This is the question that ends most debates. There are browser people and Office people, and most small businesses are one or the other. Browser people do everything in tabs: email, documents, spreadsheets shared by link, video calls. They love that a document opens on any computer instantly, that three people can type in it at once, and that nothing needs installing. That is Google Workspace's home turf - it was built for the browser first. Office people live in desktop apps: they open real Excel files, they have spreadsheets with formulas and macros that Google Sheets would chew up, their staff double-clicks icons instead of bookmarks. That is Microsoft 365's home turf - the desktop apps are the product, everything else rides along. Ask your bookkeeper which one she reaches for. The person who does your numbers every day answers this question for you, because her spreadsheets are the hardest thing to move and the worst thing to get wrong. (Still on a free personal Gmail address? The consumer-Gmail article covers why your business email needs its own lane before you pick a suite.)

4. Question two - what does the rest of your software expect?

Email does not live alone. Your accounting software, your CRM, your industry tools, and your customers all lean one way or the other. Three checks: Your accounting and line-of-business software. Some accounting tools and industry apps integrate deeply with Outlook or OneDrive and only shallowly with Gmail - or the reverse. Ask your accountant or your software's support which ecosystem it plays better with before you buy. Your customers and vendors. If your corporate clients all live in Outlook, there are real frictions: meeting invites that render slightly wrong, calendar quirks, Teams links they expect you to join. If your customers are consumers on Gmail, the reverse applies. Nobody switches suites over this alone, but it is a tiebreaker when the first question was close. Your compliance and data needs. Neither suite backs up your email for you automatically - that is a separate purchase on both sides, and a business with legal record-keeping needs should budget for it. The $22 tier is where the serious admin controls live on both platforms: who can do what, device lockdown, audit trails. If you have employees, compare the two $22 tiers on the admin controls you will actually use - that is where the money goes to work.

5. Question three - the AI question

Both companies sell you an AI helper, and the packaging is the biggest real difference left. Google includes Gemini in Workspace plans - the Standard tier and up get the full set of AI features across Gmail, Docs, and Sheets as part of the $14. Microsoft sells Copilot separately - the small-business add-on lists around $21 per person per month on top of the base plan, so a Standard user with Copilot runs about $35 a month before you count a single feature. There are also bundled plans (Standard with Copilot around $23.50, Premium with Copilot around $32, list pricing). Here is the honest framing: do not buy the AI for the email alone. AI that drafts your emails and summarizes your inbox is worth money only if you personally drown in email every day - most owners do not, and their staff will try it for a week and stop. The businesses that get real value from these assistants are the ones with heavy document, spreadsheet, and meeting loads - the same people who answered question one with "desktop Office" or "browser documents." Buy the suite for where your team works; treat the AI as a separate decision with its own math. (Microsoft's free in-app Copilot Chat got meaningfully better in the July 2026 update - inbox and calendar awareness - so test the free layer before paying for the add-on.)

6. The switching-cost reality nobody mentions

Whatever you choose, two things are true forever. Your domain is yours, not theirs. Your business email address belongs to your domain name, which you own at your registrar - the suite is just where the mailboxes live. Switching suites later means changing the mail-routing records (MX records) and moving old mailboxes over - real work, but not starting over. (The full mechanics: the email-switching article.) The cost of picking wrong is a weekend, not your business. A migration between the two suites for a small team is days of work, not a disaster. That is why the three questions above matter more than the feature lists: pick the one that matches how you already work, and you will never need to migrate. One more trap: moving TO a suite from the free email that came with your hosting is also a migration - same MX-record dance, same mailbox move. If your web person set up your hosting email and you outgrow it, the suite decision and the migration are one project, not two. And whichever suite you choose, your deliverability setup still has to be done right - the Gmail-rejection article covers why big providers reject business email that isn't authenticated properly.

7. The two things neither suite does for you

Honesty section, because sales pages skip it. Neither backs up your email automatically. If you delete something - or an ex-employee deletes something on the way out - the suite's own recovery window is short. Businesses that cannot afford to lose mail buy a separate backup service on top. Neither sets up your email authentication for you. The SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records that keep your mail out of spam folders are still your job (or your web person's job) to configure - the suite gives you the values, someone has to put them in your domain's settings. And the locking: both suites offer two-factor on mailboxes, but you have to turn it on - the 2FA article covers it for business email. The admin console both suites give you - the place to add and remove people, suspend accounts, and keep a departing employee's mailbox - is one of the real upgrades over free options: the employee-exit article walks through using it.

8. What to tell your web person

Send your assistant or web person this: "We are putting our business email on [Google Workspace / Microsoft 365] at the [Starter/Basic - $7 / Standard - $14 / Premium or Plus - $22] tier, on the annual plan. Four asks. First, confirm we own our domain at the registrar and that you have the login - the domain is ours, the suite just hosts the mailboxes. Second, set up the suite's mailboxes and point our domain's MX records at them, and confirm a test email sends and receives on every mailbox before anything else changes. Third, set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC on our domain and prove they pass - send a test to Gmail and check the authentication passes, not just that it arrives. Fourth, turn on two-factor authentication on the admin account and every mailbox, and set up the migration of our old mail so nothing is lost. Plain English, and show me each piece working." The proof is the checklist: mailboxes created, MX records pointing at the suite, a successful test send and receive on each one, authentication records passing, 2FA on, old mail migrated. If your web person did this for another client, ask them which suite that client chose and whether the team was happy a year later - the year-two answer is the honest one.
