What does “website accessibility” actually mean?

When a blind customer visits your website, they don’t see it — they hear it. A screen reader reads the page aloud, top to bottom: the headings, the links, the form fields, the pictures. A keyboard-only visitor tabs from item to item instead of using a mouse. If your page was built without these visitors in mind, whole parts of it simply don’t work for them: pictures with no description, form fields with no label, buttons that announce themselves as “button” and nothing else.

This matters twice. First, it’s customers you can’t afford to lose — a meaningful share of the public browses with assistive technology, and they spend money with businesses whose sites work for them. Second, the law: the Americans with Disabilities Act requires businesses that serve the public to be accessible, and courts have applied that to websites. There is now a steady stream of demand letters and lawsuits over inaccessible small-business sites — and the items they cite are almost always the same short list this page checks: images with no descriptions, unlabeled form fields, links with no names.

This scan reads your homepage the way an automated accessibility checker does and scores the 8 things it can judge from the page code: image descriptions, form labels, link and button names, the declared page language, the page title, heading structure, and mobile viewport settings — plus, honestly labeled, the one it can’t: color contrast, which needs the page rendered the way a visitor sees it.

What this scan can’t see: whether a keyboard-only visitor can actually reach everything on the page, whether your text contrast passes, whether focus stays visible while tabbing, whether your videos have captions. Those need the rendered page and a human pass — they’re part of the full 40-point audit, where every finding is verified against your live site.
