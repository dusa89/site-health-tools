Why does my contact form keep getting spam?

Crypto pitches, SEO offers, random characters at 3am — through your form, not to your spam folder. The two kinds of form spam, the free invisible fix, and the one kind nothing technical stops.

Every morning the same routine: three new "inquiries," and none of them are customers. A crypto promotion. An SEO agency pitching you in broken English. A message that is just random characters. Your contact form has become a spam inbox — and this is a completely different problem from your own contact-form emails landing in spam. That other problem (your replies going to the junk folder) is deliverability; this guide is about the junk coming IN through your form. Two problems, two fixes.

The good news first: form spam comes in two kinds, and each has a defense. Kind one is bots — automated scripts filling out every form on the internet, looking for a way in. Kind two is human spammers — real people pasting sales pitches by hand. The defenses work on kind one. Nothing technical stops kind two; that one needs a different answer (below).

Layer 1: the honeypot — free, invisible, do it first

The cheapest defense costs nothing and your visitors never see it: a honeypot field. It is an extra field on the form that humans cannot see but bots fill in anyway, because bots fill in everything. Any submission with that field filled gets thrown away. Done right, it has three rules. One: name it something believable, like "website" or "company" — never "honeypot," because smarter bots skip fields with that name. Two: do not hide it with a simple CSS trick alone; more advanced bots look for that pattern, and it must be marked properly (aria-hidden, removed from keyboard tab order) so screen readers and autofill do not trip it by accident. Three, and this is the one people get wrong: the check has to happen on the server — in the code that receives the form — not just in the visitor's browser. A browser-only check is a suggestion; a bot ignores suggestions (web guide, msangeetha.com, September 2026).

Pair it with a time check. A bot submits a form in milliseconds; a human needs at least several seconds to type a message. Anything that arrives faster than a human could possibly write is automation, and rejecting it catches the bots that slip past the honeypot. Both layers together are the standard first line — free, invisible to real customers, and they kill the majority of automated junk.

Where the honeypot stops working

Be honest about the limits so you do not waste money chasing the wrong layer. Bots that load the page in a full browser (headless browsers) can recognize hidden fields and leave them empty. And human spammers — the SEO pitch, the "dear business owner" template — are real people; no invisible field stops a person. A honeypot is a free first layer, not the whole defense (same September 2026 guide).

Layer 2: only when the honeypot is not enough

If spam is still getting through, the next step is a challenge layer — and it goes on only the forms being attacked, because it is the one defense that can annoy real customers. The two serious options: Cloudflare Turnstile — usually completely invisible to the visitor, privacy-focused, free for the vast majority of sites; and Google reCAPTCHA v3 — also invisible and score-based, free up to 10,000 assessments a month, then usage-priced. The older checkbox-and-image-puzzle versions still exist but add visible friction, so they are the last resort, not the starting point (comparison, formgent.com, September 2026).

And the critical detail most setups get wrong: the verification has to run inside the form's own receiver. A check that only runs on the page does nothing against a spammer who never loads the page — who posts straight to the form's endpoint. The token must be verified on every request, before anything is sent, or it is decoration. One site documented exactly this failure: the spammer's requests never touched the page, so the page-level check never ran — moving the verification into the receiving function stopped the spam cold (dev.to, September 2026).

The spam nothing technical stops

Then there is kind two: a real human submitting a real sales pitch. No honeypot, CAPTCHA, or challenge can tell a genuine inquiry from a genuine pitch, because both come from a person. Every spam guide quietly admits this (formgent.com, September 2026). The defense is human: recognize the pattern (vague flattery, no mention of your actual business, a link to a service), and either ignore it or set up a filter that routes messages with those markers to a folder you check weekly instead of your inbox. It is a content problem, solved with judgment, not software.

The ask-your-developer script

You do not need to understand any of the above to get it fixed. Send your web person this: "My contact form is getting spam. Please add a honeypot field and a submission-time check to the form, verified on the server side. If that doesn't stop it, add Cloudflare Turnstile to that form only." That is the whole job, in priority order, and any competent developer can do layer 1 in under an hour. Do not accept "we installed a CAPTCHA on every form" as the first answer — that trades your customers' patience for the developer's convenience.
