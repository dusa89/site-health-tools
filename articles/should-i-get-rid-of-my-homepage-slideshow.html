Should I Get Rid of My Homepage Slideshow?

The rotating slideshow on your homepage is the most expensive square inch of your website. It looks busy and professional — but every slide is another large photo your customers' phones have to download before the page is finished, and the research says almost nobody waits around to see slide two. Here is what the studies found, what each slide costs you in seconds, and the one-slide rule that keeps the look without the penalty.

Open your own homepage on your phone, on cellular data, and time it. Now ask yourself: how many of the slides in your rotating banner did a visitor see before they scrolled past? The answer, measured on real websites, is usually one. You paid for the other four in download time. This is the case against the homepage slideshow — and the one way to keep it if you insist.

Almost nobody sees slide two

In 2013, the University of Notre Dame measured what actually happens on a homepage carousel. Over three months, the rotating feature was shown about 315,665 times and clicked 28,928 times — roughly one visitor in a hundred clicked any slide at all. Of those clicks, 84% landed on the first slide; each remaining slide collected around 4%. Read that again: the second, third, fourth, and fifth slides combined earned about one-sixth of an already tiny 1%. The University of York ran its own test the same year and found the same shape: about half of all carousel clicks go to the first position, with attention falling off sharply after that. Cutting the carousel down to five or fewer slides was the one change that consistently earned more clicks — fewer choices, more attention.

Why does this happen? Nielsen Norman Group's usability testing gives the mechanism: content inside an auto-rotating carousel gets treated as advertising. Because it moves, visitors assume it might be an ad, and they scroll past it — a documented case of banner blindness. In one of their tests, a user could not find the special offer they were asked to locate even though it appeared in large type — because it was inside the carousel, and their eyes had already dismissed the whole region. Your rotating banner is not just unclicked. For many visitors, it is invisible.

What each slide costs you in seconds

The speed cost is structural, not a matter of a bad slideshow plugin. Every slide is another large photo the browser must fetch before the top of your page looks finished, plus the JavaScript library that runs the rotation — and all of it sits on your homepage's critical path, the sequence of downloads nothing else can begin until it finishes. Three slides means three hero-sized images competing for the same slow phone connection. Five slides means five. This is the photo-weight problem multiplied by the number of slides — and it is one of the four classic speed killers in the general speed overview, except here it lives exactly where it hurts most: the first screen of your most-visited page. If your homepage is slow while the rest of your site is fine, count the slides — the carousel is the first suspect.

There is a secondary cost too: things that move on a page push other things around. Auto-rotating content is one of the classic triggers of the page-jumping problem — the layout shifts as slides swap, and a visitor who was about to tap your phone number taps the wrong thing instead. So the slideshow costs you twice: seconds of loading, and mis-taps on the content that actually converts.

Why it is still everywhere (the real reason)

Slideshows survive because they solve an internal problem, not a customer one. Four departments each want their message on the homepage, and nobody wants to be the one left off — so everyone gets a slide, and the carousel is the compromise. Usability researchers have a name for the result: content delivered through carousels is missed by users in test after test, because the compromise produces five messages where the visitor reads one. The honest question is not “which slides do we keep?” but “which single message deserves the top of the page?” The team whose message is not slide one already lost the argument with the visitors — the analytics just have not caught up to the meeting room yet.

The one-slide rule (how to keep the look without the penalty)

You do not have to delete the slideshow code tonight. Here is the sequence, in order:

1. Put the one message that matters on slide one. One headline, one photo, one button. This is the only slide the data says anyone reads — treat the rest of the carousel as if it does not exist, because for your visitors it barely does.

2. Turn off auto-rotation. If you keep multiple slides at all, make the visitor click to advance — and give them a visible pause control. The research is consistent that motion is what triggers the “that is an ad” dismissal; a static hero with clickable tabs is read like content.

3. Cut to three slides, then one. Five or fewer was the tested improvement; one is the endpoint. Watch what happens to your contact form or phone calls after each cut. Most owners find that nothing falls — the slide-one message was doing all the work all along.
