Why do the links on my website keep breaking?

You tap a link on your own website and land on an error page. Nobody told you it was broken. Here is why links die — the five usual causes — and what that error page is quietly telling your customers about your business.

Tap a link on your own website and you land on an error page. Nobody emailed you about it. No warning, no alert — it has probably been sitting there for weeks, greeting every visitor who finds it.

That error page has a name: a 404, the web’s standard “this address doesn’t exist” message. It means the link points at an address that no longer leads anywhere. The link didn’t break on its own — something changed underneath it. Here are the five usual causes, roughly in order of how much damage they do.

1. A redesign with no forwarding instructions

This is the big one. When a site gets redesigned, pages usually get new addresses — “/services.html” becomes “/plumbing-services”, that sort of thing. But the old addresses live on in a dozen places: your own menu and footer, Google’s search index, other websites that linked to you, old social-media posts, printed flyers with QR codes. Unless someone sets up redirects — forwarding instructions that send each old address to its new home — every one of those old links dies on launch day. Most small-business redesigns skip this step entirely, because nobody told the owner it was a step.

2. Pages you deleted on purpose

You removed the holiday menu, the discontinued product, the service you no longer offer. Sensible. But the footer link, the old blog post, and the Google result still point at it. Deleting a page without pointing its address somewhere useful — the closest current page, not an error page — turns a tidy-up into a dead end for anyone who follows the old trail.

3. Pages someone renamed

A well-meaning “cleanup” of a URL — fixing a typo in the address, shortening it, making it prettier — has exactly the same effect as deleting the page. The old spelling stops working everywhere it was ever written down. Renaming a page and deleting a page are the same event as far as your links are concerned.

4. The other site moved

Some of your links point outward: a supplier’s catalog, your review profile, a partner’s site, an article you cited. Those sites redesign, rename, and shut down without telling you — and their move breaks your link. This is the cause owners find most unfair, and it’s why link-checking is a recurring chore rather than a one-time fix: the web keeps shifting under links you didn’t touch.

5. Plain typos

Menu links typed by hand, one wrong letter, never clicked by the person who typed it. It happens more than anyone admits, usually in footers nobody re-reads.

What your customer actually thinks

Here’s the part that stings: a customer who taps your menu and gets an error page does not think “broken link.” They think the business looks unattended. One dead link is a shrug — every site has one. Three starts to look like nobody’s home. Nobody calls to tell you; they just tap back to the search results and pick the competitor whose site works.

The fix is a routine, not a project

The honest part nobody sells you: links will keep breaking. Redesigns happen, pages get retired, other sites move. The fix isn’t a one-time cleanup, it’s a habit — scan your site regularly, and for every dead link either fix the address or set up a redirect to the right page.
