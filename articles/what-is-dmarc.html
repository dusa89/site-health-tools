What is DMARC — and does your business have it?

Somewhere in a note from your IT person, or a security alert you didn’t fully read, you saw the word DMARC. Here is what it actually is, why it matters more than the two records you may already have, and the one setting that decides whether fake email from “your business” reaches your customers or dies quietly.

SPF and DKIM are the two checks inbox providers run on your outgoing mail. SPF asks: is this mail server allowed to send for this domain? DKIM asks: does the message carry the domain’s unbroken signature? Useful — but neither one answers the question that actually matters: what should the inbox provider do with a message that fails the checks? That instruction is DMARC. It is the third record, the one that turns the other two from observations into a decision.

The three settings, in plain English

Your DMARC record has a policy line, and it is one of three words. p=none means “watch and report only.” Impostor mail still gets delivered; you just get a daily report about it. Think of it as a security camera: it records the break-in but doesn’t lock the door. p=quarantine means mail that fails the checks goes to the spam folder. p=reject means it gets blocked outright — it never reaches anyone’s inbox.

Here is the uncomfortable part: most small businesses that have a DMARC record at all are sitting on p=none, and many have no DMARC record whatsoever. Both states mean the same thing for your customers — a fake invoice or phishing email sent “from” your business address lands in their inbox just like your real ones.

The daily report nobody reads

DMARC records can include a report address — rua, the technical name — where inbox providers send a daily digest of every server on earth that sent mail claiming to be your domain. Legitimate ones are yours: your email host, your newsletter tool. The ones you don’t recognize are the impostors. The policy setting does the blocking work for you, but the reports are how you find out who was trying. Our free email check reads your DMARC record and tells you which policy you are actually on, plus whether the report address is set.

One honest catch

DMARC does not work alone — it leans on SPF or DKIM to pass a message. If neither of those is set up, DMARC has nothing to align with, and moving straight to “reject” can block your own legitimate mail. The order matters: SPF and DKIM first, DMARC set to “none” to watch, then tighten the dial once the reports show only your real senders.
