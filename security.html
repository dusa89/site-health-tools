What does "website security" actually mean?

The padlock in the browser's address bar means one specific thing: the connection between your visitor and your site is encrypted, so nobody on the same wifi can read or alter what's passing through. That encryption is powered by a certificate — a digital ID card for your domain, issued by a trusted authority, with an expiry date. When it lapses, browsers show visitors a full-page "not secure" warning, and most of them leave. Certificates from free providers last only 90 days, which is why expiry sneaks up on busy owners.

Beyond the padlock, your site sends a set of behind-the-scenes instructions with every page — security headers. They tell browsers things like "always use the encrypted connection, even if someone tries to downgrade it," "never let another site embed this page invisibly," and "only load code from sources I approve." Mozilla's HTTP Observatory grades all of this on an A+ to F scale, the way a building inspector grades a property: it doesn't just check the front door, it checks the locks on every window.

Most small-business sites land in the C to F range — not because the owner did anything wrong, but because the platform or host ships weak defaults and nobody ever tightened them. The fixes are usually small configuration changes, not rebuilds.
