I Got an Email Saying a Hacker Recorded Me — Is It Real?

The email lands like a punch: a stranger claims they hacked your computer, recorded you through your webcam, and will send the video to your family, your customers, and everyone you work with — unless you pay them in Bitcoin within 48 hours. And to prove it, they quote one of your real passwords. This article is the short answer and the calm-down plan. It is not real. Nobody recorded anything. But the password part deserves five minutes of your attention, because that is where the real work is. If your worry is that someone is genuinely inside your accounts right now, the password-reset article covers the actual-takeover signs; this one is about the email that only pretends someone got in.

1. The short answer, up front

No. It is not real. Nothing was recorded, nobody hacked your webcam, and nobody has a video of you. This is the single most common blackmail email on the internet, and it has been running, nearly unchanged, for close to a decade. The US Federal Trade Commission says it plainly: "The emails say they hacked into your computer and recorded you visiting adult websites. They threaten to distribute the video to your friends and family within hours, unless you pay into their Bitcoin account. Stop. Don't pay anything. Delete the message. It's a scam." (FTC consumer alert, Apr 2020; still current guidance ~Oct 2026.) The UK's National Cyber Security Centre says the same thing from the other side of the ocean: the criminals behind these emails do not know if you have a webcam, and do not know if you visited any adult website (NCSC guidance, Crown Copyright 2025). They are guessing. And they send the same guess, by the millions, to everyone.

2. What the email looks like (the anatomy of the scare)

Every version follows the same script, because the script works on panic. Step one: the password. The email opens with one of your real passwords — an old one you used years ago, sometimes a current one you reused somewhere — quoted in the subject line or the first sentence. This is the whole trick. Your brain hears "they know my password" and fills in "they must have everything." Step two: the technical story. They claim they planted malware on an adult site, that your browser secretly became a remote desktop with a keylogger, that they harvested your contacts and turned on your camera. The jargon is there to sound like proof; it is not proof. Step three: the split-screen lie. They say they made a "double-screen video" — the adult video on one side, your webcam recording on the other — and sometimes offer to "prove it" by sending the video to ten of your friends first if you reply. Step four: the deadline. Pay a few hundred to a few thousand dollars in Bitcoin (published examples range from $708 to $2,900) within 24 to 48 hours, and they will "destroy the video immediately." The deadline is fake urgency: if you miss it, nothing happens, because there is no video to release.

The password is the only real thing in the email — and it does not mean what they want you to think. Scammers buy enormous lists of email addresses paired with passwords stolen in old data breaches — the LinkedIn hack, the Adobe hack, the MySpace hack, and hundreds of smaller ones. That data is bought and sold by spammers for years afterward (explainer, kimberley.org.za). The scammer's software simply inserts your leaked password into the template next to your address. If the password is one you recognize, it means you once used it on a service that got breached — it does not mean anyone broke into your computer today. Security researchers at Malwarebytes put it cleanly: these passwords generally come from old data breaches and are unrelated to any current compromise of the victim's devices (via ConsumerAffairs, Jun 2026). The FTC's advice when the password is real: "update your password on that account, and consider updating other passwords, too" — because the danger is password reuse, not the webcam.

3. The numbers that prove it's industrial

This scam does not depend on picking the right victims; it depends on emailing everyone. The UK NCSC notes the criminals send millions of these emails in the hope that someone, somewhere, panics and pays. That volume is the whole business model — and it works often enough to matter. Security firm Symantec tracked $1.2 million in Bitcoin earned through sextortion and bomb-threat scams in a single 12-month window (via Cointelegraph, report ~Jul 2026), with the typical ask in the hundreds of dollars. The FBI's Internet Crime Report for 2025 (released Apr 2026) shows the scale in complaints: extortion was the second-most-reported cybercrime in America with 89,129 complaints, and sextortion alone drew 64,283 reports with $44.23 million in losses. One more number worth sitting with: victims aged 60 and over filed only 6,121 of those sextortion reports (9.52%) — but lost $14.89 million, which is 33.67% of the sextortion total. The scam lands hardest on the people least likely to have heard it is a bluff. If someone on your team or in your family is in that age group, this article is worth forwarding to them.

4. The calm-down plan: five steps, in order

Step 1: Do not pay. Do not reply. Paying does not buy silence — there is nothing to be silent about — and the NCSC warns explicitly that paying marks you as a "willing" customer, which invites more scams. Replying, even to say "prove it," confirms your address is read by a real human, which makes it more valuable to spammers. The New Zealand Police's 2026 guidance on a fresh wave of these emails (demands of NZ$1,900, ~US$1,150) is the same one-sentence rule: delete them.

Step 2: Change that password — everywhere you used it. This is the one real action the email demands of you, and it has nothing to do with the scammer. Go to the breached account (or the account whose password matched), change it to something long and unique, and then change it on every other site where you used the same password, because credential-stuffing — robots trying your leaked email+password pair on banks, email, and shopping sites — is the actual threat that password represents. The two-factor article covers the layer that stops credential-stuffing cold.

Step 3: Check where the password came from. Type your email address into haveibeenpwned.com (the NCSC's recommended check). It will tell you which breaches exposed your address and when — usually an old, well-known one. This turns the fear into a fact: "oh, that was the 2016 LinkedIn dump," and the email's power evaporates. If you see recent breaches you did not know about, treat those accounts as step 2's priority list.

Step 4: Look for actual evidence of compromise — and expect to find none. A real webcam or device compromise leaves traces the email cannot fake: the login-attempt noise your site would show, the password-reset emails you did not trigger, the sent-mail folder you did not write. The login-attempts article explains what bot noise looks like versus a real break-in, and the already-hacked article is the forensic checklist. The blackmail email, on its own, is not evidence of any of it — it is evidence that your email address is on a spammer's list, like most of them.

Step 5: Report it, then delete it. In the US, forward the email details to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; larger losses can go to the FBI's IC3. Reporting feeds the complaint numbers that keep this scam in the FBI's annual report. Then delete the email. There is nothing to keep, nothing to investigate on your end, and nothing to warn your contacts about — nobody received anything.

5. The one case where you should NOT delete: they show you something real

This deserves its own section because the advice flips. Everything above is about the mass email that shows no actual image or video of you — just words. If someone sends you an actual intimate photo or video of you — something that is really you, not a claim — that is a different crime (real sextortion), and the rule changes: do not delete the messages; keep them as evidence, do not engage, and contact law enforcement. The FTC's "delete the message" advice is specifically for the mass-blast bluff; real evidence is kept, not deleted. If you are unsure which one you received, the test is simple: a bluff describes a video; a real attack shows you one.

6. The questions owners always ask next

"Should I buy security software because of this?" No — not for this. This email is not malware; there is nothing on your computer for antivirus to remove, and no firewall would have stopped an email (that's the firewall article's endpoint-vs-cloud frame applied to the wrong problem). Buy security software for real threats — the hacked-site article is where cleanup lives — not because a spammer guessed your address.

"What if my employees get these?" They will — everyone gets them eventually. Tell your team the password trick in advance: the day someone reads "they know my password" without knowing it's from an old breach is the day someone pays. A two-sentence heads-up in a team meeting is the whole defense.

"They said there's a tracking pixel that proves I opened the email." That part is actually true — tracking pixels exist — and it changes nothing. Opening an email does not give anyone access to your webcam, your files, or your contacts. It only tells a spammer the address is live. It is not evidence of a hack.

"Is this the same as those wire-transfer scam emails?" Related but different. The vendor-bank-details article covers BEC — scam emails that go after your money by impersonating people you trust. This one goes after your shame by impersonating a hacker. Both want you to act before you think; both lose their power the moment you stop and check.

"Whose job is this — mine, or my web host's?" Yours, in practice. Hosts protect the server; nobody protects your inbox, your passwords, or your team's judgment for you. The responsibility article draws the line so you know exactly which part is nobody's job but yours — and this email lands squarely in it.
