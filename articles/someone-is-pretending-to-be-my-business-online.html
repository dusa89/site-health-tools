Someone Is Pretending to Be My Business Online — What Do I Do?

The call goes like this: “Hi, I already paid — I used the link from your other website.” You don't have another website. Someone built one for you, with your name on it, and your customer just handed them money. Here's how to fight back.

Most website security problems happen TO your website — it gets hacked, it gets flagged, it goes down. This one is different: your website is fine. It's your reputation that got stolen. Somewhere out there, a copy of your business is taking orders, collecting payments, and lying to your customers in your name. And the first time you hear about it is usually from a customer who already paid the stranger.

This is not rare anymore. The FTC received more than one million imposter-scam reports in 2025, with consumers reporting nearly $3.5 billion in losses — and impersonation scams have topped the FTC's fraud-complaint list in recent years. On September 24, 2026, the FTC formally asked whether the platforms whose ad tools help these scams reach people should face new requirements, because sponsored search results and social ads are now a favorite impersonation channel. The scammers aren't amateurs anymore; they're running the same ad machinery legitimate businesses use.

The four fronts of a business impersonation

Impersonators rarely build one fake thing. They build a surround — enough pieces that a rushed customer never notices anything is off:

1. The lookalike website. A domain one typo away from yours — yourbusiness.com vs yourbuslness.com, or yourbusiness-services.com. Same logo, same service list, sometimes scraped from your real site. The payment form sends the money to them. This is the cousin of domain theft, except they didn't steal yours — they built a fake one next door, which is why locking down your own domain is only half the defense.

2. The fake social profiles. A Facebook page or Instagram account with your name, your photos, and a "message us to order" prompt. They run ads from it — and this is exactly what the FTC's September 2026 proceeding is about: sponsored placements impersonating real businesses.

3. The fake listing. A duplicate Google Business Profile or directory listing with their phone number on it. Your customer searches your name, calls the first number they see, and "your" receptionist quotes them a price and asks for a deposit.

4. The email/social outreach. Messages "from" your business announcing a sale, a giveaway, or an urgent payment link — the same shape as the fake urgency paperwork small businesses already get, now aimed at your customers instead of you.

Notice what none of these require: touching your website. That's why the hacked-website playbook doesn't fit — your site isn't the crime scene. Your customers are.

Why this one hits different

When your site gets hacked, you feel the pain and you fix it. When someone impersonates your business, your customers feel the pain and you get the blame. The customer who paid the fake site doesn't think "I got scammed by a stranger" — they think "that business took my money." They leave a one-star review on YOUR page. They tell their neighbors. The impersonator walks away with the money and leaves you with the reputation damage, which is why the FTC's 2024 Rule on Impersonation of Government and Businesses explicitly prohibits materially posing as a business — this isn't just rude, it's the conduct federal regulators wrote a rule about.

There's a second sting: you find out late. Businesses usually learn about an impersonation from a confused or angry customer, weeks after it started. Every day the fake runs is another day of your name attached to someone else's scam. Speed matters more here than in almost any other security problem — which is what the playbook below is built for.

The first-48-hours playbook

Hour 1: Warn your customers. This comes before everything, because every hour your customers don't know is an hour the fake keeps collecting. The FTC's own guidance to small businesses is blunt: warn your customers as soon as possible — by mail, email, or social media. Post it on every channel you actually control. If you email the warning, send it without hyperlinks — you don't want your warning to look like a phishing email. And remind customers: never share personal information through email or text, and if their data was stolen, they should get a recovery plan at IdentityTheft.gov. One plain message: "Someone is pretending to be us online. Here is our real website and our real phone number. Do not pay anyone else."

Hour 2: Put a banner on your real site. The FINRA takedown toolkit — written for firms facing this exact problem — recommends posting a warning banner on your own site while the impersonation is active. Your real site is the one place you can be sure confused customers will land. Make the banner impossible to miss: what the scam looks like, what your real channels are, and how to report it.

Hour 3: Report everywhere, in parallel. Takedowns only happen when the right abuse teams hear about it, and each one moves at its own speed, so file all of them: the domain registrar's abuse team for the lookalike domain; each platform's abuse/reporting flow for fake profiles and listings; the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov; and the imposter reported to Chrome and Bing as an unsafe site — the same flagging machinery that can mark a real site as dangerous works against the fake. If a customer lost money, also file with local police — insurers and banks want a police report number before they open a claim.

Day 2: If money moved, chase it. If your business paid a fraudulent invoice or a customer's payment was diverted, call your bank's fraud desk on a number from your statement — not any number from the scam email — and file the IC3 complaint the same day. For qualifying wire transfers, the FBI's Financial Fraud Kill Chain can help freeze fraudulent funds, but the window is narrow: hours and days, not weeks. Then notify your business insurer early — most crime and cyber policies have reporting deadlines.

Stopping the next one before it starts

You can't make impersonation impossible, but you can make your business a much harder target — and a much faster responder:

1. Watch the neighborhood. The FBI's advice to businesses includes using domain protection services that notify you when domains similar to yours get registered. You want to hear about yourbuslness.com from a monitoring alert, not from an angry customer. At minimum, register the obvious variants of your own domain (.com, .net, the common misspellings) before someone else does.

2. Lock down what you own. Registrar locks and WHOIS privacy keep your real domain from becoming part of the problem. Then claim your name everywhere that matters — your Google Business Profile, your Facebook and Instagram handles, your directory listings — before an impersonator does. A claimed, verified profile with years of history is far harder to displace than an empty one.

3. Make your real channels unmistakable. Put your real phone number, real email, and real domain on your site, your invoices, and your social profiles — in exactly one consistent form. The customer who knows "they only ever email me from @mybusiness.com and never ask for gift cards" is the customer the impersonator can't fool. Publish that policy plainly: how you'll never ask for payment, and what your real links look like.

4. Build the reflex before you need it. Save the reporting links — ReportFraud.ftc.gov, ic3.gov, your registrar's abuse page — somewhere you can find them panicking at 9pm. Write the customer-warning message template now, when you're calm: no hyperlinks, real contact info only. The businesses that recover fastest from impersonation aren't the ones with the best lawyers. They're the ones whose warning went out in an hour instead of a week.
