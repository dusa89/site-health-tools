Why does Google say my website is dangerous?

A customer texts you a screenshot: a big red screen where your website should be, saying it is “deceptive” or “dangerous.” You open your site on your own phone and everything looks fine. Here is what that red screen is, why it hits legitimate businesses, and how to get it off your site.

That red screen is Google Safe Browsing — a protection built into Chrome, Firefox, and Safari that warns visitors before they load a site Google believes is unsafe. The exact wording varies: “Deceptive site ahead,” “The site ahead contains malware,” “Dangerous site.” The message matters less than the effect: most visitors will not click through it. They close the tab and call whoever did not scare them. The warning follows your domain on every device, not just one computer — this is not a virus on your phone.

The most common cause: your site got hacked (and you can’t see it)

This is the one that surprises owners. Hackers routinely break into small-business websites — usually through an outdated content-management system or plugin — and inject invisible junk: spam links, redirects to scam pages, or fake download buttons that only appear to visitors, not to the owner. Everything looks perfectly normal to you. Google’s crawler sees the injected garbage, flags the site, and the red screen goes up. A hacked site is also the reason Google Ads sometimes stops running for a business overnight: Google’s crawler finds the hacked code and disables the ads until it is cleaned.

The other usual suspects

A page that looks like a login trap. If your site asks for passwords or card details on a page that resembles a bank’s, or a third-party plugin loads a pop-up that mimics a system warning, the classifier can read it as phishing. Young sites that collect emails on the homepage before they have real content can trip the same alarm.

Bad third-party content. Ad networks, embedded widgets, and “free” download buttons can quietly load malicious content from elsewhere. The flag lands on your domain, not theirs.

False alarms do happen. Reclassifications and user reports can mislabel a clean site, and cached browser warnings can linger for hours even after Google clears you — one scary screenshot is not proof the flag is still live. Which brings us to the check.

Check whether you are actually flagged — free, right now

Google runs a public Safe Browsing lookup: transparencyreport.google.com/safe-browsing/search — swap in your own domain. “No unsafe content found” means you are clear at this moment. If it names a problem, you have the real answer, not a screenshot.

If you own the domain, also set up Google Search Console and open its Security Issues report: it shows the exact pages Google flagged and the category it put them in, and it is the only door to request a review after you clean things up. (The padlock and the red screen are different checks — a site can have a valid certificate and still be flagged. We explain the padlock here: why your site says “not secure”.)

If you are flagged, here is the order of operations

First, clean the site: update your CMS and every plugin, remove the injected code or have your hosting company do it (most hosts offer a malware cleanup service — use theirs before you hire anyone). Then request a review in Search Console so Google re-checks you; do not skip the review or the warning stays up. And do not ignore the flag hoping it fades: while it is up, visitors bounce, ads stay off, and Google quietly stops trusting your pages.
