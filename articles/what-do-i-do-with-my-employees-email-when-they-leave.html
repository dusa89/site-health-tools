What Do I Do With My Employee’s Email When They Leave?

An employee leaves your business — quit, fired, moved on — and now their business email address is sitting there like an empty desk with the drawer full of your company’s mail. Customers are still emailing it. The old password still works. You are still paying for it. Here is the right order to handle it, before it costs you a customer or a security headache.

One clarification before we start: this is not about your business email landing in customers’ spam folders (that is our deliverability guide — SPF, DKIM, and DMARC in plain English) and it is not about strangers sending fake email in your company’s name (that is our spoofing guide). This guide is about a real mailbox belonging to a real person who no longer works for you — what to do with their email access, their old messages, and their address, in the right order. Get the order wrong and you either lock customers out of reaching you, or leave a door open that should be shut.

The short answer: on their last day, shut off their access first — then keep their address alive just long enough to catch the stragglers. Most small businesses do it backwards: they delete the account immediately (and destroy the forwarding that would have caught incoming quotes) or they do nothing at all (and pay for a mailbox a former employee can still read for months).

The four things that go wrong

1. They can still get in. Until you change the password and block their sign-in, a former employee can read and send email as your business from their phone — including from home, months later. It does not matter whether they were trustworthy. This is the one step that cannot wait.

2. Customers keep emailing the old address. Vendors, repeat customers, and leads who saved that person’s address will keep writing to it. Without a plan, those quotes, invoices, and follow-ups land in an inbox nobody opens. Lost mail is lost money, and nobody tells you it happened.

3. You keep paying for a ghost. Business email is billed per user. Every month an ex-employee’s account sits there, you are paying full price for a mailbox you only need as an archive. There is a cheaper way to keep the old mail — see the checklist below.

4. The old mail disappears — or can’t be found. Old quotes, customer agreements, and project threads live in that mailbox. Delete the account carelessly and that history is gone with it. On Microsoft 365, you get about 30 days to change your mind — then it is permanent.

The checklist (in this order)

Step 1 — Same day: cut their access. Change the password and block their sign-in (or suspend the account) on their last day. On Google Workspace the official guidance is to suspend rather than delete — suspending locks them out while keeping all the data. Either way: nobody logs in as that person again, starting now.

Step 2 — Same day: set up forwarding and an auto-reply. Route new mail from the old address to you or whoever takes over their work, and turn on an automatic reply: “Thanks for your message — [name] no longer works here; please contact [new person] at [address].” This is the step most businesses skip, and it is the one that saves actual customers. Do this before you delete anything — Microsoft’s own documentation warns that forwarding needs the account to still exist to anchor it.

Step 3 — Within the week: preserve the old mail. Give the person taking over access to the old mailbox, or convert it to a shared mailbox — Microsoft 365 does this conversion when you hand a deleted user’s email to someone else, and the new owner can keep an eye on it. On Google Workspace, transfer the mail (and their Drive files) to a manager before any deletion, or archive the account — archiving keeps the data without the monthly license fee.

Step 4 — Only now: delete the account and free the license. Deleting the user frees the license for your next hire. On Microsoft 365 you then have roughly 30 days to restore the account if you need something back — after that, without a retention hold, the data is permanently removed. The 30-day window is a safety net, not a plan; do steps 1–3 first so you never need it.

Step 5 — Tell your email provider or IT person what you did. If someone else manages your email, tell them the forwarding and auto-reply are in place so they do not “clean up” the old account in a way that breaks them. And if a new hire is coming, give them their own new address — never hand them the old employee’s password. One address, one person, always.

What not to do

Don’t delete the account on day one. It feels decisive, but deleting first destroys the very things you set up to catch incoming mail — and on Microsoft 365 the forwarding dies with the account it was anchored to. Access off first, forwarding second, deletion last.

Don’t let them keep the password “just in case.” “In case we need to ask them something” is how a former employee keeps read access to your customer list, your pricing, and your internal conversations for months. If you need to ask them something, email their personal address.

Don’t do nothing. The abandoned mailbox is the most common outcome: still licensed, still logged in on an old phone, still receiving customer mail nobody reads. It is the most expensive and least secure option — you are paying for a mailbox that is working against you.

Don’t skip the auto-reply. Forwarding alone sends mail to the right person, but the sender never learns the person they wrote to is gone. A month later they call asking why “[employee]” never replied. The auto-reply closes that loop in one line.

The bottom line

An employee’s departure takes five steps and about twenty minutes: lock the account, forward the mail, auto-reply the senders, preserve the history, then delete and stop paying. Do it in that order and a resignation costs you nothing more than the resignation. Do it out of order — or not at all — and the real cost shows up quietly: a quote that sat in a dead inbox, a former employee still reading your mail, and a license fee you kept paying for months.
