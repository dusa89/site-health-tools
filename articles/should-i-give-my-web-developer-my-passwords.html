Should I give my web developer my passwords?

It's the normal way to do it — and it's how your developer's laptop quietly becomes a back door to your business. The numbers on credential theft, and the four-step right way.

It usually arrives as a casual text: "Just send me your logins and I'll take care of it." Your web developer needs to get in — the hosting account, the WordPress admin, the domain registrar, maybe your email — and asking for the passwords feels like the normal way to do it.

It is the normal way. It is also the way that quietly turns your developer's laptop into a back door to your business. The honest answer: never hand over the master keys. Give them their own door instead.

Problem 1: once they log in as you, nobody can prove anything

When two people share one login, the site's records show one person: you. If a page disappears, a price changes, or something breaks at 2 a.m., you cannot tell whether it was your developer, you, or someone who stole the password — because the system only ever saw you. That is exactly the "who broke it" argument that turns into a billing dispute: no audit trail, no answer, just two people remembering differently.

Problem 2: passwords are the single most reused thing on the internet

This is where the numbers stop being abstract. Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report — 12,195 confirmed breaches analyzed — found stolen credentials were the initial access vector in 22% of breaches, and 88% of basic web application attacks involved stolen credentials. In 2024 alone, roughly 2.8 billion passwords were posted for sale or free on dark-web markets and criminal boards. And researchers who analyzed 19 billion leaked passwords from April 2024 to April 2025 found 94% were reused or duplicated across accounts — only 6% unique (Cybernews, via SQ Magazine 2026).

The caveat, stated plainly: these are enterprise-scale datasets, not small-business websites. But the mechanism is identical on your site: the average person reuses passwords across half their accounts (Verizon's infostealer research: the median user keeps only 49% of their passwords distinct). So the password you text your developer is probably also — or nearly — the password to something else you own. And now it lives in their texts, their email, and their notes app, on a laptop that is not yours.

Problem 3: your developer counts as a "third party" — and third parties are where breaches are growing

The same Verizon report found third-party involvement in breaches doubled in a single year, from 15% to 30%. Read that in small-business terms: if your developer's laptop gets infected — an email attachment, a dodgy download — and your hosting password is saved in their browser, your website is compromised by an attack that never touched you directly. This is not about distrusting your developer. It is about the fact that their security is now your security, and you get no vote on it.

What to do instead

1. Give them their own user account, never your login. WordPress lets you add them as a separate user (Users → Add New) with an Administrator role — takes sixty seconds. Most hosting companies have sub-users, collaborators, or team members. They get their own login, their own password, and when the job is done you remove the account in one click.

2. When the system has no sub-users, share once and expire it. Some domain registrars really do only have one login. In that case: put the password in a password manager's shared item (not a text message), and change it the day the work is finished. Shared passwords have a shelf life; the mistake is letting them become permanent.

3. Turn on two-factor for every admin account. This is the single highest-leverage thing on this page. Two-factor means the stolen password alone is not enough — the attacker needs the second code too. It is why those constant login attempts bounce off instead of landing. Most hosts and WordPress offer it free; the five minutes it takes is the best security investment your site will ever get.

4. Run the exit checklist when they are done. Remove their user account. Change any password they were given. Revoke any API keys or app passwords created for them. It is not personal — it is the same reason a business collects the keys when a contractor finishes. A credential you forget about is a door you left open on purpose.

The rule that covers every case: ownership stays with you, access is lent and revoked. The domain registrar login stays yours (losing control of the domain is how businesses lose their name), the hosting master account stays yours, and everyone else gets a named account with an expiry date. If something goes wrong, the records show exactly whose door was used — which is also how a hack gets investigated and cleaned up instead of argued about.
