Other Websites Link To Pages I Deleted — Am I Losing Customers?

A local directory lists your business. A partner links to your services page from their website. A customer shared your booking page on Facebook last year. Then you renamed the page — or deleted it in a cleanup — and every one of those links now leads to “page not found.” You never see it happen. Your site looks perfectly fine to you. But some of the warmest visitors you will ever get — people who were already convinced enough to click — are landing on a dead page and leaving.

If you have read our guide on why links keep breaking, this is the mirror image of that problem. That guide is about the links on your pages pointing at dead addresses. This one is about dead addresses on your site that other people’s pages point at — and you cannot fix it by editing your own pages, because the broken link is not on your site at all.

The short answer: yes, you are losing those visitors — and you can get most of them back without resurrecting anything. Find which deleted addresses still receive visitors (your site’s “page not found” log shows you), then give each dead address a forwarding address to the closest live page. Ten minutes of setup, and every old link pointing at you starts working again.

The cruel part: these are your warmest visitors

Think about who clicks a link on someone else’s website. They were reading a directory listing, a partner’s recommendation, a review, a local blog post — and something convinced them to click through to you. That is not a casual browser. That is a person with intent, arriving pre-sold by whoever linked you. And the page they land on says the site has nothing for them.

Most of them will not tell you. They will not email to say “hey, your link is broken.” They will click back and call whoever was listed next. You will never know how many it was, because a dead page does not show up in your normal browsing — your own site looks fine, your menu works, your homepage loads. The damage is invisible from the inside, which is exactly why it goes unfixed for years.

How other people’s links to you go dead

You renamed a page. /plumbing-services became /services/plumbing in a redesign or a tidy-up. Every old link still points at the old address. This is the single most common cause — renames feel harmless because the page still exists, just somewhere else.

You deleted a page on purpose. A discontinued service, an old special offer, last year’s event page. Sensible cleanup — but the links pointing at it did not get the memo.

Nobody maintains old links. The directory that listed you three years ago is not checking whether your pages still exist. The partner who linked you in 2023 is not auditing their site. Old social media posts and old emails live forever, links and all. Once a link to you exists out in the world, assume it exists permanently — the only part you control is what happens when someone clicks it.

How to find which dead addresses still get visitors

You do not need to guess, and you do not need to check every directory on the internet. Your website already keeps the list — it is the log of “page not found” hits. Every time a visitor lands on an address that does not exist, most platforms record it: the dead address, and how many times it was hit. Sort that list by hits and the top entries are your answer — those are real visitors, arriving from real links, hitting dead pages.

Where to look depends on your platform: WordPress sites usually get this from a free redirect plugin, which keeps the not-found log as its main feature; Shopify, Squarespace, and Wix each have their own analytics or redirect sections that surface the same thing. If you use Google Search Console (free, one-time setup), its Links report shows which outside sites link to you — cross-check the most-linked addresses against your site, and any that 404 are the same problem from the other direction.

The fix: give every dead address a forwarding address

A redirect is mail forwarding for web addresses. You moved; the post office forwards your old address to the new one. Nobody has to update their address book, and no letter gets lost. Set a redirect from the dead address to the closest live page, and every old link — directories, partners, ancient Facebook posts — starts delivering visitors to a working page again.

The key word is closest. The old plumbing-services page should forward to the new plumbing page, not to your homepage. A deleted product should forward to the product category or the nearest equivalent product. A visitor who clicked “emergency plumbing rates” and lands on a generic homepage has to start their search over — most will not bother. Forward to the specific thing they came for, or as close as you can get. (There is a search-engine side to dead addresses too — our guide on broken links and rankings covers it — but the lost visitors are the bigger cost.)

Where the redirect button lives: every major platform has one. WordPress has free redirect plugins (which double as the not-found log mentioned above). Shopify, Squarespace, and Wix each have a redirects or URL-mapping feature in their settings. Search your platform’s help pages for “redirect” — it is a standard feature, not an add-on you have to buy. Each redirect takes under a minute once you know where the button is; the not-found log tells you which addresses are worth the minute.

What not to do

Don’t resurrect the page. If you deleted it for a reason — discontinued service, outdated offer — bringing it back just to satisfy one old link trades a dead page for a misleading one. Forward the address instead.

Don’t forward everything to the homepage. It feels like the safe default and it is the worst one. The homepage answers none of the visitor’s specific questions, and the click that brought them had a specific question. One extra step is all it takes to lose them.

Don’t ignore it because it feels small. “It’s just a few old links” is what everyone says, right up until the not-found log shows which addresses are actually being hit. You cannot judge the size of this from the inside — the log can. Check it before deciding it does not matter.

The bottom line

Links to you, once published, are permanent — but your pages are not. The gap between those two facts is a steady leak of your warmest visitors onto dead pages you never see. The not-found log shows you exactly where the leak is, and a redirect — a forwarding address, set once — seals each one for good. Do the ten-minute version this week: check the log, forward the top dead addresses to their closest live pages, and every old link pointing at you starts working again.
