Should My Business Use a VPN?

A VPN is sold as a magic shield: install it and you are safe and private online. The truth is narrower and more useful. A VPN does one job well - it builds a private tunnel for your internet traffic on networks you do not trust - and it does nothing at all about phishing emails, viruses, or the websites that track you because you logged in. Here is what it is, what it is not, who in a small business actually needs one, and how to avoid the free VPNs that make your privacy worse.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-12 - not promises about your situation. VPN companies differ wildly, and the market changes fast. The mechanics below are the same either way.

What a VPN actually does

Think of it as an armored tunnel. When you turn on a VPN, your device builds an encrypted tunnel to the VPN company's server, and everything you do on the internet travels through that tunnel. The coffee shop whose wifi you are sitting on, the hotel network, your internet provider at home - none of them can see which sites you visit or read the contents of your traffic. The websites you visit see the VPN company's address instead of yours, so your real location is masked from them. The FTC's consumer blog has pointed to VPN apps for this job since a February 2018 post: protecting your traffic in transit over public networks like coffee shops and airports. That is the real job. It is a good job. It is also the only job.

What it does not do

This is where the marketing gets dishonest. A VPN does not protect you from viruses or malware - it is a tunnel, not an antivirus. It does not stop you from typing your password into a fake bank login page; phishing does not care about your tunnel. (Our password-reset emails article covers the lures a VPN never touches.) It does not stop websites from tracking you when you are logged in - cookies, browser fingerprinting, and tracking pixels all keep working, because those live in your browser, not your network connection. If you are logged into Google, Google knows it is you no matter which address your traffic comes from. It does not make you anonymous; the FTC itself warns that VPN apps "generally don't make users entirely anonymous." And your traffic does not stay in the tunnel all the way: it pops out at the VPN company's server and travels the regular internet to its destination. Most sites today already encrypt the trip anyway (the padlock in your browser), which is why the FTC notes public wifi is safer than it used to be - a VPN adds a layer on top, not a new foundation. (Our two-factor article and browser-passwords article cover the defenses a VPN never replaces.)

The trust shift nobody mentions

Here is the FTC's most important line, paraphrased: a VPN shifts your trust from your internet provider to the VPN company. Every byte of your traffic now flows through their servers instead. If that company is careful, audited, and honest, that is a trade up. If it is shady, you just handed your entire browsing life to someone worse than your internet provider - and paid nothing for the privilege, which should tell you something. A study of 300 VPN apps that the FTC cited found many did not even use encryption, requested sensitive permissions they had no business asking for (like reading your text messages), and sold user information to third parties for advertising and analytics. You do not pick a VPN the way you pick a utility. You pick it the way you pick a business partner who sees everything.

Why "free" is the most expensive option

Running a VPN costs real money: servers around the world, bandwidth, engineers, customer support, lawyers. A company that charges you nothing has to get that money somewhere. Sometimes it is advertising. Sometimes it is a paid tier that subsidizes the free one - that arrangement is fine, and it is how the reputable free plans work. Sometimes, though, the money comes from collecting and selling your data, which is exactly the thing you installed the VPN to prevent. Security researchers at Zimperium found serious flaws in hundreds of free VPN apps and called them the weakest link in a business's security posture. A June 2026 investigation went further: of the 390 VPN apps most downloaded in the US, 64 trace back to Chinese companies - 31 of them hiding their ownership behind offshore corporate structures - and dozens were quietly collecting GPS location data on top of the usual advertising trackers. Those apps were downloaded 13.2 million times in a single month. As a CNET senior writer put it: with a strictly free VPN, you are the product. A free plan from a known paid company is a limited version of a real product; a fully free app with no visible way it makes money is the one to distrust.

Who in your business actually needs one

Not everyone, and be suspicious of anyone selling you one license per employee as a blanket rule. The people who genuinely benefit: anyone who works from coffee shops, airports, hotels, or client offices on wifi they do not control; remote workers connecting back to office systems; the owner checking the website admin panel from the road. (Our coffee-shop-wifi article walks through the public-wifi scenario in detail.) The person sitting at the office desk on the company network? The tunnel adds almost nothing - the network is already trusted, and the sites they visit are already encrypted. A VPN is a tool for untrusted networks, not a general-purpose shield. It also protects nothing on a stolen phone: if someone takes your unlocked device, the tunnel is irrelevant. (That is the stolen-phone article's territory - and a VPN never prevented an email takeover either.)

If you get one, pick it like a partner

Buy, do not borrow: a paid plan from a company you can name, with a clear privacy policy that says what it collects, how long it keeps it, and whether it shares anything with advertisers or third parties. Look for independent security audits - not just a marketing claim of "no logs," but a claim someone outside the company has tested. (Consumer Reports flagged both directions of this: one famous VPN handed user logs to Homeland Security in 2018 despite a no-logs promise, while another told a court it had no traffic data to hand over. Treat every no-logs claim as a promise you cannot verify from the outside.) Check the app's permissions before installing: a VPN needs network access, not your contacts, your texts, or your location. And read the marketing with cold eyes - "100% anonymous," "zero trace," "military-grade invisibility" are the tells of a company selling a feeling, not a service. The honest ones say what the tunnel does and what it does not.

The bottom line

A VPN is worth it for the people in your business who work on networks they do not control - the road warriors, the remote workers, the owner at the coffee shop. It is not worth it as a blanket purchase for every desk, and it is never a substitute for the defenses that actually stop the attacks small businesses face: phishing awareness, updates, strong unique passwords, and two-factor authentication. Buy it from a company with a real business model and an audited privacy policy, and skip the free ones entirely unless the free plan is the limited version of a paid product you trust. The tunnel is real. Just make sure you know who owns the other end.
