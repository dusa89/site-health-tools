Do I Need Email Authentication on a Domain That Never Sends Email?

You own more domains than you email from. The parked one you bought "just in case" - or the old brand name sitting after your rebrand - might be the one scammers are using right now, precisely because you never look at it. The good news: this is the one domain where the strongest protection is also the safest to turn on. Three records, ten minutes, free.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-13 - not promises about your situation. The three DNS records shown are the published standard pattern for non-sending domains; the "what this costs you" framing is our analysis.

The short answer

Yes - and you are lucky. On your real domain, email authentication has to be rolled out gently: monitor first, tighten later, or you can accidentally block your own invoices. On a domain that never sends mail, there is nothing legitimate to break, so you can go straight to the strictest setting on day one. Three DNS records say, in so many words: this domain accepts no email, no server may send as it, and anything claiming otherwise should be rejected. Every one of your idle domains gets all three.

Why scammers love your quiet domains

Think about it from the attacker's side. Your main domain is guarded: it has authentication records, your provider watches it, and a fake email from it has to get past real defenses. Your parked domain has none of that. No protection records means receiving mail servers have no technical reason to reject a fake that claims to be from it - the fake sails straight into someone's inbox with your old brand name in the From line. Industry guides on parked-domain protection say attackers actively scan for these unprotected domains. And you will never notice on your own, because nobody complains about a fake email that was never supposed to reach you. This is the flip side of the spoofing problem we covered separately: that guide was about fakes of your active domain; this one is about locking the doors you forgot you had.

The three records that lock it down

In your DNS settings (wherever you bought the domain - GoDaddy, Cloudflare, Namecheap, all have a DNS section), add these three records. Replace example.com with your idle domain:

1. MX record — priority 0, value . (just a period, nothing else)

2. TXT record at the domain itself — v=spf1 -all

3. TXT record at _dmarc — v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:reports@your-real-domain.com

What each record actually does

The first record is called a "null MX" record, and it is the simplest of the three: it tells the entire internet that this domain accepts no email at all. It is defined in RFC 7505, an internet standard published in June 2015, and it works because a lone period is not a valid server name - so no receiving system can mistake it for a real mail server. This also stops bounce messages piling up when spammers forge your idle domain as a return address: instead of queuing and retrying for days, the sending server learns immediately that delivery is impossible.

The second record is an SPF record with a minus-all at the end. In plain terms it says: no server anywhere on earth is authorized to send email for this domain. Not your mail provider, not your newsletter tool, nobody. Any email claiming to come from this domain is therefore, by your own declaration, a fake.

The third is a DMARC policy set to reject. DMARC is the record that tells receiving servers what to do with fakes: deliver them, quarantine them, or reject them outright. On your active domain you start with "monitoring only" and tighten slowly. Here you go straight to reject: block everything. The reporting address at the end is a bonus - even with reject in place, you still get aggregate reports showing who tried to spoof your domain, so you can see the attacks you are now stopping.

Why the strict setting is safe here - and risky on your real domain

This deserves emphasis because the advice flips depending on the domain. On the domain you actually email from, jumping straight to "reject everything" is genuinely dangerous: one forgotten invoice tool or a misconfigured record and your own legitimate email gets blocked silently. That is why every guide tells you to start with monitoring, read the reports for weeks, then tighten. On a domain that sends nothing, that whole careful dance is unnecessary - the parking-lot is empty, so you can put up the concrete barriers immediately. Multiple provider guides confirm this: parked domains can go straight to p=reject on day one, no gradual rollout needed, because no legitimate email should ever originate from them.

One hard warning, and it matters: never publish the null MX record on a domain you actually send from. The RFC itself warns about this - if a domain with null MX sends email, receiving systems may reject your real messages because the record declares the domain takes no mail. These three records are for idle domains only. Your sending domain keeps its real mail server records and its own, carefully-rolled-out authentication.

The one thing people get wrong: where the reports go

The reporting address in record 3 has to be a mailbox you actually read - on a domain that actually receives email. Reports from Gmail, Yahoo, Microsoft, and every other receiver flow to that address daily. If you point it at an inbox on the idle domain itself, the reports have nowhere to land, because the null MX record you just published tells the world that domain takes no mail. Point it at your real business address, or a dedicated reporting mailbox on your main domain. And if the reporting address sits on a different domain than the policy, DMARC rules require an extra authorization record on that other domain before the reports will be delivered - your provider's docs will show the exact line.

Which of your domains this is for

Walk your domain list and apply the three records to every domain in these buckets: the parked "just in case" domain you bought and never used; the old brand name you kept after a rebrand; the .net or .org variant you bought so nobody else could; campaign microsites from promotions that ended years ago; and any domain that is just a redirect to your main site. The rule is simple: if it never sends email, it gets the three records. Ten minutes per domain, once, and it never needs touching again.

Sources for the facts above, all checked 2026-10-13: the null MX record format and meaning - a single MX with preference 0 and exchange "." - plus the warning not to publish it for domains you send from (RFC 7505, published June 2015, via datatracker.ietf.org); the three-record pattern for non-sending domains (null MX, SPF v=spf1 -all, DMARC p=reject with a reporting address) as the published standard (pearpages.com email-authentication guide, 2026-10-07; DNSimple's domain-spoofing-protection docs); that parked domains can go straight to p=reject on day one with no gradual rollout, and that aggregate reports still show spoofing attempts under reject (dmarcreport.com parked-DMARC guide; duocircle.com non-sending-domains guide).
