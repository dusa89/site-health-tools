Should I Buy an Email List for My Business?

The pitch arrives by email, naturally: a broker offers you 50,000 "targeted, verified" email addresses in your market for a couple hundred dollars. Instant audience. No slow months of list-building. Just upload, send, and watch the orders roll in. Here is the honest answer in one paragraph: buying the list is probably the least of your legal worries — but uploading it is where the trouble starts. The law mostly doesn't care where your addresses came from. Your email platform cares enormously. And the spam filters care most of all. Walk through all three gates before you pay anyone.

Start with what the broker's email leaves out. There are three separate gatekeepers standing between a purchased list and a customer who buys from you — and you have to clear all three, not just one. They are: the law, the platforms, and the spam filters. The law is the easiest gate to walk through. The other two are where bought lists go to die.

Gate 1: The law — CAN-SPAM probably doesn't forbid it

This is the part that surprises people, so get it straight: in the United States, buying an email list is not itself illegal. The federal CAN-SPAM Act regulates what you send, not where you got the addresses. Under the FTC's rule, every commercial email has to carry accurate header and subject lines, identify itself as an advertisement or solicitation, include a valid physical postal address of the sender, and offer recipients a clear way to opt out — with opt-out requests honored within 10 business days. The FTC reviewed the whole rule in 2019 and voted to keep it with no changes, so this is the current law as it stands today. Get any of those pieces wrong and the penalties run to roughly $53,000 per individual email, which is why the compliance details in the guide on why your newsletter needs a physical address apply to a bought list exactly as they do to one you built yourself. The law doesn't care where you got the list; it only cares what you do with it.

Two sharp edges worth knowing. First, harvested addresses — scraped off websites by automated robots — are a different animal: the Act treats sending to harvested addresses as an aggravated violation with criminal exposure, so if a broker can't tell you how the addresses were collected, that is a red flag with teeth. Second, the United States is the lenient end of the map. If anyone on that list is in the EU, GDPR requires explicit opt-in consent, which a purchased list almost never has. Canada's CASL is strict consent-based too. A list that is merely risky in Texas can be a genuine liability in Toronto or Berlin. Know where your "targeted" addresses actually live before you mail them.

Gate 2: The platforms — most email tools will throw you out

Here is the gate that kills the plan for most businesses, because it has nothing to do with the law and everything to do with contracts you signed without reading. The major email marketing platforms — Mailchimp, Constant Contact, Campaign Monitor, and most of the others — explicitly prohibit sending to purchased or rented lists. Constant Contact's own permission guide states it plainly: it "does not permit the use of bought or rented email lists," and most purchased lists "will get your account flagged by our Account Review team, and you'll be asked to remove the list from your account immediately." The penalties in the contract are suspension or termination of the account — the full picture is in the guide on why email marketing accounts get suspended, and bought lists are one of the most common triggers.

Why are the platforms so hostile to a list you paid real money for? Because a purchased list is a reputation bomb sitting on their sending infrastructure. Their deliverability — and every other customer's inbox placement — depends on keeping complaint rates down. A cold list generates bounces, unsubscribes, and spam complaints at rates no organic list ever would. The platforms set hard tripwires for this: industry coverage of Brevo's limits, for example, puts them at roughly 2% hard bounces, 1% unsubscribes, and 0.2% complaints before the account gets flagged. A decent purchased list blows through all three on the first send. So the broker sells you the list, you upload it to the tool you already pay for, the tool suspends you — and the broker keeps your money. Read that contract term again before you spend anything.

Gate 3: The spam filters — the gate you can't negotiate with

Say you thread the first two gates: you comply with CAN-SPAM to the letter and you find a sender that tolerates cold lists. The third gate is automated, unsympathetic, and much harder than the first two. Purchased lists are old, resold, and contaminated. They carry spam traps — addresses that exist only to catch bulk senders — and a share of dead, mistyped, and role addresses that bounce on contact. And the humans on the list didn't ask for your email, so they complain. A lot. Complaints train the filters at Gmail, Outlook, and the corporate gateways, and once your sending domain earns a bad reputation, it doesn't just affect the bought list — it poisons your regular business mail too. Your quotes, your invoices, your "where are you" replies to real customers: all of it starts landing in spam because you mailed 50,000 strangers once. That reputation death spiral is documented in the guide on why business emails get blacklisted, and it is the single most expensive thing a bought list can do to you — far costlier than the broker's fee.

The honest math completes the picture. Industry estimates for bought-list campaigns put engagement at a fraction of what an owned list produces — single-digit engagement against the 30%-plus an organic list earns — with deliverability damage in the 40-60% range (a 2026 industry risk roundup, cited as an estimate). So the realistic outcome isn't "50,000 instant customers." It's a few dozen annoyed replies, a complaint rate that flags your account, and a sending reputation you then have to rebuild for months. The broker's "targeted, verified" language deserves heavy skepticism too: a list that gets resold to five businesses is "verified" in the sense that the addresses exist, not in the sense that anyone on it wants to hear from you.

What to do instead (and the one honest exception)

The unglamorous answer is the right one: build the list. Every customer you ever serve, every quote you ever send, every person who walks into your shop is a permission you can earn once and keep forever. A signup link on your website, a QR code at the counter, a checkbox on the contact form — the machinery is cheap and the list it produces is the one asset no platform can suspend and no filter will punish, because every person on it asked to be there. The guide on whether to send your newsletter from your business email covers the sending side of doing this properly.

The one honest exception: cold sales outreach is a different discipline from email marketing. Buying B2B contact data for one-to-one prospecting emails sent through a sales tool — short, personal, relevant, with real opt-outs — is how a lot of businesses legitimately fill a pipeline. That is not what this article is about, and it is not what the broker is selling you. The broker is selling a blast list. Uploading a blast list to a marketing platform is the thing that gets accounts suspended and reputations killed. Don't confuse the two.

So: should you buy an email list? The law says you probably can. Your email platform says you can't — at least not through them. And the spam filters say you'll regret it. Three gates, and the bought list only clears the easiest one. Build the list. It's slower, and it actually works.
