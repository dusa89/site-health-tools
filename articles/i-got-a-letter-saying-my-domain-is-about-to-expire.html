I Got a Letter Saying My Domain Is About to Expire — Is It Real?

It arrives in a real envelope, on letterhead, with your actual domain name and the word EXPIRATION in bold: your website's domain is about to expire, pay now or lose your online identity. It is probably not from your registrar, and paying it is probably how you LOSE the domain, not how you keep it. This is the oldest mail scam on the internet - and it is still running today.

First, where this guide sits among its siblings: this is not about someone stealing your domain through your registrar account (that trick is here), it is not about what happens when a domain genuinely lapses (that one is here), and it is not about your SSL certificate's renewal cycle (different thing, different sender). This is about a letter in your mailbox - or an email in your inbox - that LOOKS like a bill from your domain company and is actually a stranger's sales pitch. Your website is fine. Your domain is fine. The letter is the threat.

The short answer: if the letter came from any company other than the registrar that actually manages your domain, it is not a renewal bill - it is a solicitation. Paying it does not renew your domain. It transfers your domain to them, usually at a higher price, which is exactly the opposite of what you wanted.

What the letter actually is

The practice has a name: domain slamming. A company gets your domain name and its expiration date from public registration records - the same public records explained in the WHOIS guide - and mails you an official-looking "expiration notice" or "domain name renewal service" offer. The letter urges you to act fast: warnings about a "Register Lock," the "loss of your online identity," your website going dark. Pay the enclosed amount and your domain is "renewed."

What you actually signed is a transfer authorization. The fine print - and it is always in the fine print - says the payment moves your domain registration to THEIR company. You did not renew. You moved. And you moved to one of the most expensive registrars in the industry, at a price nobody would have agreed to if the letter had said "please switch to us" in big letters instead of "EXPIRATION NOTICE."

This is not a new trick. The company most famous for it, Domain Registry of America, was charged by the Federal Trade Commission in 2003 for mailing millions of these letters and misleading owners into transferring their domains under the guise of renewal. A federal court barred the conduct, and roughly 50,000 customers who cancelled their transfers were owed full refunds. The same year, Network Solutions (then VeriSign) settled FTC charges for notices that claimed domains were "about to expire" while hiding the real expiration dates - months or years away - and hiding that "renewing" meant transferring the domain to Network Solutions. A Canadian operation pulled a cruder version in 2008: fake invoices claiming owners would lose their domains unless they paid, some bundled with promised search-engine services that never materialized. The court ordered $4.26 million in judgments.

And it never stopped. As of this year, "Domain Name Services" letters are still arriving in business mailboxes: your real domain name, your real expiration date, a payment deadline, a detachable payment form, $265 for five years - with the fine print quietly admitting you are under no obligation to pay and that the offer involves switching registrars. The confession is always in the small print.

The six red flags

Run any "expiration notice" through this checklist before you believe a word of it.

1. The company name is not your registrar's name. Your domain is managed by ONE specific registrar - the company you (or your web person) originally bought it from. If the letterhead says anything else, it is a stranger, whatever the letter claims. Not sure who your registrar is? The expiry guide walks through checking the real record.

2. It arrived months early - or names no real date. The classic slamming letter arrives long before anything expires, because urgency is the engine. The FTC's 2003 case against Network Solutions turned on exactly this: "about to expire" with the actual date hidden - months or years away.

3. It threatens scary consequences. "Register Lock." "Loss of your online identity." "It may be impossible for you to get it back." A real renewal reminder from your registrar says "your domain renews on this date for this amount." Fear is the sales pitch.

4. The price is high. A normal domain renewal from your own registrar runs roughly $10-15 a year for a .com. The solicitation letters charge far more - $265 for five years is a common 2026 figure - because the price is the product, not the service.

5. The fine print confesses. Look for "this is not a bill," "this is a solicitation," "you are under no obligation to pay," or language about transferring/switching registrars. It is there because the law requires it after the FTC cases. It is small because the business model requires you not to read it.

6. It asks you to "renew" by signing a transfer. Any form, checkbox, or payment that authorizes a registrar transfer is the whole trick. A real renewal never asks you to move the domain.

The 5-step check before you pay anyone

Step 1 - Don't pay, don't sign, don't call the number on the letter. Calling them is how "no thanks" turns into a "yes" on the phone. The letter goes in a drawer until you have independent facts.

Step 2 - Look up the real record yourself. ICANN's public lookup (lookup.icann.org) shows your domain's actual registrar and actual expiration date, from the registry itself - not from the letter. This takes two minutes and settles the question completely.

Step 3 - Renew only through your actual registrar. Log in to YOUR registrar's own website - the one from step 2 - and renew there. Never renew through a link, QR code, or payment form that arrived in the mail from someone else.

Step 4 - Report the letter. The FTC takes these at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and the Better Business Bureau's Scam Tracker collects them too. Keep the original letter and the envelope - investigators connect cases from the details on them. Reporting is how the 2003 and 2008 cases happened: enough owners complained.

Step 5 - If you already paid: move fast. Call your REAL registrar today. You likely authorized a transfer, and transfers take days to complete - there is a window to stop or reverse one. Check that your domain's transfer lock is on afterward (transfer mechanics explained here).

What not to do

Don't assume "it has my domain name, so it must be legit." Your domain name and its expiration date are public information. Anyone can print them on a letter. Public data on a scary letterhead is still just public data (how much of it is public, and how to protect it, is here).

Don't confuse this with your SSL certificate renewal. Your certificate renews through your host or registrar on its own cycle, and its notices look nothing like this. A domain-renewal letter and a certificate-renewal notice are different things from different senders (the certificate cycle explained here).

Don't let the scam train you to ignore ALL renewal mail. This is the sneakier damage: after one fake scare, owners start throwing away every renewal notice - including the real ones from their actual registrar. That is how the genuine lapse happens (and a genuine lapse is worse than any letter). Verify every notice; ignore none.

Don't "renew early to be safe" through the letter. There is no version of this where paying the stranger helps. The safe early renewal is done at your own registrar, in your own account, on your own schedule.

The bottom line

An "expiration notice" from a company that is not your registrar is not a bill - it is a transfer authorization wearing a bill's costume, a trick old enough to have been sued twice by the FTC and still arriving in 2026 mailboxes. The defense is two minutes of independent checking: your real registrar, your real expiration date, your real account. Pay the right company, report the wrong one, and the letter goes in the recycling where it belongs.
