Do I Need DMARC If I Already Have SPF and DKIM?

Your web person set up SPF and DKIM and called email security done. That is two of the three checks - but two checks with no enforcement behind them. DMARC is the piece that turns authentication into protection, and skipping it leaves a gap many small business domains have.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-13 - not promises about your situation. Nobody can tell you exactly how inbox providers score your mail; anyone who promises a specific inbox rate is selling something.

The short answer

Yes. SPF and DKIM prove an email really came from your domain, but they tell receiving servers nothing about what to do when that proof fails. Without DMARC, a forged email that fails both checks can still be delivered - the receiving server shrugs and passes it through. DMARC is the instruction that closes the gap: it says "when an email pretending to be from us fails the checks, here is what to do with it." Think of SPF and DKIM as two locks on your front door, and DMARC as the guard who decides what happens when someone picks one.

What SPF and DKIM actually do - and the gap they leave

SPF is a list you publish saying which servers are allowed to send email for your domain. DKIM is a digital signature your server stamps on each outgoing email, which receivers verify against your published key. Both answer the same question: "is this email really from who it says it is?" Both leave the same hole: neither one says what happens when the answer is no. A scammer who sends a fake invoice from your address fails both checks - and Gmail receives the failure, has no instruction for what to do with it, and often delivers the email anyway.

What DMARC adds: alignment plus an instruction

DMARC does two things the other two cannot. First, it requires alignment: the domain in the From address you see must match the domain that passed SPF or DKIM. This is the gap attackers used for years - they would pass SPF with their own domain and fake your address in the From line, and everything looked fine. Second, DMARC carries your policy - the one-line instruction telling the receiving server what to do with mail that fails. That policy is the whole reason the record exists.

The three policies, in plain English

Your DMARC record has a setting called the policy, written as p= followed by one of three words, and it controls what happens to mail that fails:

p=none: watch, do not touch. The receiving server delivers the failing email normally and sends you a report about it. This is the starting position, and it is for learning - the reports show you every server sending as your domain, including the ones you forgot about.

p=quarantine: suspicious goes to spam. Mail that fails gets dropped into the spam or junk folder instead of the inbox. This is the middle step, and it is the first one where the policy actually changes what happens to mail.

p=reject: failures do not arrive. The receiving server refuses the mail outright - it is bounced back and never lands anywhere. This is the finish line: the strongest protection against someone sending mail as your domain.

The trap: p=none is not protection

This is the mistake half of small businesses make. Someone sets up DMARC at p=none, sees "DMARC: found" on a checker, and thinks the job is done. It is not - p=none tells the world "I am aware of DMARC" and explicitly instructs receivers to deliver forged mail anyway. Stages 1 and 2 behave identically for the person reading the email; only quarantine and reject change delivery behavior. That is why email checkers flag p=none as "DMARC policy not enabled" even though a record exists. If your record has been sitting at p=none for a year, you have monitoring, not protection - and the "safe path" below is how you move it to real enforcement without breaking your legitimate mail.

The safe path: none to quarantine to reject

You cannot jump straight to p=reject unless you already know every legitimate sender is passing, or you will start bouncing your own invoices, appointment reminders, and newsletters. The order most sources recommend: publish at p=none with reporting turned on, watch the reports for two to four weeks to find every system sending as your domain (your newsletter tool, your CRM, the billing platform nobody documented), fix each one's SPF or DKIM so it passes, then move to p=quarantine - optionally starting at 25 or 50 percent of failing mail first - and finally to p=reject once clean mail is consistently passing. Slow on purpose: one missed sender at p=reject is a bouncing payroll email.

The 2024 rule change that settled this for everyone

In October 2023 Google and Yahoo announced what had been a recommendation was becoming a requirement, and enforcement began in February 2024: anyone sending roughly 5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail or Yahoo addresses must authenticate with both SPF and DKIM and publish a DMARC record of at least p=none - or the mail gets delayed, spam-foldered, or rejected. Microsoft brought in the same requirements for Outlook.com in May 2025. Most small businesses never hit 5,000 a day, but inbox providers tend to now treats authenticated domains as more trustworthy by default - so the same records that satisfy the bulk rules also make your everyday quotes and invoices land better. There is no meaningful downside to ending at p=reject even if you send fifty emails a month.
