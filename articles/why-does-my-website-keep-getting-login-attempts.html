Why does my website keep getting login attempts?

Your security log shows thousands of failed logins from strangers around the world. Nobody is targeting you personally — bots sweep the entire internet. Here are the real 2026 numbers, the three attack types hitting your login box, and the 6-step lock that stops them.

Sooner or later, almost every WordPress site owner sees it: you open your security plugin’s log and there they are — hundreds or thousands of failed login attempts from IP addresses in countries you have never done business in, hammering your wp-admin page at 3 a.m. The first reaction is panic: someone is targeting me. The truth is both less personal and more serious. Nobody is targeting you. Bots are targeting everyone — and your little site is simply on the list.

What the attack actually is

A login attack is exactly what it sounds like: automated software trying username and password combinations against your website’s login form until one works. No human is sitting at a keyboard guessing your password. A bot network is running through lists — sometimes millions of combinations — across hundreds of thousands of sites at once. Your site is one row in a spreadsheet.

There are three flavors, and all three hit the same login box:

1. Classic brute force. The bot guesses common passwords against a known username — very often literally “admin,” which WordPress still ships as the default username on many installs. Short, common passwords fall in seconds.

2. Dictionary attacks. The bot works through long lists of real passwords pulled from past data breaches. If your password is a dictionary word with a number tacked on, it is already on someone’s list.

3. Credential stuffing. The quietest and most successful. The bot takes email-and-password pairs stolen from other websites’ breaches and replays them against your site, betting that you reused the same login. You can have a strong password and still lose — because the password is strong on your site but identical on a site that leaked it.

The real numbers

The scale is hard to overstate. Wordfence, which runs one of the largest WordPress security networks, reported blocking 18.2 billion brute-force login attacks in the second quarter of 2026 alone — up 13.8% from the quarter before — from 106.2 million unique attacking IP addresses, averaging 171 attempts per IP. The quarter before that was 16 billion. (These are Wordfence’s own network figures; they sell security software, so take the direction, not the decimal places — but the direction is a flood, and it is rising.)

The credential side is just as stark. Verizon’s 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found that compromised credentials were the initial way in for 22% of confirmed breaches — the single most common entry method tracked. And in their analysis of sign-in logs, a median of 19% of all authentication attempts were credential stuffing — roughly one login attempt in five is not a real user. For small businesses the share was 12%: lower, but on the same internet, facing the same bots.

The modern versions are also getting sneakier. One hosting provider documented distributed campaigns that stretched 2,160 hours — a full 90 days — at about one and a half attempts per IP address, so slow that no per-IP limit catches them. From any single address, it looks like someone mistyping a password once. The pattern only becomes visible when you can see across many sites at once — which is exactly what the attackers count on you not having.

Why your small site is on the list

Attackers do not pick victims by size. They sweep the entire internet, and a small business site with a weak login is cheaper to crack than a bank with a security team. You are not being singled out; you are being included. The economics are simple: test a million sites, a fraction fall over, and each one that does becomes a launchpad.

What happens after a break-in is where it gets expensive. The attacker’s goal is rarely your website itself. A cracked admin account becomes a home for redirect hacks (our guide on sites redirecting to spam covers one version), hosted phishing pages, or spam-sending infrastructure — and once Google flags it, your site gets the red warning page and your customers stop coming. The break-in takes minutes; the cleanup and the lost trust take months.

The 6-step lock

1. Delete the “admin” username. It is the first guess on every list. Create a new administrator account with an unusual username, log in as it, and delete the old “admin” account. This one change kills the cheapest attack class outright.

2. Use a long, unique password — stored in a password manager, not your head. Unique matters more than clever: a password that exists only on your site cannot be credential-stuffed from another site’s breach. If you have reused your site password anywhere else, assume the attackers already have it.

3. Turn on two-factor authentication for every admin account. This is the single highest-leverage step. With 2FA on, a stolen password alone is useless — the attacker needs your phone too. WordPress does not include 2FA by default, so add it with a security plugin; it stops the vast majority of credential-dependent attacks cold.

4. Add a limit on login attempts. A good security plugin (Wordfence’s free tier is the common choice) locks out an IP after a few failures. Per the distributed-attack problem above this will not stop the slow ones — but it stops the loud ones, which are the majority of attempts, and it costs nothing.

5. Disable XML-RPC if you do not use it. XML-RPC is a WordPress feature used by the Jetpack plugin and the WordPress mobile app. Attackers abuse it to bundle hundreds of password attempts into a single request, slipping past login limits. If your site does not use Jetpack or the mobile app, disabling XML-RPC removes a whole attack surface. A security plugin can do this with one toggle.

6. Keep everything updated. Attackers pivot to login attacks partly because the other doors keep getting locked. Our guide on why website updates matter covers the plugin-and-theme side — but note the asymmetry: updates close software holes; only steps 1–3 close the login hole, and software can be patched for you while your login habits cannot.

One last reframe: if your security log shows thousands of blocked attempts, that is the system working — those are the ones that failed. The number to worry about is a successful login you do not recognize. With the steps above in place, that number should stay at zero.
