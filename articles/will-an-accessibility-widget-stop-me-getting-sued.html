Will an accessibility widget stop me getting sued?

The blue icon, the toolbar, the promise of instant compliance — and the documented fact that roughly 1 in 5 sued companies already had one installed.

A sales email lands, or a banner ad catches your eye: "Make your website ADA-compliant in 5 minutes — one line of code, no developer needed." It sounds perfect: a little widget with the blue icon, a toolbar pops up, customers can enlarge text and change contrast, and you are protected. This guide is only about that promise — the overlay widgets, not the broader question of demand letters (covered in our sued guide). And the honest answer is: a widget is not the shield its marketing says it is.

The one number that should end the debate

In July 2026, UsableNet — one of the firms that tracks accessibility lawsuits — reported that roughly 20% of the companies sued over website accessibility already had an accessibility widget installed. One in five defendants paid for the very thing that was supposed to prevent the lawsuit, and it did not prevent it. That is a documented fact about lawsuits that actually happened, not a theory. If the widget were a reliable defense, that number would be near zero.

Why a toolbar on top cannot fix what is underneath

Think of it mechanically. An overlay widget adds a panel on top of your page — it lets a visitor zoom the text or strip the colors. But the barriers that get websites sued live inside the page's code: a checkout button with no name a screen reader can read, a form field with no label, a pop-up that traps keyboard users with no way out. A toolbar sitting on top of the page does not rewrite any of that. It is a coat of paint on a staircase with missing steps — the steps are still missing. This is not a statistic; it is how the products are built, and it is why testers using real screen readers keep flagging widget-equipped sites.

The regulator has already weighed in

In 2025 the FTC took action against accessiBe — the biggest name in accessibility overlays — in a $1 million settlement over deceptive claims that the product could make websites compliant with WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (version 2.1, level AA) that courts and testers treat as the de facto standard. The regulator's message was plain: claiming a widget equals compliance is a claim you cannot make. (And to be clear: this is not legal advice, and it is not about whether any particular company is guilty of anything — it is the documented record of what happened.)

What actually reduces the risk

The unglamorous answer is the real one. Risk goes down when the page itself is built right: images that mean something carry descriptions; every form field has a real label; color combinations a low-vision user can read; the whole site — menus, checkout, pop-ups — works by keyboard alone. That is a list of code fixes, not a purchase. Most of them are small individually; a competent web person can work through a typical small-business site in days, not months. A widget can still sit on top as a convenience for visitors — nobody is harmed by a text-resize button — but it is the decoration, not the defense.

The ask-your-developer script

You do not need to learn WCAG to get this handled. Send your web person this: "Please check my site against WCAG 2.1 AA basics — form labels, image descriptions, color contrast, and full keyboard navigation. I want the underlying code fixed, not an overlay widget on top." If a vendor pitches you a widget as the whole answer, ask one question: "If 20% of sued sites already had one of these, what is this protecting me from?" The honest vendors will talk about code fixes. The dishonest ones will change the subject.
