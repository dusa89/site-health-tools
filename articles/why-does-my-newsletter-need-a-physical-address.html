Why Does My Email Newsletter Need a Physical Address? — CAN-SPAM in Plain English

You signed up for an email service, built your first newsletter, and the service will not let you send until you type in a postal address — the kind that goes in the footer of every single email you send. If you run the business out of your house, your first thought is probably: absolutely not, I am not putting my home address on the internet. Reasonable instinct. But the requirement is not the email service being nosy. It is the law, and the service is enforcing it because it has to.

The short answer: a US federal law — the CAN-SPAM Act of 2003 — says every commercial email must include a valid physical postal address where the sender can receive mail. Your email service enforces it because the law applies to them too when they send on your behalf, and because emails that break the rules get flagged, blocked, or used as grounds to shut down your sending account. The address requirement is not a settings page. It is compliance.

What the law actually requires

CAN-SPAM covers commercial email — anything that advertises or promotes a product or service, including your newsletter, your monthly deals email, and your "we're running a special" blast. It does not cover purely transactional email: invoices, receipts, shipping confirmations, password resets. Those can skip the address.

For commercial mail, the rules are a short list:

  • A valid physical postal address. A street address, a P.O. box registered to you, or a rented mailbox. It has to be real and able to receive mail.
  • A working unsubscribe mechanism. Every email must explain how to opt out, and you must honor the request promptly — the law says within 10 business days.
  • Truthful header information. The From, To, and routing information cannot be false or misleading. That is the rule behind the impostor-email problem we covered in another guide.
  • Non-deceptive subject lines. The subject cannot mislead the reader about what is inside the email.

Notice what is NOT on the list: a rule about how often you email, or a requirement that every recipient double-opt-in. CAN-SPAM is a US law; if your customers include Canadians, Canada's CASL has stricter consent rules, and Europe's rules under GDPR are stricter still. If you only email US customers, CAN-SPAM is the floor.

Why the email service blocks you until you add one

Your email service did not invent this. They enforce it for three reasons:

  • Shared liability. When the service sends your email, it is part of the sending chain the law covers. A service full of non-compliant senders becomes a legal target.
  • Deliverability. Gmail and the other big providers treat missing footers — no address, no unsubscribe — as spam signals. An email service that lets you send non-compliant mail is burning its own sending reputation, which is exactly what gets sending accounts suspended.
  • Automated enforcement. The address field is the cheapest compliance check to automate, so every major ESP runs it as a hard gate before the first send.

The practical read: if a service does NOT ask for an address, that is a red flag, not a feature. It is not enforcing the rules everyone else enforces.

What counts as a valid address

The address must be one where you can actually receive postal mail. Acceptable options:

  • Your business address. If you have a storefront, office, or shop, use it. This is the simplest answer.
  • A P.O. box registered to you. The law explicitly allows this. A post office box in your name or your business's name satisfies the requirement.
  • A rented mailbox with a street-style address. Services like the UPS Store give you an address that reads like a suite number rather than a box number. Same legal function as a P.O. box, more professional-looking in a footer.

What does NOT work: a fake address, a previous address you no longer receive mail at, or writing "address withheld" in the footer. The point of the rule is that a recipient can reach you by mail.

If you work from home and do not want your home address public

This is the real worry, and it is a legitimate one. You have two honest options:

1. Get a P.O. box or rented mailbox. This is the standard small-business answer. The footer shows the box, not your kitchen table. A basic P.O. box is one of the cheapest fixed costs in a business, and it doubles as the return address for your whole operation.

2. Decide whether the newsletter needs to be commercial email at all. If what you actually want is to email customers their receipts, appointment reminders, and "your order shipped" updates, those are transactional emails — no address required. The moment you add a coupon, a product announcement, or a "check out our new service" line, it becomes commercial. The fix is not hiding the address; it is being honest about which kind of email you are sending.

What not to do: do not put your home address in and hope nobody notices. Newsletters get forwarded, archived, and posted publicly. Put an address in the footer that you would be comfortable seeing screenshotted onto the internet — because someday it will be.

The bottom line

Your email service is not being nosy; it is enforcing a federal requirement that applies to every commercial email you send. If you are still deciding whether the newsletter should come from your business address at all, settle that first — then get a real address into the footer before you send the first campaign. The address must be real and mail-receiving — a storefront, a P.O. box, or a rented mailbox all qualify. If you work from home, a P.O. box is the standard answer, and it is cheap insurance against both legal trouble and the deliverability damage of non-compliant mail.
