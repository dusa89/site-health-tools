Why Can't I Trust My Email Open Rates Anymore?

The honest answer: a good chunk of your reported opens are machines, not humans. Since 2021, Apple's privacy feature has been 'opening' every email on behalf of Apple Mail users the moment it arrives - whether anyone reads it or not. Industry data shows recorded open rates roughly doubled after that change while click rates barely moved. Below: how the open rate got broken, how much of it is noise, why even click numbers are starting to lie, and the two numbers you should actually run your decisions on.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - email numbers differ list to list and change as your list and your tools change. The facts below describe how the measurement works in general; your own email platform's reports are the verdict on your list.

How the open rate got built - and how it got broken

The open rate was always a little trick. Email has no "they read it" receipt built in, so the industry invented one: every marketing email hides a tiny invisible image, called a tracking pixel. When the recipient's email program loads that image, the sender's platform logs an open. No image load, no open. That is the entire measurement - an image download standing in for human attention.

Then, in September 2021, Apple changed the game. Apple Mail Privacy Protection - an opt-in feature for Apple Mail users - has Apple's own servers download every image in an email the moment it arrives, including the tracking pixel. The pixel fires. Your report counts an open. Nobody read the message. Apple's feature also hides the reader's IP address and device detail, and there is nothing you as the sender can do to turn it off - it is Apple's machine talking to your server, and your server cannot tell it apart from a person.

The doubling that gave the game away

Here is the tell. The DMA's 2026 email benchmarking report shows industry-average open rates sitting around 19% from 2016 through 2021 - then jumping to 43.5% by 2025. Over the same period, click rates stayed between 1.5% and 2.5%. Read that twice: the opens more than doubled while the clicks - the thing a human actually has to do - barely moved. When two numbers that should move together split apart like that, one of them is no longer measuring people.

And the pollution is not small. Apple Mail holds about 46% of the email-client market (Litmus, September 2025 data), and an estimated 50-60% of all recorded opens are affected by the privacy pre-loading. Put together, roughly a quarter to a third of every industry open rate is phantom events - machines, not readers. One data contributor to the DMA report recorded 40% unique opens on a list where the true open rate, once the machine traffic was stripped out, was 11%. Nearly three-quarters of the "opens" were not a human reading anything. In July 2026, over 62% of all tracked email opens - a sample of more than a billion - came from Apple devices, and Litmus itself flags privacy-protection opens as unreliable.

Even clicks are starting to lie

If opens are the broken speedometer, clicks were supposed to be the honest odometer - a click takes a deliberate human finger. Not quite anymore. Corporate security software now pre-clicks the links in incoming mail to check them for threats: Microsoft's Safe Links, for example, rewrites and scans inbound links as part of its protection. A security scanner visiting your link counts as a click in your report. Validity's 2026 deliverability report now warns that bot clicks distort click data too. Clicks are still far more honest than opens, but "a click" on a business-heavy list - where many of your readers sit behind corporate email security - needs the same healthy suspicion.

What to actually trust

Stop making decisions from the open rate as a precise number, and demote it to a trend line. The inflation hits every send on your list roughly equally, so the direction still tells the truth even when the number does not: opens sliding for three months straight means something real is happening - tired subject lines, a cooling list, or a sender-reputation problem brewing. Direction, not precision.

Promote the click-through rate to the headline number. Most clicks still require a deliberate human act, which is why the email industry now treats CTR as the safer primary metric. The fancier cousin, click-to-open rate, divides clicks by opens - and since the opens in the denominator are inflated, the ratio inherits the rot. It keeps one legitimate job: comparing two subject lines or designs sent to the same segment, where the inflation is identical on both sides and cancels out. And the numbers that cannot be faked at all - replies, purchases, bookings, form fills - are the ones that pay the bills. If you track one number seriously, make it revenue per email.

And none of this measurement matters if the mail never reaches the inbox at all. It also matters the other way around: when your engagement numbers look suspicious, the platforms notice. The three traps

Trap one: judging subject lines by opens. The classic test - send two subject lines, keep the winner by open rate - is measuring which version the machines liked. Run the test on click rate instead, or run both versions to the same segment and compare click-to-open rate, where the machine inflation cancels out.

Trap two: deleting "dead" subscribers by open data. Pruning everyone who "never opens" is standard list hygiene - but when a quarter to a third of opens are fake, the truly dead hide among phantom opens, and real readers who block images look dead. Clean by clicks and purchases, not by opens. Our newsletter-cleanup article walks the full decision.

Trap three: the pixel itself in Europe. If any of your subscribers are in France or Italy, the tracking pixel now carries its own legal tripwire: regulators there have ruled that open-tracking pixels used for marketing need explicit consent, separate from consent to receive the email. That is a second reason to build your measurement around clicks and conversions - the numbers you are allowed to trust are also the ones you are allowed to collect.

What to ask your web person

Four questions that get a straight answer: (1) "Does my email platform label Apple privacy opens separately - and can you show me my click rate with and without them?" (2) "Can we strip bot clicks from my link reports, or at least flag corporate-security traffic?" (3) "What is my revenue per email for the last three campaigns - not the open rate, the money?" (4) "If I want to test two subject lines, will you set it up as a same-segment test judged on clicks, so the machine inflation cancels out?"
