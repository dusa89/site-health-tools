Will Adding SSL Slow Down My Website?

The short answer: no — adding SSL will not slow your website down, and in practice it usually makes it faster. The padlock costs a tiny secret-handshake (a fraction of a blink), but it is also the ticket that gets you onto the modern fast lane: every major browser only runs HTTP/2 — the version that loads everything at once instead of one file at a time — over a secure connection. Skipping SSL to "stay fast" means staying on the old, slower road. Below is what the lock really costs, why it is now non-negotiable, and the four setup mistakes that make an SSL switch feel slow — so you can tell your web person exactly what to avoid.

1. What the lock actually costs you

Before your visitor's browser trusts your site, the two sides do a quick secret-handshake: they agree on the encryption and prove the certificate is real. That handshake is the only speed cost SSL adds. Under the modern TLS 1.3 protocol it costs one round trip — one there-and-back between your visitor and your server — down from two round trips under the older TLS 1.2. On a typical connection a round trip is tens of milliseconds; a fraction of a blink. The handshake then happens once per visit, and returning visitors can skip it entirely with a resumption shortcut. It sits inside the dead time before anything appears (the TTFB guide breaks that waiting period down) — and it is the smallest slice of it.

2. The flip: the padlock unlocks the faster highway

Here is the part that surprises owners. The HTTP/2 spec does not technically require encryption — but every major browser only supports HTTP/2 over HTTPS. No padlock, no HTTP/2. Why does that matter? The old HTTP/1.1 loads your page's files in a narrow line: one file at a time per connection, or a small handful in parallel. HTTP/2 multiplexes: it fires dozens of requests down a single connection at the same time, so your CSS, JavaScript, fonts, and images arrive together instead of queuing. On a typical business page with thirty or forty pieces, that one change matters far more than the handshake costs. That is the honest math of the switch: you pay tens of milliseconds for the lock, and you gain the fast lane — which is worth hundreds of milliseconds on anything but the simplest page (the mobile guide covers why this matters most on phones, where connections are slower and every round trip counts double).

3. It is also non-negotiable now, speed aside

Two business reasons, both bigger than milliseconds. First, trust: Chrome shows "Not Secure" on any page still on HTTP, and from October 2026 Chrome enables "Always use secure connections" by default — meaning it warns visitors before loading a public HTTP site. A customer who lands on your quote page and sees a security warning leaves. Second, Google: HTTPS has been an official ranking signal since August 2014, and with over 95% of Chrome traffic already on HTTPS, plain HTTP is now the exception that stands out for the wrong reasons. An SSL certificate is free in minutes from Let's Encrypt or from your host's panel — the free-vs-paid SSL guide covers which kind you need (and note: free and paid certificates are speed-identical; the difference is validation paperwork, not milliseconds).

4. Gotcha #1: the redirect chain (the classic "SSL made it slower")

The most common way an SSL switch feels slow is not the lock — it is the detours the visitor takes getting to the locked page. After the switch there are usually four addresses for your homepage (http with www, http without, https with www, https without), and sloppy setups bounce visitors through two or three of them before landing: http → https → www-version, each hop costing a full round trip. The rule is simple: one hop, straight to the final address (the redirect guide breaks chains down fully). This is the first thing to check if the site felt fine before SSL and sluggish after: the lock did not slow it; the chain did. And if you are routing through Cloudflare, make sure its SSL mode is Full (Strict) — the Cloudflare guide explains how the wrong mode loops.

5. Gotcha #2: mixed content leftovers

After moving to HTTPS, some pages still reach back over plain HTTP for old images, scripts, or fonts — hard-coded http:// addresses baked into the template. Browsers flag or outright block those pieces ("mixed content"), which can leave a page half-rendered or stamp a warning on an otherwise secure page. It is a cleanup job, not a defect in SSL: your web person searches the template for hard-coded http addresses and updates them to the secure versions. A two-minute console check (press F12, reload the page, look for "Mixed Content" lines) shows exactly which files are guilty.

6. Gotcha #3: the host's ancient encryption

The one-round-trip handshake number is TLS 1.3. If your host has not enabled it — some budget shared hosts run only TLS 1.2 — you pay the older two-round-trip price on new connections and miss out on 1.3's snappier resumption. While your web person is in there: confirm the server offers TLS 1.3 and HTTP/2 (nearly all modern hosts do; it is a configuration line, not a purchase). A CDN terminates the encryption at its edge near your visitor instead of at your distant server — the CDN guide — which shrinks the handshake round trip to almost nothing for far-away customers.

7. Gotcha #4: skipping HSTS

One more redirect you can eliminate entirely. Even with the single-hop rule, a first-time visitor who types your address without https:// pays one redirect to reach the secure version. HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security) is a header your site sends once that tells the browser "from now on, only ever use the secure address for this domain" — after that, return visitors skip the redirect hop completely. The order matters: enable it only after HTTPS is working cleanly everywhere (it locks the browser into secure-only, so half-migrated pages get locked out instead of merely warned). Set up once, keep forever.

8. What to tell your web person (the exact words)

Send them this: “We are adding SSL — free certificate from the host or Let's Encrypt. Four requirements. First, confirm the server supports TLS 1.3 and HTTP/2, not just TLS 1.2. Second, every address variant redirects to the one canonical https address in a single hop — no chains, so check http-with-www, http-without, https-with-www, and https-without individually. Third, hunt down all mixed content: search the template for hard-coded http:// assets and update them, then verify with the F12 console on the homepage, contact page, and checkout. Fourth, once HTTPS is clean everywhere, enable HSTS. Judge the result against the 2.5-second target — the handshake costs tens of milliseconds, so if the page got slower, something in those four is wrong and it is not the lock.” A good web person nods at all four. One who blames the padlock for the slowness has not found the chain yet.
