How Do I Know My Web Person Actually Fixed My Speed?

The uncomfortable truth about speed work: “it's faster now” is not a result. Speed is measured in seconds, and any real fix produces two numbers you can compare - the before and the after. If your web person cannot show you both, measured the same way, you have a promise, not a result. The good news: the comparison takes about five minutes, costs nothing, and you can do it yourself without touching a line of code.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - measurement conditions differ test to test. The facts below describe how Google's own speed tests work in general; your own before-and-after numbers are the verdict on your website.

"Faster" is a before-and-after number, not a feeling

Here is the whole discipline in one sentence: ask for the before number and the after number, measured on the same page, with the same test. A legitimate speed job starts with a measurement ("your homepage takes 4.2 seconds to load on a phone") and ends with a new one ("now it takes 2.1 seconds"). Everything else - the invoice, the screenshots, the "trust me, it's much faster now" - is decoration.

Do not rely on your own feeling either. You visit your own site constantly, your browser keeps parts of it saved (our caching article explains how), and you are probably on fast wifi. Your customers are on phones, on cell connections, visiting for the first time. Your feeling is the least reliable speed test in the building - which is exactly why the numbers matter.

Demand the seconds, not the score

Speed tools hand out a 0-to-100 score, and it is the wrong thing to grade your web person on. The score blends a dozen measurements, and some of the easy-to-move ones barely matter to visitors. The number that matters is seconds to load - specifically the largest-contentful-paint figure, which is Google's own stand-in for "when did the page actually look ready?" Our score article breaks down the score-vs-seconds distinction in detail - the short version is that a jump from 62 to 78 points means nothing if the loading time barely moved, while two seconds off the clock is a real win even if the score barely twitched.

Google's own yardstick for those seconds, still unchanged in 2026: under 2.5 seconds is good, over 4 seconds is poor. That is the finish line. If your web person says the work is done and you are still at 5 seconds, the work is not done - no matter what the invoice says.

The Google ranking number takes a month to catch up

Here is the trap that makes honest people look dishonest. Google's speed test shows two kinds of numbers. The lab number is a simulation: Google loads your page on a virtual mid-range phone with a throttled cell connection, from its own servers. It reflects a fix immediately - run it today and the seconds are the seconds.

The field number is real life: it is measured from actual Chrome users visiting your actual site, averaged over a rolling 28-day window, updated roughly once a month. That is the number that feeds Google's page-experience ranking signal - the lab score is just the diagnostic, not what Google grades you on. Which means if the fix went in last Tuesday, the field panel still shows mostly pre-fix visitors for up to four weeks. Judge the fix on the lab number now; check the field number next month. Expecting the field data to move the same week is how owners conclude a real fix did nothing.

This also explains the weirdest everyday speed experience: the test says fast and visitors feel slow. Our feel-slow article covers that mismatch - but for vendor verification, the lab number is the right ruler because it is the only one that reacts to the fix on day one.

The 5-minute fair-comparison checklist

Speed scores wiggle between runs even when nothing changed - our variance article explains why. So a fair before/after needs rules. They take five minutes:

1. Same page, same test. The exact URL (not "homepage" vs "services page"), the same speed tool, and the mobile toggle both times. Mobile is the test that matters - Google's mobile simulation is a mid-range phone on slow 4G, and mobile is where the pain lives. A desktop-only comparison is not a comparison.

2. Run it three times, take the middle. Throw out the fastest and the slowest; keep the middle number. That is your before and your after. One run each is a coin flip - three runs each is a measurement.

3. Use a private window. Your browser saves parts of your site, so your own revisits load faster than any customer ever will. A private/incognito window blocks most of that saved-up speed. This is the single most common reason owners think a fix worked when it did not.

4. Test the money pages, not just the homepage. Vendors optimize what you check. Customers land on service pages, product pages, the contact page - every one of those is a first impression too. Run the before/after on the three pages that make you money.

Three ways a "fix" can look real but isn't

Most web people are honest. But there are three patterns worth knowing, because all three produce a prettier report without helping a single customer:

The desktop switch. The before was mobile, the after is desktop. Desktop tests simulate a fast connection and a powerful computer - of course it is faster. Ask: was the after number measured on mobile?

The one-page polish. Only the homepage got fixed, and that is the only page you tested. The service pages your ads and Google send people to are untouched. Rule 4 above exists because of this one.

The cache illusion. The vendor tested the site themselves, from their own machine, where everything was already saved in their browser - or the test ran through a freshly primed cache that no first-time visitor ever gets. This is why the private window and the middle-of-three rules matter: they strip out the illusion.

One more common disappointment, which is usually nobody's fault: some "fixes" do not fix anything real. The classic is the hero image that got lazy-loading applied - lazy-loading the biggest image on the page actually makes loading slower, as our hero-image article shows. If the after number did not move, the fix may have been a mistake rather than a deception. Ask what each change did to the seconds, and you will find out which.

What to ask your web person

Four questions that get a straight answer: (1) “Send me the mobile seconds for my three money pages, before and after, measured the same way.” (2) “Which single change saved the most time, and how many seconds?” - this tells you whether they understand what they did or are guessing. (3) “When will the real-visitor numbers show this?” - the right answer mentions the roughly four-week window, not tomorrow. (4) “If the speed drops again in six months, what do I check first?” - speed rots as plugins, photos, and scripts pile back on; a real answer is a maintenance plan, not a shrug.

If the answers come back evasive, or the before number never existed, that is itself the finding - and it is worth exactly as much as the invoice you paid.
