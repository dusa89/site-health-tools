Can I Use One Email Inbox for Two Businesses?

The short answer: yes - and this is a designed feature, not a hack. Every serious email platform has a mechanism that lets one person receive mail for several business addresses in a single inbox and send mail that wears each business's name. Google calls it a user alias domain; Microsoft calls them proxy aliases. Below: the three ways to set it up, how sending from the right identity works, how to keep the two businesses' mail sorted inside one inbox, what it costs, the three traps (including the one that tells your customer exactly which setup you use), and the point where two businesses genuinely need two separate accounts.

1. What the one-inbox setup actually is

There are three ways to get two businesses' mail into one inbox, and only two of them are good. Way one: aliases. Your email provider gives your existing mailbox extra addresses. Mail to any of them lands in your inbox, and you can reply wearing whichever business's name you choose. This is the designed mechanism. Way two: send-as identities on free Gmail. Gmail's "Send mail as" lets you add another address under Settings and send from it inside your Gmail account (clean.email; getmailbird.com, updated ~46 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). Way three: forwarding. You point business B's address at business A's mailbox and it all lands in one place. It works for receiving - but forwarding cannot send as business B, and it is the sloppiest of the three. (Forwarding's full story, and its own risks, is the forwarding article.) (And if you are doing the opposite - one business, separate addresses for sales, support, and billing - that article is the mirror image of this one.) The rest of this article assumes you want the real setup: aliases or send-as identities, not forwarding.

2. The three ways to do it properly

On Google Workspace: the user alias domain. If you own the second business's domain, Google's own documentation describes your exact case: "you might manage multiple businesses or brands, each with their own domain" - and gives you two choices. Add the second domain as a user alias domain and every user gets addresses at both domains at no extra cost per user (support.google.com; crawled 2026-10-11). The same name before the @ works at both domains (cloudhq.net; crawled 2026-10-11). Add it as a secondary domain instead when each business has its own separate users - and then you pay a license per account (support.google.com; crawled 2026-10-11). Two businesses, one owner, one login: the alias domain is the one. On Microsoft 365: aliases. Each Microsoft 365 user can carry up to 400 aliases, with no additional fees or licenses - the address just needs to be added to the user (Microsoft Learn admin docs, ms.date 02/02/2026; crawled 2026-10-11). One tenant can run aliases across multiple brand domains; Microsoft's own Q&A calls it a "widely adopted best practice" for organizations that want cost efficiency with centralized management (learn.microsoft.com; crawled 2026-10-11). On free Gmail: send-as identities. Settings, See all settings, the Accounts and Import tab, "Add another email address" under Send mail as. Gmail sends a verification code to that address to prove it is yours; once verified, you pick which identity to send from in the compose window's From dropdown (clean.email; getmailbird.com; crawled 2026-10-11). Two paid licenses of nothing: Workspace users each get up to 30 aliases (skynetiks.com; crawled 2026-10-11), M365 users up to 400, Gmail send-as free.

3. Sending AS the right business - the From discipline

Receiving was the easy half. The half that matters to customers is the sending identity: when you reply to business B's customer, the From line must say business B. With Gmail's send-as, you choose the sender address in the compose window's From dropdown - one click per message (getmailbird.com; crawled 2026-10-11). Gmail's replies can be set to reply from the address each message was sent to - a setting worth switching on - which keeps every thread wearing the right brand without you picking the identity each time. On Microsoft 365, sending from an alias works natively once it is enabled in the Exchange admin center (learn.microsoft.com Q&A; crawled 2026-10-11). The discipline is the human part: an invoice from business A going to business B's customer is a professionalism problem, not a technology problem. If one business sends the vast majority of your mail, set that identity as the default and make the other one the conscious choice.

4. Keeping the two businesses sorted inside one inbox

One inbox that receives two businesses' mail needs a sorting system or it becomes a junk drawer. Filters and labels are the designed answer: a filter that matches mail addressed to business B's address and applies business B's label, so you can read "one business at a time" inside the single inbox. Gmail's plus addressing adds a tagging trick for free: mail to you+plumbing@gmail.com lands in your inbox (the + word is ignored for delivery), and a filter can catch "+plumbing" and route it to a label (pcworld.com, Sep 14, 2026; crawled 2026-10-11). Useful for sign-ups and lead forms - you can see which business surface the mail came through. Two honest limits on the plus trick: it does not hide your real address (the address is sitting inside it), and there is no off switch for one plus address - filters can label or delete its mail, but the address itself keeps working (dev.to; crawled 2026-10-11). Plus tags are for organizing, not for professional addresses. (If the question behind this one is really "my inbox is overwhelming in general," that is a different article's job - this one is about which business the mail belongs to, not volume.)

5. The free-mailbox option: shared mailboxes on Microsoft 365

If business B does not need to be inside YOUR inbox at all - it just needs an address that exists - Microsoft 365 has a fourth option. Shared mailboxes are unlimited on Business Standard, require no license, and each holds 50GB (intertecdatasolutions.com, updated ~103 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). Use one when you want business B's address to live its own separate life - its own inbox, its own sent folder, possibly with an assistant who has access - while business A stays yours alone. Microsoft's own guidance draws the line this way: aliases when one person manages everything; a shared mailbox when an address needs its own inbox or multiple hands (learn.microsoft.com Q&A; crawled 2026-10-11). That same line is the answer to the most common "should I add a person?" question - the assistant-access article covers what changes the day someone besides you needs in.

6. The three traps

Trap one: the "on behalf of" leak. A send-as identity configured properly shows a clean From line. Configured carelessly, it shows "sent by yourname@gmail.com on behalf of you@yourbusiness.com" - which tells the customer exactly how small your setup is. This is not a mystery: the via/on-behalf-of article explains the exact cause and the fix. The workaround-comparison that documents this puts it in a table: the alias route gives clean outbound branding, while sending through group-style routing leaks the on-behalf-of identity (helloari.com, updated ~109 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). Trap two: the calendar and files leak. On Google Workspace, aliases work in Gmail - but the rest of the suite does not know about them. Calendar invites and Drive shares sent "from" an alias still reveal your real primary identity (helloari.com; crawled 2026-10-11). If business B's clients get calendar invites, they will see business A's name. This is the trap nobody warns about because the alias works perfectly right up until it does not. Trap three: forwarding masquerading as aliasing. Forwarding only fixes the receiving half - you can read both businesses' mail, but replies go out wearing the wrong name unless you also set up a send-as identity. People discover this the first time a customer replies "who is this?" Read the forwarding article before you lean on it. (One more neighbor trap: if business B's mail is coming from a web host's free email package instead of a real provider, the hosting-email article explains why that email breaks differently.)

7. When two businesses genuinely need two accounts

The one-inbox setup is perfect for one person wearing two hats. It breaks down at three points. People: the day someone else needs to read or send business B's mail, you need separation - a second account, a shared mailbox, or delegation. One shared inbox with everyone's password is how credentials get stuck to departing employees. Legal and money separation: if the two businesses are separate entities for taxes, liability, or contracts, commingled mail is commingled evidence - your accountant will eventually ask why business B's invoices live in business A's Sent folder. Reputation independence: remember the newsletter-subdomain logic - the subdomain article explains why email streams need their own reputation lanes. If business A runs a promotional campaign that generates spam complaints, business B's mail is only at risk if both businesses send from the same domain; keep a separate domain per business and each stands or falls on its own reputation. One person, two brands, clean separation on paper: one inbox. Anything involving other people, other entities, or shared reputations: separate accounts. (If business B is running on a free @gmail.com address right now instead of its own domain, the free-Gmail article is the first fix - identities only look professional when the address does.)

8. What to tell your web person

Send your assistant or web person this: "I run two businesses and I want one inbox. Four asks. First, set up my second business's domain as an alias domain (Google Workspace) or add its addresses as aliases on my account (Microsoft 365) - no new paid seats, my existing mailbox receives both. Second, configure send-as identities for the second business's addresses and verify them, so the From line wears the right business on every reply - and set replies to default from the address each message was sent to. Third, build me a filter-and-label system so each business's mail gets its own label the moment it lands - I want to read one business at a time. Fourth, test it with me: send to each address, confirm it lands labeled correctly, and confirm a reply wears the correct From name with no 'on behalf of' showing. And tell me honestly whether my calendar invites would reveal the other business's name - if they would, say so before I find out from a customer." The proof is a test message to each address, each labeled right, each reply wearing the right name.
