Why Doesn’t My Business Email Show a Logo in Gmail? — The Blue-Checkmark Problem

You have seen it in your own inbox: some businesses get their logo sitting next to their name, and some get Gmail’s blue checkmark beside that. Then there is yours — a grey initial in a circle, looking unestablished next to them. The logo is not a setting in your email tool and it is not something you buy from Google. It is the reward level above everything our other email guides diagnose: if authentication is passing the exam, the logo is the gold star you get for having passed it. Here is the actual program, the real cost, and the one gate almost everyone fails.

First, where this guide sits among its siblings. Our other email guides diagnose the trust icons in your inbox: the question mark, the “via”, the spam-folder slide, and DMARC itself. This guide is the level above all of them — not “why does my email look broken” but “how do I get the logo everyone notices.” The mechanism is called BIMI — Brand Indicators for Message Identification — and it is the only program that puts your logo next to your name in the inbox. Gmail’s blue checkmark is the second level of that same program, available only to a subset.

The short answer: your logo appears in the inbox when a mailbox provider trusts that you are really you — and the way you prove it is a DNS record, a certificate you pay for yearly, and a DMARC policy at full enforcement. Three requirements, in order, and the order is the point: almost everyone who wants the logo is disqualified at requirement one.

Requirement 1: DMARC at enforcement — the gate almost everyone fails

BIMI requires your DMARC policy to be at quarantine or reject. Google states outright that BIMI does not support p=none — the monitoring-only setting most small businesses sit on. No enforcement, no logo, no exceptions: the certificate question does not even arise until this is done.

This is why so few senders have the logo. Valimail’s 2026 report puts BIMI adoption at about 4% of senders, and the same report puts DMARC enforcement at 42% — meaning more than half of businesses have not finished the step the logo requires. The report names the gap directly: mailbox-provider mandates drove everyone to publish a DMARC record (reporting), but most organizations “set it and forgot it” at the non-protective level. An email-authentication vendor put it bluntly in 2026: BIMI projects stall at DMARC enforcement, not at the certificate. The weeks spent finishing authentication do more for your email than the logo ever will.

The owner test is one question for whoever handles your domain: “What is our DMARC policy?” If the answer is p=none or a shrug, you are not even eligible — and fixing that is deliverability work that pays off whether or not you ever chase the logo.

Requirement 2: the certificate — and which one you actually want

The logo has to be certified as yours, or impersonators would just upload everyone’s logos. Two certificates exist, and the difference between them is the whole decision:

The Verified Mark Certificate (VMC) is the full version. It proves — through a Mark Verifying Authority — that your organization owns the logo, and in Gmail it is the only certificate that produces the blue checkmark. The catch is structural: a VMC requires your logo to be a legally registered trademark (USPTO, EUIPO, WIPO, or a government seal). If you do not already hold one, trademark registration typically takes six to twelve months — the certificate timeline is measured in years, not days.

The Common Mark Certificate (CMC) gets the logo displayed but no blue checkmark in Gmail. It exists for businesses without a registered trademark. Cheaper than a VMC, and the honest choice if you want the logo without the trademark project.

The pricing, from published third-party figures checked in 2026: a VMC runs roughly $1,000 to $1,500 a year depending on the issuer (DigiCert’s own list price, checked August 2026, was $1,752; resellers list around $1,249), and a CMC around $949 to $1,416 a year. Those are per-year subscription prices, they move, and they do not include the trademark — which, if you need one, is a separate legal expense on that six-to-twelve-month timeline. Get a current quote rather than budgeting from an article.

Requirement 3: the logo file itself

The easy part, once the above is done: an SVG Tiny PS version of your logo, minimum 96 by 96 pixels for Gmail, in absolute pixel dimensions, on a solid background, 32KB or smaller — published in a BIMI DNS record. If your logo has transparency or fancy gradients, this is where the design conversation happens: the inbox logo is a small, flat mark, and complex logos get simplified for it.

Where the logo actually shows up

BIMI support is not universal. The BIMI Group lists the participating mailbox providers as Apple, AU (KDDI), Fastmail, Gmail, La Poste, Yahoo, and Zone — and each sets its own display criteria, including the right to decline a logo on reputation grounds even when the configuration is correct. Translation: do everything right and your logo still may not show everywhere — it shows where the providers trust you.

One hard boundary worth knowing: none of this is available on a free @gmail.com address. The blue checkmark and BIMI are for organizations on a custom domain — if you are still sending business email from a free address, that is the step before any of this (that guide).

So should you do it?

Honest read, in owner terms: for a services business sending a few thousand emails a month, BIMI is a nice-to-have that comes after DMARC enforcement, never before it. Getting to enforcement is the work that actually improves deliverability; the logo is the badge you get for having done it. For a consumer brand where inbox recognition drives open rates — retail, hospitality, anything where the customer picks which email to open — the math changes, and the logo earns its keep. Either way, the order is the same: enforcement first, certificate second, logo third. The businesses you see with the checkmark did not buy it — they qualified for it.

The bottom line

The logo next to the name is not a feature of your email tool and not a Google upsell — it is a public proof that you finished your email authentication properly. Three requirements, in a fixed order: DMARC at quarantine or reject (the gate that disqualifies most businesses — about 4% of senders qualify, per Valimail’s 2026 report), a certificate (the VMC for the blue checkmark, which requires a registered trademark; the CMC for the logo without it), and the logo file itself. Ask your domain person the one question — “what is our DMARC policy?” — and you will know exactly which of the three you are standing in front of.
