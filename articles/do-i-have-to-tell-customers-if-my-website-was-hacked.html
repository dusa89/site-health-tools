Do I Have to Tell My Customers If My Website Was Hacked?

The honest answer: if customer information was actually taken, the law in your state almost certainly says yes - and being a small business does not get you out of it. All 50 states, plus DC, have data-breach notification laws, and they apply to every business type - sole proprietors and LLCs included. The duty is triggered when someone unauthorized gets hold of your customers' personal information: names with account numbers, passwords, Social Security numbers, that kind of thing. Below: what triggers it, how fast the clock runs, who else has to hear about it, and the first-48-hours checklist.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - deadlines and thresholds change when laws change. This is general information about how breach-notification laws work, not legal advice. If customer data was taken, talk to a lawyer before you send any notices - the details of your situation decide which states' laws apply.

The verdict first: yes, probably, and the clock is already running

Every state has its own version of the rule, but the shape is the same everywhere: if your business owns or holds computerized personal information about residents of a state, and that information is acquired by someone unauthorized, you must tell the affected people. Being small is not an exemption. A one-person shop with a breached customer list is under the same duty as a chain.

About 20 states put a hard number on the deadline - between 30 and 60 days. Florida, California, and Colorado give you 30 days; Texas gives you 60. The rest say "without unreasonable delay," which is lawyer-speak for: don't sit on it. If you have customers in the EU, their rule is stricter: 72 hours to notify the regulator after you become aware of the breach.

What actually triggers the duty - and what does not

The trigger is not "my site got defaced" or "there was malware in the theme." The trigger is personal information in the wrong hands: a customer's name combined with an account number, password, Social Security number, driver's license, or medical info. If the hacker only planted spam links and never touched the customer database, most laws do not require notification - but you still have to be able to prove that, which is why the investigation matters.

One important exception: data that stayed properly encrypted, with the encryption key safe, generally does not trigger the duty in most states - the thief got gibberish, not information. This is one reason our article on not storing card numbers matters: information you never held can never leak.

It is not just your customers who have to hear about it

In roughly 36 states, big-enough breaches must also be reported to the state attorney general - the thresholds are things like 500 affected residents in California and Colorado, 250 in Texas. If more than 1,000 people are affected, most states' laws also require notifying the credit bureaus. And if the breach touched payment cards, your payment processor has its own reporting rules on top of all of this. A breach that feels like "a website problem" can turn into four or five separate notifications - which is why people who clean up hacks for a living will tell you: get the facts straight before you announce anything.

The first-48-hours checklist

One: do not wipe the server. Your first instinct will be to delete everything and start fresh. Resist it. The server logs are the evidence that shows what the hacker took - and what they did not. Back up the hacked site first, exactly as it is, then clean it. Our hacked-website response article walks through the mechanics.

Two: lock down access immediately. Change every password connected to the site - hosting, admin, database, email. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere it is offered. If a former developer or employee had access, revoke it now; see our old-developer-access article.

Three: get a professional to confirm what was taken. The notification duty depends on facts - which tables, which fields - that are hard to establish from the inside. This is some of the most valuable money a breached business spends. If you carry cyber insurance, call the carrier before hiring anyone: many policies cover the forensics and notification costs but require their own approved responders, as our cyber-insurance article explains.

Four: talk to a lawyer before notifying. The notices have to say specific things - what happened, what data was involved, what customers should do, what you are doing about it, and how to reach you. A lawyer makes sure the notice satisfies every state involved, because your customers do not all live in your state.

The one thing you must not do: cover it up

In 2016, Uber had a breach affecting 57 million users - and instead of notifying, the company paid the hackers to delete the data and said nothing. When it came out, the cover-up became a bigger legal disaster than the breach. The lesson for a small business: the fine for late notification is bad, but the penalty for hiding a breach is worse - and customers who hear it from you are far more likely to stay; customers who hear it from the news are far more likely to leave. Our piece on why small sites get targeted explains why "too small to matter" is exactly the wrong comfort.

The deeper lesson: breaches are detected by finding them, and finding them is a security-check problem. Malware that sits on a site for months - the kind our reinfection article describes - is a breach waiting to be discovered.
