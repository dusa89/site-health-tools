Can my website really get me sued — and what does 'accessible' even mean?

4,928 web accessibility lawsuits were filed in the US in 2025 — and settling once doesn't end it. Here are the five things plaintiff testers actually flag, why the $50 widget in the corner won't save you, and the honest, cheap protection plan.

Picture a letter arriving at your shop. It says your website discriminates against blind customers, cites a federal law you have never read, and demands a settlement check plus a website overhaul — within 30 days. It is not from a customer. It is from a law firm that sends hundreds of these a month, and the website it names is yours.

This is not a scam, exactly. It is an industry. And whether you are a likely target has almost nothing to do with how nice your website looks.

The numbers behind the letters

Accessibility company UsableNet reviews the actual complaints filed in federal and key state courts every year. Its 2025 year-end report (published January 8, 2026) counted 4,928 web accessibility lawsuits filed in the US in 2025. The federal count alone was 3,117 — up 27% from 2,452 in 2024 (Seyfarth Shaw's federal tally). Since 2021, plaintiffs have filed more than 4,000 of these every single year.

Two caveats that matter. First, a filed case is not a guilty verdict — the vast majority settle quietly, and the settlement terms are rarely public. Second, the lawsuits are only the visible part: for every case filed, many more businesses get a demand letter and pay or fix without anything ever reaching a court. Nobody publishes a count of those, so the true volume is bigger than any number you will read.

Where the cases land is not random. Roughly 70% target e-commerce defendants — if you sell anything online, you are in the hunted class. In 2025, 35.8% of the top 500 e-commerce retailers received at least one accessibility lawsuit (UsableNet, January 2026). New York state courts alone account for over a third of all state-level suits, with New York and California state courts combined seeing nearly 2,000 filings in 2025 — and a business does not need a physical location in those states to be sued there. If a New Yorker can open your website, a New York court may hear the case.

And settling once does not end it: 46% of 2025 federal cases involved companies that had already been sued before. Paying the first letter without fixing the website is buying a subscription to the problem.

Why a letter can reach you at all

The law behind most of these is the Americans with Disabilities Act — the 1990 federal law written for wheelchair ramps and braille signs. Its Title III covers "places of public accommodation." For thirty years courts have argued about whether a website counts, and the split is still real. But the trend is one-directional: the Justice Department has long taken the position that the websites of public-facing businesses are covered, and judges keep letting cases proceed.

California adds its own engine: the state's Unruh Act sets a minimum of $4,000 in statutory damages per violation of disability-discrimination law. That is a published statute, not a rumor — and it is a large part of why California businesses see so many demand letters. A few alleged violations and the number on the page gets serious fast.

The practical standard everyone points at is called WCAG 2.1 Level AA — the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, a public checklist of about 50 requirements written by the World Wide Web Consortium. No federal law names it for private businesses, but the Justice Department pointed state and local governments at it in an April 2024 rule, and plaintiff firms treat it as the test. If a tester can show your site fails its checklist, you are negotiating, not debating.

What the testers actually check

The people behind these cases are not browsing your site like a customer. A tester opens it with a screen reader — software that reads the page aloud for blind users — and walks a script: can I reach everything with a keyboard? Does every image have a text description? Does every form field have a label? The same five failures show up in complaint after complaint:

1. Photos with no descriptions. Your product and team photos are blank to a screen reader if they carry no text alternative. This is the most common flag in the complaints — and the easiest to fix.

2. Forms nobody labeled. A "contact us" box that shows "Name" inside the field but tells a screen reader nothing. The placeholder text you see is invisible to the software reading the page.

3. Buttons and links with no names. "Click here" links and icon-only buttons (the little magnifying glass, the hamburger menu) that announce themselves as "button" and nothing else.

4. Keyboard traps. Menus and pop-ups that only work with a mouse. A visitor who navigates by keyboard — or by voice control — gets stuck or locked out.

5. Missing basics. No declared page language, no meaningful page title, text too low-contrast to read. The unglamorous stuff that takes minutes to fix and shows up in nearly every complaint.

Here is the uncomfortable part: these are template-level fixes on most small business sites, not rebuilds. The same audit that finds them finds the other 39 things wrong with your site — which is why one professional check beats a decade of hoping.

The $50 widget in the corner is not a shield

You have seen them: the little accessibility icon that opens a menu of font sizes and contrast toggles. Vendors sell these overlays as lawsuit protection. The filing data says otherwise. UsableNet's July 2026 analysis found that roughly 20% of the companies sued already had an accessibility widget installed — and stated plainly that installing one "does not establish that a website is accessible, nor does it necessarily resolve the underlying barriers alleged in a claim."

Worse, the marketing around them attracted the government's attention: in 2025 the FTC ordered overlay vendor accessiBe to pay $1 million over deceptive claims that its product could make websites compliant with accessibility guidelines. The order was about the claims, not the code — but the message lands the same either way. A widget that sits on top of broken code does not fix the code, and a plaintiff's tester can see both.

The honest protection plan

1. Find out where you stand, free, in ten seconds. The check below reads your live homepage the way a tester's software does — the images, the forms, the labels, the structure — and scores what it finds, in plain English. No signup, no widget to install.

2. Fix the big five above. Alt text on images, labels on every form field, real names on every button and link, keyboard-reachable menus, declared language and title. On a typical small business site this is an afternoon's work for whoever runs your website — not a redesign.

3. Keep a record. Publish a short accessibility statement page saying what standard you aim for and how to report a problem. It does not stop a lawsuit, but it is evidence of good faith — and it gives a real person with a real problem a way to reach you before a law firm does.

4. Recheck after every site change. A new theme, a new plugin, a redesigned menu — any of them can silently re-break what you fixed. Accessibility is a maintenance item, like the certificate that keeps your padlock green.

One more thing, plainly: this guide is not legal advice. It is what the lawsuit numbers and the actual complaints show. If a letter ever arrives, talk to a lawyer before you pay or sign anything — and fix the website either way, because the second letter is the one that really costs.
