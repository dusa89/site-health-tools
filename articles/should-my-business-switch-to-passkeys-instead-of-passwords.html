Should My Business Switch to Passkeys Instead of Passwords?

Your customers unlock their phones a hundred times a day without typing anything, while their business accounts still depend on passwords that leak, get reused, and get phished. A passkey takes the phone's way of logging in — fingerprint, face, PIN — and puts it on your accounts, with one decisive upgrade: there is nothing to type, so there is nothing for a scammer to steal. Here is what that actually means, the parts nobody tells you, and the order to switch things over in.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-12 - not promises about your situation. Account setups differ, and providers change details over time. The general picture here is stable; the specifics of your accounts are worth checking with your email provider or web person.

What a passkey actually is (no math required)

A password is a secret you and the website both know — and that is the whole problem. Every copy of that secret is a target: the one in your head, the one in your browser, the one in the website's database, the one you type into a page that might be fake.

A passkey replaces the shared secret with a key pair made just for one website. Your device creates two keys that fit each other: it keeps the private key locked inside the device and gives the website only the public key, which is useless on its own. When you log in, you press your finger to the phone or look at it, and the device uses the private key to sign a one-time challenge from the site. Nothing you know is ever typed, sent, or stored on the website's servers. Our browser-password guide named passkeys as the structural fix — this is the full version of that answer.

Think of it this way: a password is a combination lock where you shout the combination through a door and hope the person on the other side is who they claim to be. A passkey is your phone vouching for you to a bouncer it already knows, with your fingerprint as the ID check.

Why a phishing email cannot steal one

This is the part that matters most, because phishing is the attack your business will actually face. A phishing email tricks you into typing your password and code into a lookalike page, and then the attacker's software relays them to the real site in real time. Every defense that depends on you being careful — check the URL, don't click links — fails the moment one tired employee has a bad day.

Passkeys move that judgment from the human to the device. When your passkey was created, it was glued to the real website's exact domain. When you land on a lookalike — say, a clever fake of your bank or your email provider — the device checks the address the way a bouncer checks a guest list. The fake domain is not on the list, so the device simply refuses to offer a credential. There is no secret for you to type, so there is nothing you can accidentally hand over, and nothing the attacker can relay. The attack dies quietly, without anyone needing to be clever.

That is also why a passkey beats the two-factor code on its own. A code sent by text or generated by an app still gets typed into a page — and relayed in real time if the page is fake. Our two-factor guide is still worth following everywhere passkeys are not yet offered, because 2FA on a password is far better than a password alone. But a passkey does the job of both halves in one step, in about three to five seconds, with success rates around 98% reported in industry deployments — versus the familiar wrong-password-try-again dance.

The numbers behind the shift

This stopped being a tech-demo years ago. Google says more than 800 million Google accounts now sign in with passkeys; when Google made passkeys the default for personal accounts back in late 2023, passkey sign-ins rose 352%. Microsoft made passkeys the default for all new Microsoft accounts in May 2025 and saw authentications jump 120% — and inside Microsoft itself, phishing-resistant authentication now covers 99.6% of its own users and devices. The FIDO Alliance, the industry group behind the standard, put the worldwide total at 5 billion passkeys in active use as of May 2026, with about 90% of people having heard of them and three in four having enabled at least one.

Context for why this matters to a business owner: Verizon's 2025 breach report found that 88% of attacks against basic web applications involved stolen credentials. The attacks are not breaking the locks; they are stealing the keys people typed somewhere. Passkeys remove the typing.

One honest footnote on coverage: passkeys are everywhere at the top of the internet and thin below it. Roughly half to three-fifths of the biggest 100 websites support them — Google, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, eBay, PayPal, the social networks — but only about one in five of the top thousand, and an estimated one in ten to one in twenty of the top ten thousand. Your bank and your big accounts almost certainly offer passkeys today. Your small niche tools may not yet. That is fine: you switch what you can and keep strong unique passwords plus 2FA where you cannot.

The parts nobody tells you (the honest trade-offs)

Passkeys are the best login technology available, and they still have failure modes you should plan for. Here they are, plainly.

1. Losing your phone. If your passkeys sync through your Apple or Google account, a lost phone is an errand, not a disaster: sign into your Apple or Google account on the replacement and the passkeys come back. Apple's recovery uses a code to your trusted number plus your old device passcode; Google uses your password-manager PIN or previous screen lock. If you need to log in before the replacement arrives, most sign-in screens can show a QR code that you scan with any device holding the passkey — a Bluetooth check confirms you are physically nearby, and the private key never leaves your phone. Either way, revoke the passkey tied to the lost device in your account settings as soon as you can. And keep your recovery phone number and recovery email current — those are your backup doors.

2. The cloud becomes the new master key. Synced passkeys live behind your Apple or Google account. If someone takes over that account, they get the passkeys with it. That does not make passkeys worse than passwords — your password resets mostly live behind the same account — but it means your Apple or Google account deserves the strongest protection you have: passkey on it first (yes, accounts can protect themselves with passkeys), recovery details current, and ideally a second sign-in method kept offline. The alternative some security folks prefer is device-bound passkeys on a hardware key like a YubiKey, which never syncs anywhere — maximum security, but lose the key with no backup and you are locked out, so those users keep a second key somewhere safe.

3. Passwords kept as backup keep the risk. Most sites that offer passkeys still keep your old password as a fallback. As long as that fallback exists, the account has a phishable path, and the industry is blunt about the endpoint: the goal is to eliminate passwords, not just supplement them. Where a service lets you remove the password entirely after setting up passkeys, do it. Where it does not, the passkey still upgrades your daily logins — just keep 2FA on and treat the password like a fire exit: present, but never the front door.

4. Passkeys guard the door, not the room. A passkey stops attackers from logging in as you. It does not stop malware on your own computer from grabbing your active browser session after you have logged in. That is the same advice as ever: keep devices updated, and treat unexpected password-reset emails with suspicion — our password-reset-email guide covers how attackers abuse the recovery path.

What to actually do (the practical order)

1. Passkey your master accounts first. Your email account is the master key to everything else — an attacker who owns your email can reset their way into most of your accounts, as our email-takeover guide explains. Turn on passkeys for your Google or Microsoft business email, your Apple ID, and your bank, in that order. On Google this is under account Security settings; the account will then ask for the passkey for sign-in and sensitive actions.

2. Use a synced manager, not one device. Store passkeys in iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, or a cross-platform manager so a lost phone is a recovery errand, not a lockout. This is the step that turns the lost-phone scenario from trade-off #1 into a non-event.

3. Keep one offline backup. Save each account's recovery codes somewhere that is not your phone — printed and filed, or in a safe. A hardware security key as a second passkey for your most important accounts is the professional-grade version of the same idea.

4. Turn it on everywhere it is offered. Each major account you convert is one fewer password that can leak in a breach — and our breach-leak guide is a sobering reminder of how often those databases spill. Leave 2FA switched on wherever a passkey is not yet offered.

5. If you have a team, start with the privileged few. The accounts that can move money, publish to your website, or change DNS are where passkeys pay first. Brute-force bots hammer password boxes all day — our login-attempts guide shows what that looks like — and a passkey gives those bots nothing to guess. If you run WordPress, ask your web person about passkey login plugins for the admin accounts.

6. If you run Google Workspace, ask about the admin switch. Google Workspace gives administrators passkey settings and reporting for end-user sign-in, and Microsoft's business platform provides targeted passkey profiles with adoption reporting — the rollout is now an admin setting, not a development project.

Four questions for your web person

1. “Which of our important accounts — email, bank, website admin, social — offer passkeys today, and can we turn them on this week?”

2. “Are our passkeys syncing through our Apple or Google accounts, and is there a second device or a hardware key as backup?”

3. “For the accounts where we added passkeys, can we remove the old password entirely — and if not, is two-factor still on?”

4. “Are our recovery phone numbers, recovery emails, and offline recovery codes current, so a lost phone is an errand and not a lockout?”

The bottom line

Passwords ask you to memorize secrets and then blame you when the secrets get stolen. Passkeys ask your device to vouch for you with a fingerprint — and because there is nothing to type, a phishing email has nothing to catch. The trade-offs are real but manageable: sync through your Apple or Google account, keep an offline backup, and remove the password fallback wherever the service allows it. Five billion passkeys are already in use, the biggest platforms made them the default, and your business's most important accounts probably offer them today. Start with your email. Everything else gets easier after that.
