Why Does My Website Show Spam Only on Phones? — The Hack That Hides From You

A customer tells you their phone sent them somewhere strange when they visited your website. You pull the site up on your laptop, and everything looks perfectly normal. So you dismiss it — phone glitch, bad ad, customer error. But it was not any of those. Your site has been hacked in a way designed to hide from you specifically: the malware checks who is visiting, shows you the real site, and redirects everyone on a phone to spam. Here is why your checks passed, how to catch it, and what to do next.

First, where this guide sits among its siblings: it is not the ordinary redirect hack — that guide is for redirects everyone can see, including the owner. It is not the injected-ads problem covered here, which is also visible to the owner. And it is not the general hacked-site recovery walkthrough — that is here. This guide is about the one variant where the owner is the LAST person to know: the malware picks its audience, and you are not in it.

The short answer: your website is not fine. A class of malware — called cloaking — decides what to show based on the visitor’s device, browser, location, and where they came from. It shows the owner the clean site and redirects mobile visitors to spam. Your desktop spot-check is exactly what it was built to pass. Three tests below, in order, will surface it.

Why your site looked fine: the malware picks its audience

Think of it like a storefront with a bouncer. When you — the owner — visit from your laptop, you see your real website. When a customer visits on a phone, the malicious code checks the details of the visit (is this a mobile device? did they arrive from Google? is this a real person’s connection, not a security scanner’s?) and quietly sends them somewhere else: a fake shopping site, a scam app download, a “you won a prize” page.

The design is deliberate, and it has been the playbook for years. Google updated its guidelines with a mobile-specific example of the abuse: “desktop users receive a normal page, while mobile users are redirected to a completely different spam domain.” Google’s hacked-sites guidance says the same about how it behaves in the wild — the redirect can depend on referrer, user-agent, or device, so clicking your URL from search results redirects a visitor while you, typing the address directly into a desktop browser, see nothing wrong.

The scanners get fooled the same way you do. Phishing kits studied in September 2026 were found to show scam pages only to phones on home or mobile connections in the targeted country — while data-center scanners (where security companies’ automated checks run) got a blank page or something harmless (Geek Mamas, Sept 11 2026). Site malware uses the same audience-gating: clean to the people who check, dirty to the people who pay.

A Cloudflare client-side security report from September 2026 showed how sophisticated the gating has become: one “paid-mobile cloaker” campaign only fired when a long condition list matched — target host, narrow viewport, specific marketing tags, limited page-view window — disabled nine monitoring and analytics tools so nobody would notice, and carried a 325-entry IP list to hide from cloud systems, VPNs, and security researchers. These payloads, the report noted, are “written to wait for the right victim, then act in ways that would be easy to miss during a quick scan.” That is what you are up against: it is not a bug that looks like a glitch. It is software engineered to survive your attention.

The 3 mobile checks that surface it

Run these in order. Each one catches what the last one can miss.

Check 1 — A real phone on a real mobile connection. Borrow a phone that is not yours — a friend’s, a family member’s — and open your site in its mobile browser with Wi-Fi OFF (cellular data on). Some cloaking code checks whether the phone is one it has “seen” before, and some checks the connection type. If the site misbehaves on that phone and not on yours, you have found your answer. A template-malware checklist published in September 2026 puts it plainly: a real phone surfaces mobile-only redirects that your desktop browser cannot.

Check 2 — Search-and-click, not type-and-visit. On the phone, search your business name on Google and click your listing. Then, separately, type your web address directly into the browser’s address bar and visit. If the search click redirects but the direct visit does not, the malware is gating on the referrer — exactly the behavior Google describes. This is the most common owner-proof failure: they typed the address, saw a clean site, and concluded the customers were wrong.

Check 3 — Ask a security scanner, but the right one. Run Sucuri’s free SiteCheck (sucuri.net) — but know what it can and cannot do: it checks the browser-visible code and blocklist status, not your server’s files, and a clean result from one device still does not prove safety. Its real value here is the blocklist half: if Google or others have already flagged your domain, that tells you the problem has been running long enough to get noticed. If SiteCheck is clean AND Check 1 found a redirect, that is not a contradiction — it is confirmation the malware hides from scanners too.

Where the code hides (so your cleanup actually finishes it)

Once the checks confirm it, the cleanup has to hit every hiding place at once — because this is the kind of infection that “comes back.” In October 2026, researchers at Sucuri disclosed “SC” malware that persists across at least eight locations at once — website files, the database, even shared server memory — and rebuilds any deleted component from the survivors on the next page load (ThaiCERT, Oct 2 2026). Cleaning one spot while eight others stand is why owners remove the hack three times and watch it return each morning.

On a WordPress site (where most small-business hacks land), the usual hiding places for cloaked redirects are: the .htaccess file at the site’s root (server-level redirect rules); the database, especially options tables and post content (redirect code survives file cleanups); theme files and functions.php; rogue JavaScript injected into the page footer or header; and Must-Use plugins, which don’t appear in the normal plugin list. The redirect code itself often contains a device check — code that reads the visitor’s user-agent string for “android”, “iphone”, or “mobile” before firing. If you find a user-agent check you didn’t write, you found the bouncer.

If you are not technical, give this paragraph to your host or developer: “The site has device-selective redirect malware. It needs a full-file + database scan, not a plugin sweep, and every backdoor must be cleared in the same pass — persistence-style malware rebuilds from surviving copies.” A competent host cleans this as a routine job; it is the partial cleanups that fail.

Why you cannot ignore this: the Google half of the damage

The spam redirects are the visible half. The invisible half is what Google does when it catches on. Google’s “sneaky redirects” guideline treats a mobile-only redirect to a spam domain as a violation — and the penalty for guideline violations runs up to manual action, including removal from Google’s index entirely (via Search Engine Land, on Google’s Webmaster blog). Your site can look perfect in your browser while Google quietly prepares to erase it from search results. The longer a cloaked hack runs, the deeper the hole: every week of spam redirects is a week of customers who will never trust the address again.

This is also why “it looked fine when I checked” is the most expensive sentence in small-business security. In the c/side Q2 2025 client-side attack report, 72,740 compromised websites were identified across campaigns that included mobile redirects — one vendor’s detection dataset, but a sense of the scale: your site is not special, and the malware is not personal. It is automated, and it banks on you checking from your laptop.

What not to do

Don’t declare it clean because it looked fine on your desktop. That check is the one the malware is designed to pass. Check 1 and Check 2 above are the actual tests.

Don’t trust one clean scanner result. Scanners see one snapshot of one environment; selective payloads wait for conditions the scanner doesn’t match. A clean scan that conflicts with what a real phone shows means the phone is right.

Don’t clean one spot and stop. Persistence-style infections rebuild from surviving copies. If the redirect returns after cleanup, the previous pass missed a hiding place — database, shared memory, or a file nobody looked at — not a new infection.

Don’t keep the site live “until you get around to it.” While a cloaked redirect runs, Google may be watching, customers are being sent to scams in your name, and some of those scam pages are harvesting the visitors’ information — against your reputation. A temporary maintenance page beats a live hijack.

The bottom line

If your site looks clean on your laptop but misbehaves on phones, assume a cloaked hack until the three checks say otherwise: a real phone on cellular data, a search-and-click comparison against a direct visit, and a scanner run for blocklist status. The malware is not a glitch and it is not random — it is engineered to hide from exactly the person who owns the site. Check the way the victims see it, clean every hiding place in one pass, and get it done this week: Google treats mobile-only spam redirects as a violation, and the clock on that penalty runs whether you can see the problem or not.
