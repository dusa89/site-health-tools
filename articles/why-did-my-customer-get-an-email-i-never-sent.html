Why did my customer get an email from my business that I never sent?

Your customer paid an 'invoice' you never sent — because scammers are emailing people while wearing your business name. FBI numbers: $3.05 billion lost to business email compromise in 2025. How the costume works, why your domain is free for the taking, and the 5-step lock that costs nothing.

Here is a phone call no business owner expects: a customer rings up and says, “I got your invoice — I already paid it.” You check your sent folder. You never sent an invoice. Then you find out the customer did pay someone — just not you.

This is one of the most damaging scams in business email, and it hits in two directions. Criminals send emails wearing your business’s name to your customers, asking them to pay a fake invoice. And they send emails wearing your vendor’s name to you, asking you to send your next payment to new bank details — which belong to the thief. Either way, the money lands in a stranger’s account, and the real business is left holding the apology.

How the trick actually works

It almost never starts with hacking. It starts with the costume.

The display name is not the address. Your email app shows you the friendly name — “Shawn Todd Plumbing” — and hides the real address unless you look for it. Scammers set the display name to your business name while the real sender address is something like shawntoddpIumbing@gmail.com, or a lookalike domain: acmeplurnbing.com (that is “rn” standing in for “m”), or yourname-billing.com. At a glance on a phone, it looks exactly like you.

The urgent boss note. A message arrives that appears to be from the owner: “Are you free? I need a quick wire done before noon, it’s confidential.” Microsoft documented a campaign running August 3–5, 2026 that sent more than one million such emails in three days — impersonating CEOs and CFOs, pushing accounts-payable staff toward ACH transfers of nearly $50,000, dressed up with fake invoices and fabricated email threads that made it look like executives had already approved the payment. Microsoft also reported indicators that generative AI helped draft the templates — so the next wave will be more polished than this one.

The vendor bank-detail change. This one is quieter and often costlier. An email arrives that looks like it is from a supplier you have paid for years: “We’ve changed banks — please use these details for the next invoice.” The invoice attached looks exactly like their real ones. You pay it. Days or weeks later the real supplier asks where their money is. The money is gone, and the bank that received it may be three countries away.

The real numbers

The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center published its 2025 annual report in April 2026. Business email compromise — the crime family these scams belong to — drew 24,768 complaints and about $3.05 billion in reported losses, making it the second-costliest internet crime type of the year. Separately, phishing and spoofing was the most reported crime type of all: 191,561 complaints. (FBI figures are reported losses only — most victims never file, so the true totals are higher; the report is the floor, not the ceiling.)

The consumer side confirms how far the impersonation playbook reaches: the FTC reports Americans lost $3.5 billion to imposter scams in 2025, the most-reported fraud type for the ninth year in a row, and roughly one in three fraud reports involved someone pretending to be a trusted business, financial institution, government agency, or vendor.

In the business world specifically: a December 2025 PYMNTS survey of 60 heads of payments at companies with $100 million to $1 billion in revenue found 47% had experienced fake-invoice scams in the past year — the most common social-engineering attack reported. And a September 2026 research write-up from procurement platform Ivalua found 40% of organizations had been hit by invoice fraud in the previous 12 months, with an average loss of £270,000 per incident and an average of nine days before the fraud was even identified. Ivalua sells fraud-prevention software, so treat its numbers as directional — but the direction matches everything else: this is routine now, not exotic.

Why your name is free costume — and how to lock it

Here is the part most owners never learn: by default, anyone on earth can send an email wearing your business name. Nothing stops them. Email was designed without a caller-ID check, and the fix — three records on your domain called SPF, DKIM, and DMARC — is optional. Our guide on what DMARC is explains the machinery; the short version is that DMARC is the one record that tells Gmail and Outlook, “if a message claims to be from this domain but cannot prove it, reject it.”

Without that policy, a scammer sends “invoices” from your name and providers have no instruction to stop them. With it — set to quarantine or reject, not just monitor — your name stops being a free costume. That single record is the difference between scammers impersonating your business at will and having to find another costume.

Note the asymmetry: the free email check below tests your domain’s records. When criminals impersonate your vendor, the lock that protects you is the same lock on their domain — which you cannot control. That is why the callback rule in step 2 below matters more than any technology.

The protection plan

1. Put the lock on your own name today. Check your domain’s SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records with the free tool below. If DMARC is missing or set to “none” (monitor-only), that is the gap scammers walk through. Moving it to quarantine or reject is usually a one-line change — your registrar or email provider’s support can do it in minutes. This is the highest-leverage email-security action a small business can take.

2. Make the callback rule company policy. Any request to pay new bank details — from a vendor, a boss, anyone — gets verified by calling a phone number you already have on file, not one in the email. Never the number in the suspicious message. This one habit would have stopped most of the invoice-fraud cases in the Ivalua numbers above.

3. Learn the display-name test (30 seconds). In Gmail, click the small arrow or hover the name to see the real address. Check the domain letter by letter — scammers bank on skimming. If a “vendor” email comes from a free-mail address (@gmail, @outlook) while their real invoices never have, that is a red flag on its own. Teach this to anyone in your business who touches money.

4. Warn your customers before a scammer does. A one-line note on your invoices or in your newsletter — “We will never email you new bank details. If you get one, call us before paying.” — turns every customer into a tripwire. The businesses that recover fastest from being impersonated are the ones whose customers called first instead of paying.

5. Report it. Forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org, and file a complaint with the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. The numbers in this article exist because people filed — and each report makes the pattern easier to trace.

A customer who pays a scammer wearing your name does not just cost you that payment. They tell themselves your business got them scammed. The name you built your business on is worth more than every invoice you will ever send — and locking it costs nothing.
