Why Do My Big Email Attachments Keep Bouncing?

Here is a special kind of frustrating: you attach the signed contract or the before-and-after photos, your email says the limit is 25 megabytes, your file is 20, and it should sail through. Ten minutes later a cold little message lands in your inbox: not delivered. Nothing is broken and you did not do anything wrong. You just ran into the three quiet rules of email attachments that nobody prints on the box.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - providers change limits and your account's help page is the final word. The mechanics below are how email attachments work everywhere; the exact megabyte numbers are what the providers publish today.

The 25 MB limit is not what you think it is

Rule one: the limit covers the whole message, not the file. Gmail's 25 MB cap counts the headers, your written message, your signature, and every attachment combined - the envelope, not just what you picked in the file picker. One guide to Gmail's limit puts it plainly: the maximum applies to the entire email including the encoded attachments, so the usable room for your file is always a little less than 25 MB.

Rule two: your file gets bigger on the way out. Email was built for text, so attachments are converted into a text-safe encoding before they travel, and that conversion inflates them by about a third. A 20 MB file becomes roughly 26 to 27 MB in transit. Do the math and the mystery dissolves: your "under the limit" file was over the limit the whole time. The practical rule of thumb from the same guide: multiply your file size by 1.33. If the result is still under your provider's cap, it can travel as an attachment. If not, it cannot - and no amount of re-sending will change that.

This is also why several smaller files can gang up on you. Three 8 MB photos are 24 MB of files, which is about 32 MB of actual message - over the cap, even though every single photo "fits." (Our general bounces article covers the other reasons mail comes back - bad addresses, spam filters, reputation problems. This article is about the one bounce cause that is purely about size.)

The customer on the other end gets a vote

Rule three: the recipient's server has its own limit, and the smaller of the two limits wins. Your mail can be perfectly fine on your side and still bounce because the customer's server only accepts, say, 10 MB messages. You will never see their limit; you only see the bounce. So when a file goes through to one customer and bounces to another, that is not randomness - it is two different mailboxes with two different ceilings. Keep this in mind before you blame your own setup: if it only fails with one recipient, the problem is almost certainly on their end, and the fix is the same link-instead-of-attach move described below.

A related limit with a similar shape: Gmail also caps how many messages you can send per day. That is the quantity quota; this article is the size quota. Our 500-emails article covers the count side if you hit that wall instead.

The real numbers, by provider

Here is what the major providers publish as of 2026. Remember the 1.33 multiplier - the "effective file" column is what your file can actually be on disk before encoding pushes it over:

Gmail (personal): 25 MB to send, 50 MB to receive. Files over the send limit automatically become a Google Drive link instead of an attachment.

Outlook.com / Hotmail: 20 MB per attachment, 25 MB total per message. Oversized files become a OneDrive link. The personal limit is fixed - it cannot be raised.

Microsoft 365 (business): 35 MB to send, 36 MB to receive by default; your IT person can raise it as high as 150 MB.

Yahoo Mail: 25 MB per message.

iCloud Mail: 20 MB, but its Mail Drop feature sends files up to 5 GB as a download link that expires after 30 days.

Email that came with your hosting plan: this is the wild card. Hosting-bundled email usually runs on older-style setups, and the desktop email programs people use with them commonly cap out around 20 MB. Check your host's help docs for the real number instead of assuming Gmail's. (Our hosting-email article walks through when that free email is fine and when it is costing you.)

Why zipping sometimes does nothing

The classic advice is "zip it," and for some files it works beautifully: Word documents, spreadsheets, and PDFs can shrink to a fraction of their size. But photos and videos are already compressed by the camera. Zipping a 20 MB phone photo usually saves almost nothing, because there is nothing left to squeeze out. If your attachment is photos, the real fix is resizing, not zipping: a phone photo shrunk to a normal viewing size drops from megabytes to hundreds of kilobytes, and on a customer's screen it looks identical. For documents, zip away. For photos, resize. For video, do not even try - upload it somewhere and send the link.

The link is the actual answer

Every provider has now built the escape hatch directly into the compose window, because this problem is that common. Attach something too big in Gmail and it offers to upload it to Google Drive and put a download link in the email (free Drive storage is 15 GB, which is room for an enormous number of contracts and photo sets). Outlook does the same with OneDrive. iCloud's Mail Drop handles up to 5 GB. And the dedicated transfer services exist for the cases the providers do not cover.

The link has a second advantage nobody mentions: it dodges the recipient's limit too. A link is a tiny message with a tiny body, so the customer's 10 MB ceiling never comes into play. It also keeps your mailbox slimmer - every giant file you send and receive sits in your mailbox eating your storage quota, which is one more reason our email backup article exists: big files deserve to live somewhere built for files, not inside your inbox.

The routine that ends this problem

Five habits, none of them technical:

1. Treat 10 MB as your real limit. One published 2026 roundup of every provider's limits lands on the same practical rule: keep attachments under about 15 to 18 MB, and under 10 MB when you do not know the recipient's setup. Ten megabytes of file clears every major provider's ceiling with room to spare.

2. Resize phone photos before attaching. Your phone's share menu usually offers a smaller size. Use it. The customer cannot tell the difference, and a photo set that was 40 MB becomes 4.

3. One or two files per email, not ten. Files share one envelope, so a stack of small photos can trip the limit together. Split big sets across emails, or better, link them.

4. Link anything over about 15 MB. Upload it to Drive, OneDrive, or Dropbox and paste the link. It is faster to send, impossible to bounce on size, and the customer gets a proper download button.

5. After sending something big, ask once: "Did you get it?" A two-second confirmation beats discovering on Friday that Monday's contract never arrived. (And if a website contact form is the thing not delivering, that is a different machine - our contact-form article covers that one.)

One last distinction for the worried: a size bounce is not a reputation problem. If Gmail is rejecting your mail for other reasons - authentication, spam complaints, sending patterns - that is covered in our Gmail-rejection article. Size bounces say "too big," plainly, every time. Fix the size and they stop.
