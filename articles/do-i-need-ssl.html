Do I need SSL for a small business website?

Your site is just a brochure — nobody is typing card numbers into it. So do you actually need SSL, the padlock encryption thing? The short answer is yes, and not for the reason most people tell you. Here is the honest version.

SSL is the encryption behind the padlock — it runs on a certificate, a digital ID card for your domain, that turns your plain http address into https. You do not need to understand the cryptography. You need to know when it is required and what happens if you skip it.

The three cases where it is not optional

1. You collect anything on a form. A name, an email, a phone number — anything. Since 2018, Chrome labels every page without encryption as “Not secure” in plain text next to the address, on every page, not just checkout pages. The visitor filling in your quote form sees that flag and most of them stop typing. It is not that encryption is technically required for a contact form; it is that the warning is automatic and you cannot turn it off for your visitors.

2. You take payments on the site. If a card number is ever typed on your pages, the payment-industry rules require that data to travel encrypted — that is not a recommendation, it is a compliance requirement. If your checkout hands off to Stripe or Square, the handoff page still needs to be secure.

3. You want Google to take you seriously. Google has treated HTTPS as a ranking signal since 2014 — a light one, not a magic switch, but real. And there is a quieter effect: Google’s own tools grade unencrypted pages down, and an unencrypted page can never reach the top grades on any reputable security or performance scan, which is what sophisticated partners and customers increasingly check.

“But my site is just a brochure”

This is the most common objection, and here is the honest answer: even a pure brochure site benefits, because the certificate is free and the warning is automatic. There is no trade-off to weigh anymore — it is not a paid upsell, it is a checkbox. The brochure sites that still lack it are not saving money; they are running with a “Not secure” flag on every page and usually just never had someone tick the box.

What it costs in 2026

The standard certificates are free — providers like Let’s Encrypt issue them at no cost, valid for 90 days at a time, and most reputable hosts offer them as a one-click toggle that also handles the renewals. Paid certificates exist, but for a standard small-business site they buy you nothing the free one does not. If your host wants to charge you a monthly fee for basic SSL, that is the kind of line item our audit cost guide warns about — it is worth a second look at what you are actually paying for.
