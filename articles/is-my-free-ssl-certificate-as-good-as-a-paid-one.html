Is My Free SSL Certificate As Good As a Paid One?

The short answer: for the encryption itself, yes — identical. A free certificate scrambles the connection between your visitor and your site exactly as well as a paid one, and your visitors see the exact same padlock. What the money buys is everything around the encryption: an insurance policy, a human support team, certificates that last a year instead of three months, and three levels of identity checking. Here is what each of those is worth — and the honest ladder for when free is fine and when it is not.

1. What a certificate actually does (and what it doesn't)

A certificate does one job: it creates a private, scrambled connection between your visitor's browser and your website, so nobody on the coffee-shop wifi can read what passes between them. It is a padlock on the pipe. It is not a stamp on your business. If you haven't yet settled whether you need one at all, that question has its own article — this one picks up after you already have the free one installed and someone is telling you it isn't "real."

The free kind almost always comes from Let's Encrypt, a nonprofit certificate authority backed by big tech companies. Your host probably installed it for you automatically — most managed hosts (SiteGround, WP Engine, Kinsta, Hostinger) bundle Let's Encrypt with auto-renewal at no extra cost (sslinsights.com, 2026). The paid kind comes from commercial companies like DigiCert, Sectigo, or GlobalSign. Same pipe. Same padlock. What that padlock means to your visitor doesn't change with the price tag.

2. The three levels, in plain English

Certificates come in three validation levels — three answers to "who does this certificate belong to?"

Level one: DV — "I control this domain." Domain Validation. The certificate authority checks that whoever asked for the certificate actually controls the website address — usually by email or a small file placed on the site. That is all it proves: this certificate belongs to whoever runs acmeplumbing.com, not to Acme Plumbing the registered business. Free certificates are DV only. For the pipe-encryption job, DV is all anyone needs.

Level two: OV — "This is a real registered business." Organization Validation. The authority checks the domain plus the company's legal existence — business registration, address, phone. The company's name goes inside the certificate's details. Only paid certificates offer this.

Level three: EV — "We ran a full background check." Extended Validation. The deepest vetting: legal existence, operational existence, physical address, authority of the person requesting it. EV certificates are paid, cost the most, and come with the biggest warranties. And here is the twist — the main thing EV ever bought you is gone.

3. The twist: browsers stopped showing the difference

EV's big selling point used to be the green bar: browsers displayed the company's verified name right in the address bar, so visitors could see they were dealing with the real bank, not a lookalike. Then the browsers killed it. Google's Chrome 77, released in September 2019, removed the company name from the address bar entirely and buried it behind a click on the padlock; Firefox 70 followed weeks later, and Apple's Safari had dropped it earlier (bleepingcomputer.com; computerworld.com; zdnet.com; dotkeeper.com).

Google's reason was blunt: its security team found that the EV indicator "does not protect users as intended" — users did not make safer choices when the indicator was there versus when it wasn't. Security researcher Troy Hunt summed up the industry mood in August 2019: "EV is now really, really dead." So the most expensive certificate's headline feature — the visible company name — is now something visitors only see if they click the padlock and go looking. On phones, where most of your customers are, it might as well not exist.

4. What the money actually buys — the honest list

A warranty: $10,000 to $2,000,000. Paid certificates come with an insurance policy from the certificate authority. In plain English: if the authority itself messes up — issues a certificate to a fraudster who then impersonates your site, or a flaw in their systems causes you losses — the warranty pays out. Free certificates carry no warranty at all. Be clear-eyed about what this is: it is insurance against the authority's mistakes, not insurance for your business. It matters to contracts and cyber-insurance policies; it matters to almost nothing else (9mood.com; sslinsights.com, 2026).

A human support team. Paid certificates come with the company's support staff — phone, chat, someone who fixes a botched installation. Free certificates come with documentation and community forums: smart people, but nobody whose job it is to answer you. If you have a web person who handles the technical side, you will never miss this. If you manage the site yourself, it is the one paid feature you might actually use.

Certificates that last longer. Free certificates live about 90 days and renew themselves automatically — that is by design, and the lifetimes are reportedly getting shorter (one October 2026 comparison reports Let's Encrypt has announced 64-day certificates from February 2027 and 45-day from February 2028 — reported plans, worth knowing because they make reliable auto-renewal more important every year). Paid certificates last up to about a year per issuance. Longer life mostly means fewer renewal events to go wrong — but automatic renewal, when it works, makes the difference moot. The renewal mechanics have their own article if yours keeps lapsing.

The extras shelf: site seals, easier wildcards, the org name in the details. Paid certificates often include a trust seal — the little badge some sites display. Free certificates can cover subdomains (wildcards) but need more technical setup; paid wildcards are simpler. And the OV/EV organization name sits inside the certificate details — visible, again, only to visitors who click the padlock and look (spyboy.blog, Oct 2026; eukhost.com).

What the money does NOT buy: stronger encryption. Every comparison says the same thing — free certificates provide "the same 256-bit TLS encryption as any paid certificate" (sslinsights.com, 2026). Anyone selling you a paid certificate on the grounds that it "encrypts better" is selling you fiction.

5. The honest ladder: when free is fine, when it isn't

Free is fine if: your site is a brochure, a blog, a portfolio, or a booking/info site with no payments and no logins — which is most small-business sites. The padlock works, Google treats the HTTPS the same, visitors see no difference. This is also what GoDaddy's own 2026 guide says: free certificates are "ideal for personal websites or small businesses that require standard encryption."

Consider paid DV or OV if: customers pay you directly on the site, or log in with accounts and stored data. Not because the encryption is better — because the warranty, the support line, and (for OV) the verified business identity match what a payments-handling business should carry. GoDaddy's guide again: paid fits "larger businesses or ecommerce sites where enhanced security and customer trust are paramount."

Paid OV/EV is genuinely required if: a compliance framework (PCI-DSS, HIPAA, enterprise procurement) says so, or a contract or cyber-insurance policy demands a warranty above $10,000. At that point it is not a choice — it is paperwork (sslinsights.com, 2026).

The gotcha to check first: free only works if auto-renewal works — a free certificate that fails to renew gives your visitors a scary expired-certificate warning. And one thing worth knowing: some web hosts deliberately block free certificates so they can sell you their own (eukhost.com). If your host tells you the free option "isn't available," ask whether that is a technical limit or a sales strategy. The host/owner split covers where the host's responsibilities end and yours begin. One more distinction: if a visitor's browser says "not secure" on a page that has a certificate, that is usually a different problem — mixed content — and that article walks through it.

6. What to tell your web person (the exact words)

Send them this: "Someone told me our free SSL certificate isn't good enough and we should buy one. Five asks: first, what certificate do we actually have right now and who issued it? Second, is auto-renewal turned on, and has it renewed successfully at least once — show me. Third, quote me a paid DV and a paid OV for one year, and tell me in one sentence what each buys us that we don't have. Fourth, does our host allow free certificates, or does it block them to sell its own? Fifth, whatever we decide, verify the padlock is showing on every page afterward." Five asks, and the sales pitch turns into a decision with numbers on it.
