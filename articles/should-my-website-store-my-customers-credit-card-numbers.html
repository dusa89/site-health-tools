Should My Website Store My Customers' Credit Card Numbers?

The honest answer: no - do not store card numbers on your website. Let the payment processor hold them, and keep a token instead. This is not about being careful with a database. The moment your website holds actual card numbers, you sign yourself up for the card industry's heaviest compliance paperwork (a 250-question self-assessment), plus the promise to encrypt, guard, and audit those numbers forever. There is a way to get everything you wanted from storing them - faster checkout, subscriptions, card-on-file convenience - without holding a single number: tokenization. Your site keeps a worthless placeholder; the processor keeps the card. Below: what the card brands require you to promise, the two forms that decide how much paperwork you face, how tokenization works in plain English, the one catch that keeps you fully in scope anyway, and the three traps - including the spreadsheet.

1. This is a contract problem, not just a security problem

Start with the frame most guides skip. PCI DSS - the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard - is not a law. It is a promise you made without reading it: your merchant agreement with your processor or acquiring bank requires you to follow it, and the card brands enforce it through that chain (smallbizhandbook.com, updated 4 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). Being small does not exempt you. It only changes how you prove compliance - a small merchant files a self-assessment questionnaire instead of hiring an outside auditor, but the underlying rules are the same. So the question is not "can I be careful enough." The question is "which form am I signing up for." That turns out to be the whole decision, because the forms are wildly different sizes.

2. The two forms - 27 questions vs 250

How you handle cards decides which Self-Assessment Questionnaire you file each year. If the payment is fully outsourced - the customer pays on a hosted checkout page or an embedded secure form, and your systems never touch card data - you file SAQ A: 27 requirements, one of the shortest forms there is (smallbizhandbook.com; crawled 2026-10-11). If your website stores card numbers electronically and you do not fit another category, you file SAQ D: 250 requirements (smallbizhandbook.com; crawled 2026-10-11). One guide for small merchants puts it bluntly: SAQ D is "everything else, including anyone storing cardholder data. Hundreds of questions. Try not to end up here" (equaltechsolutions.com, Sep 2026; crawled 2026-10-11). That is the price of holding the numbers: not just encryption, but documented encryption, key management separated from the encrypted data, minimum-necessary storage with a written business reason, access controls, audits - hundreds of commitments, renewed every year (cyberphore.com, updated ~79 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). Nobody who wants to run a business rather than a compliance department chooses this voluntarily.

3. What you actually wanted - and the answer that gives it to you

Nobody stores card numbers for fun. You wanted something specific: faster repeat checkout, "keep my card on file," subscriptions that charge monthly, the convenience your customers expect from big stores. All of that is achievable without holding a single card number. The answer is tokenization: when a customer pays, the processor returns a token - a stand-in string with no value of its own - and your website stores that. When it is time to charge again, your site hands the token to the processor, which holds the real card in its own vault and runs the charge (xplorpay.com, updated 4 days ago; shift4.com; crawled 2026-10-11). Think of it like a coat check: you hand over the coat (the card number), they hand you a ticket (the token). The ticket gets you your coat back, but if someone steals the ticket, all they have is paper. Subscriptions, one-click checkout, card-on-file - the modern versions of all three run on tokens, not on stored numbers. And the same never-touch-the-numbers logic decides what your host's backups are actually keeping - a backup of a database full of card numbers is a second vault you did not mean to build.

4. The catch that keeps you in scope even when you tokenize

Here is the line people miss. Tokenization only shrinks your compliance burden if the card number never touches your servers - the customer types it into a hosted checkout page, a client-side script sends it straight to the processor, or an embedded secure iframe collects it. Do it that way and you stay on the short form. But if the raw card number reaches your website's backend even briefly - a form that posts to your server first, a log file that catches the field, an email notification that includes it - you remain fully in PCI scope, even if you tokenize the number right after (medium.com @shejwalhrishikesh, updated ~193 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). The rule is about what your systems touch, not what they keep. This is the question to put to your web person in section 8: "show me the exact path a card number takes from the customer's keyboard to the processor, and prove it never lands on our server." If they cannot draw that path, you are on the long form until they can.

5. The money side - what noncompliance actually costs

The card brands may fine the acquiring bank $5,000 to $100,000 per month for compliance violations - and the bank passes that cost down the chain until it lands on the merchant (refreshtech.com, updated 6 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). Before any breach, plain noncompliance fees can start at $10-$100 a month and grow the longer you stay noncompliant (securityjourney.com, ~345 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). After a breach involving stored numbers, remediation runs from a few thousand dollars to $500,000 depending on severity and how many customers are affected, and a forensic investigation alone can cost $50,000 to over $500,000 (securityjourney.com; crawled 2026-10-11). And the targeting is not what people expect: over 90% of card-data compromises hit the smallest merchants (Level 4), with 91% coming through always-on internet connections - in other words, the standard small-business setup - a website living on an always-on connection (Trustwave figures via optimizedpayments.com; crawled 2026-10-11). The numbers argue for the same architecture twice: don't be the vault; don't even be the hallway the numbers walk through.

6. The three traps

Trap one: the spreadsheet (and the CRM, and the email). Card numbers do not only live in databases. A phone order typed into a spreadsheet, an invoice email with the full number in the body, a CRM note with "card on file" - each one is stored cardholder data, and each one pulls its container into PCI scope (the paper and side-channel controls live under Requirement 9.4 and the retention rules under 3.2.1; pcidssguide.com, updated ~14 days ago; crawled 2026-10-11). If your team handles cards by phone, they should key them into a hosted virtual terminal - not write them down. Trap two: "we encrypt it, so we're fine." Encrypting a database column is one of hundreds of SAQ D requirements, not a substitute for them - key management, access logging, who can decrypt, rotation, documented business need. Encryption without the rest is a lock on a screen door. Trap three: "the plugin handles it." Payment plugins collect card details in many shapes - some use hosted fields or iframes (short form), some post the data through your server first (long form). The plugin's marketing page rarely spells out which. Ask the path question from section 4 and get the answer in writing. (When it has already gone wrong, the card-breach article is the response map.)

7. The quick verdict

Do not store your customers' card numbers on your website. Store tokens, issued by your payment processor, and let the processor run the vault. The architecture to demand: the card number travels from the customer's keyboard straight to the processor (hosted page, client-side tokenization, or secure iframe), never lands on your server, and your site keeps only the token - which keeps you on the 27-question SAQ A instead of the 250-question SAQ D. The subscription and repeat-checkout features you want all run on tokens. The one thing never allowed, ever: the CVV, magnetic-stripe data, or PIN may not be stored after authorization under any circumstances (cyberphore.com; crawled 2026-10-11). The other security decisions around this one - whether a firewall is worth it, customer accounts, and who actually owns the security decision - all get simpler once the numbers are out of your hands.

8. What to tell your web person

Send your assistant or web person this: "I don't want our website to store customer card numbers anywhere - not in the database, not in a spreadsheet, not in email, not in the CRM. Four asks. First, draw me the exact path a card number takes from the customer's keyboard to the payment processor, and confirm it never lands on our server - hosted checkout page, client-side tokenization, or a secure embedded form. Second, replace any stored card numbers with processor tokens for repeat billing and subscriptions, and delete the real numbers once the tokens work. Third, confirm nothing else captures card data along the way - no form emails with the number, no logging the field, no phone orders written down. Fourth, tell me which PCI self-assessment form we file now (it should be SAQ A, the short one) and show me the confirmation in writing. Prove it with a test: run a test charge while watching the server logs, and show me the card number never appears. If anything along the path touches the raw number, redesign the path before the next real order." The proof is a test charge with server logs that contain no card number - and a short form, not a long one. (And if your web person is the person who set this up in the first place, the passwords article and the web-person article cover the access side.)
