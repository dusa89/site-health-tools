Does my Google Business Profile decide what AI says about my business?

Ask an AI assistant “who is a good plumber near me?” and it answers with confidence: names, services, hours, ratings. Now ask where it got those facts. The uncomfortable answer, backed by 2026 research on nearly two million citations: more often than not, it read your Google Business Profile — not your website.

Try it as the experiment it is. Open an AI assistant and ask the question a real customer asks — your trade, your town, phrased the way a person would phrase it. It names a few businesses and gives each one a tidy summary: what they do, when they are open, what customers say. Now compare that summary against your own business. The facts it got right — and the ones it got wrong — almost certainly came from one place: your Google Business Profile. Here is why, in the data’s own words.

The number-one source, by the numbers

BrightLocal’s 2026 local-AI citation study ran 60,970 checks — five prompts at nine map points for each of 1,355 business locations across the US, UK, and Australia — on Google AI Overviews, Google AI Mode, and ChatGPT. It tracked roughly 1.9 million citations. The Google Business Profile was the single most-cited source at 28.63 percent of all citations (563,272 of them). Yelp came second at 9.53 percent, then Facebook at 2.23 percent and TripAdvisor at 1.79 percent. The researchers found 415 different directories and listing sites being used as sources to some degree. And the bluntest line in the findings: Google used the Business Profile for virtually every location. For Google’s own AI products, your profile is the default answer sheet. (Sources: BrightLocal’s 2026 local AI visibility study and its companion local AI sources research.)

Why the profile beats your website

Four reasons, none of them flattering to web designers. First, the profile is a complete fact file in one place: services, hours, photos, reviews, address — everything an AI needs to describe you, already structured. Second, corroboration: a claim on your own website is just a claim, but the same fact on your profile, confirmed by a dozen customer reviews, is evidence — and AI assistants visibly prefer sources they can treat as evidence. Third, your profile is written in a format the machine can lift verbatim, while your homepage is written in a format humans enjoy. Fourth, Google’s own assistants have the easiest time reading Google’s own data. None of this means your website is useless: a separate 53,900-page study by Synup found that 45 to 61 percent of AI Overview citations point at business websites, parent-brand websites, or Business Profiles, and Google’s own guidance still requires a page to be indexed before it can appear as a supporting web link. The correct model is additive: the website owns the canonical facts, the profile owns the conversation — and when they disagree, the profile usually wins.

The five fields that do the talking

1. Services, written like answers. Every service you list has a description field. One practitioner guide notes that Gemini can quote 150-to-300-word service descriptions directly — so write what the service involves, who it is for, and what the process looks like, not keyword salad. Your services list is the menu the AI reads from.

2. Photos and weekly posts. A photo of recent work with a sentence about what you did and where, once a week. Not for the humans who browse your profile — as a freshness signal. Consistent activity tells the AI the business is alive and operating; a profile frozen since 2023 reads like a rumor of a business.

3. Reviews, and your replies to them. Review text is citable evidence: detailed reviews describing real work (“replaced our water heater the same day”) get quoted more than generic praise. And every reply you write is more text attached to your name that the AI can read — thank-yous with specific details beat copy-paste lines. Our reviews guide covers the full playbook for keeping them coming.

4. Hours — exact, and honest about holidays. When the AI tells a customer “they close at 5,” it read your hours. If they are wrong, you lost a job to a profile you forgot to update. Special hours for holidays and closures are part of the same field; treat them like a promise, because the AI treats them like one.

5. The Q&A section and attributes. Those little detail tags — wheelchair accessible, appointment required, online estimates — are direct answer fodder. And the Q&A section lets you plant the exact questions customers ask, answered in your own words.

The traps that hand your facts to someone else

An unclaimed or duplicate profile. If you never claimed yours, anyone’s edits can steer it — and if a duplicate exists, the AI may read the wrong twin. Claim it, merge the duplicates.

The wrong primary category. “Electrician” versus “Electrical contractor” versus “Handyman” changes which questions you get named for. Pick the category your customers actually search.

A profile that disagrees with your website. Different phone number on the site than the profile, old address on Yelp, services listed in one place and not the other — conflicting facts make the AI unsure you are one business at all. Name, address, and phone: identical everywhere.

The tempting shortcut. Keyword-stuffing your business name (“Mike’s Plumbing — Best Plumber in Las Vegas 24/7 Cheap”) is against Google’s policy and can get the whole profile suspended — and with it, your single biggest AI source. Your real name is the safe one.

The honest check you can run today

Open your Google Business Profile and read it like a stranger who has never met you. Could that stranger say what you do, where you work, when you are open, and why customers trust you? Now fill the five fields above — the whole job is about two hours, and it costs nothing. Then ask the AI assistant the customer’s question and note which facts it repeats. One caveat: think in months, not days. AI training data updates slowly, so the facts you fix today surface over time — but every one of these fixes also helps your Google ranking today, so none of the work is wasted either way.
