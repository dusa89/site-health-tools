Should I send my newsletter from my business email?

Your newsletter and your quotes share one domain reputation — and the newsletter is spending it. Three spam complaints per thousand recipients can sink your standing with Gmail. Here is why one address for everything backfires, in numbers, and the 4-step split that fixes it.

It usually happens like this. You build a customer list over the years — a few hundred names, maybe a thousand. Then you send a promotion: a holiday sale, a new service announcement, a monthly newsletter. You send it from the same address you always use, info@yourbusiness.com, because that is your business email and it feels personal. Open rates look fine. Then, two months later, a customer calls: “Hey, did you ever send that quote?” You did. It is sitting in their spam folder. And so are your last three invoices.

One address for everything poisons the well

Here is the part nobody tells small business owners. Gmail, Outlook, and Yahoo do not score your intentions — they score your domain. Every email sent from yourbusiness.com feeds one reputation score: the newsletter, the promo blast, the quote, the invoice, the appointment reminder. Marketing email is the risky kind — it gets ignored, deleted unread, and marked as spam, because that is what people do with bulk mail even when they once signed up for it. Transactional email — quotes, invoices, replies — is the kind that has to land. When both come from the same domain, the risky mail spends the reputation the important mail needs.

Think of it like a credit score for your domain. Every spam complaint is a late payment. Every blast to a stale list is maxing out a card. Your quotes and invoices are the mortgage application — and they are being judged on the score your newsletter built.

The real numbers

The rules got teeth in 2024. Google announced in October 2023 that, starting February 2024, anyone sending 5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail accounts must authenticate with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, offer one-click unsubscribe, and — the number that matters most — keep the spam complaint rate under 0.10%, and never let it reach 0.30%. That is Google’s own sender guidelines, the actual rulebook, not a best-practice blog post. At 1,000 recipients, a 0.3% complaint rate is three people clicking “report spam.” Three. Your domain’s reputation with the world’s largest mailbox provider can hinge on three clicks.

And the background failure rate is higher than owners assume. Validity’s 2024 email deliverability benchmark measured global inbox placement around 85% — meaning roughly 1 in 7 legitimate marketing emails never reaches the inbox at all, filtered to spam or gone missing. (Validity sells deliverability tooling, so read the exact figure as directional — but the direction is consistent across years: a meaningful share of real business email never arrives.) Your promo blast is already swimming upstream. Sending it from the same domain as your quotes drags them into the same current.

The mailbox providers are also watching what recipients do with your mail. Mail that gets opened and replied to builds reputation; mail that gets deleted unread or ignored teaches the filter your mail is unwanted. A newsletter blasted to a list you have not cleaned in three years — dead addresses, people who forgot they signed up — generates bounces and silence, and both count against the domain your invoices travel on.

The 4-step split

1. Split the streams. This is the single biggest fix. Keep your main domain — info@yourbusiness.com — for one-to-one mail: quotes, invoices, replies, appointment confirmations. Send bulk mail (newsletters, promos, announcements) from a subdomain like news.yourbusiness.com. The subdomain builds its own reputation, quarantined away from the address your money travels on. Most email platforms set this up in an afternoon, or our free email check will show you how your domain currently looks to the filters.

2. Authenticate both streams. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are the three records that prove your mail is really from you — and Google now requires all three for bulk senders. Authentication alone will not save a burned reputation, but without it you are starting every send at a disadvantage. Our guide on what DMARC actually is walks through it in plain English.

3. Clean the list before you send. Remove addresses that bounced, people who have not opened anything in a year, and anyone you cannot prove opted in. A smaller list that wants your mail beats a big list that reports it — every time. List hygiene is unglamorous and it is the highest-return email work most businesses ever do.

4. Send mail people open. This sounds obvious and almost nobody does it. One genuinely useful email a month beats four “just checking in” blasts. Engagement — opens, replies, clicks — is reputation fuel. Every ignored blast is reputation burned. If you would not open it, do not send it from your business’s name.

One reframe to carry with you: your domain’s email reputation is an asset, like your Google review score. Your newsletter is allowed to spend it — that is what it is for — but never from the same account your quotes and invoices depend on. If customers have already told you they never got your quote, or your mail suddenly started landing in spam, a burned shared reputation is the first suspect — and a blacklist check is the second.
