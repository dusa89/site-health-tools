Do Those Pretty Fonts Slow Down My Website?

You paid for a beautiful redesign — the new typeface looks expensive, and then your speed score fell off a cliff. The photos didn’t change. The hosting didn’t change. The fonts did, and nobody told you fonts have a loading cost. Here is what those pretty typefaces are doing behind your back, the trap that makes text invisible while they load, and the fixes — starting with a one-parameter change — that keep the beauty and get the speed back.

First, the distinction this guide is built on: your theme and your fonts are not the same bill. The theme is the whole package — the template, the builder, its own scripts (we have a whole guide on whether your theme makes your site slow). The fonts are the typefaces inside it — and they have their own download mechanics, their own connection to a second company’s servers, and their own nasty trap that the theme guides never mention: text that refuses to show up at all. This guide is the font side.

The short answer: yes — fonts are software files your visitor’s phone must download before the words can appear. Every font family and every weight of it is another download, plus a round trip to Google’s servers. A typical business site loads far more typeface than it actually uses, and the default settings make the text invisible while it all arrives. Two of the five fixes take about a minute and the theme never has to change.

What a font costs your site

Forget “font” for a minute. Think files. When your page uses a typeface like Montserrat, the browser downloads the actual letters — and it downloads them separately for every weight you asked for. Regular, medium, semi-bold, bold: that is four files, each roughly 15–30 KB in the modern compressed format. Four families at four weights each is sixteen files and a few hundred kilobytes, all before your headline can render.

Now the part nobody warns you about: if those fonts come from Google Fonts — and on small-business sites they almost always do — your visitor’s phone has to make friends with Google’s servers before anything moves. (A photo is one big download from your own server — we have a whole guide on photos; fonts are many small files from someone else’s server. Different bill.) First it looks up the server (DNS), shakes hands (TCP), and sets up encryption (TLS) — twice, once for the server that hands out the instructions (fonts.googleapis.com) and once for the server that holds the actual font files (fonts.gstatic.com). That connection setup runs about 100–250 ms per server even when everything else is optimized, and it happens in a waterfall: the page fetches the instructions, and only after they arrive does it request the font files themselves. The fonts are the last thing in line, but they gate the most important thing on the page: the words.

The invisible-text trap. Here is the nasty part. By default, while the font files are downloading, the browser shows nothing where your text should be — a behavior with the lovely name FOIT, flash of invisible text. Your visitor sees your headline’s layout, your photo, your buttons… and a blank space where the words go. On a slow phone that blank can last a second or more. Your competitor whose text just appears in a plain system font won the customer while your page was still waiting to be pretty.

The 5 fixes, in priority order

1 — Trim the weights (the biggest win, no developer needed). Open your site’s theme settings and look for anything about font weights — many themes load every weight from 100 to 900 by default, even though your design only uses regular and bold. Turn off the weights you don’t use. Two or three weights per family is the expert ceiling; most business sites never need more than regular and bold. This one change can delete half a dozen font files from every single page load.

2 — Add display=swap (sixty seconds, kills the invisible text). Find where your site loads Google Fonts — in the theme settings, a header script, or the code the developer pasted — and add &display=swap to the end of the font URL. It tells the browser: paint the text immediately in a plain system font, then swap in the pretty font when it arrives. The words show at first paint instead of blank space. Yes, there is a brief moment where the text re-renders in the real font (the flash of unstyled text) — readable words that shift slightly beat invisible words every single time.

3 — Preconnect (sixty seconds, warms the handshake). Paste these two lines near the top of your page’s head, before the font link: <link rel="preconnect" href="https://fonts.googleapis.com"> and <link rel="preconnect" href="https://fonts.gstatic.com" crossorigin>. They tell the browser to start the connection handshake with Google’s servers before it needs the fonts, so the wait happens in the background. About a hundred to three hundred milliseconds saved — on a phone, that’s felt.

4 — Cut to two families, max (the discipline fix). Every extra font family is a whole new download set plus its own handshake chain. Headlines get one, body text gets one, everything else uses those two. The logo can keep its special treatment — but the third accent font for subheadings is paying a speed tax on every page for styling nobody’s brand depends on.

5 — Self-host, or go variable (the developer fix). If the fonts are doing real brand work, the expert move is hosting the font files on your own site instead of Google’s — the double handshake disappears entirely. The best version of this is a variable font: one file that covers the whole weight range instead of four separate files. Honest note: this is developer territory — ask your developer to quote it, or weigh it against the 90% of the win that steps 1 through 4 give you for free.

What not to do

Don’t preload every font. Preload is a hint that says “this file is critical, grab it first” — and it only works when you mean it. Slap preload on six font files and the browser has to juggle them against your images and scripts; the one file that actually matters loses its priority. Preload at most one or two, usually the body-text font.

Don’t leave the default loading behavior on. That default is the invisible-text setting. If your developer or theme never set display=swap or the equivalent, your text is waiting on font files it could have skipped. This is the setting behind “my site feels slower than the test says” — the numbers don’t show blank text, but visitors see it.

Don’t confuse “my theme is heavy” with “my fonts are heavy.” A slow theme (see the theme guide) loads its own scripts and layout machinery. Fonts are passengers inside it. If the redesign looked the same but felt slower after a font change, the culprit is almost always the fonts — the weights, the families, or the missing swap — not the theme that’s been there for years.

Don’t solve this by deleting your brand fonts. The answer is never “just use the plain font.” Trim the weights, set the swap, preconnect the servers — keep the typeface that makes your site yours. A business site with no brand voice is a different problem than a slow one, and steps 1–3 above close most of the gap without touching the look.

The bottom line

Your fonts are files, and files have a loading cost — weights you never use, a handshake with Google’s servers on every visit, and a default that renders your text invisible while it all arrives. Cut the weights to what you use, add display=swap, preconnect the two font servers, hold the line at two families — that’s an hour’s work with no developer, and the invisible text is gone in the first sixty seconds. The typeface stays. The wait doesn’t.
