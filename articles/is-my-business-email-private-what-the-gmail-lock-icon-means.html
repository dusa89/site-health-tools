Is My Business Email Private? What the Lock Icon in Gmail Really Means

You have probably seen the little lock icon in Gmail and assumed it meant your email was private. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it means something much narrower. And sometimes the lock is red and hanging open, which is Gmail's way of tapping you on the shoulder. Here is what each version actually promises — and the one thing none of them promise.

All facts below are reported from the cited sources, checked 2026-10-12 - not promises about your situation. Email setups differ, and providers change details over time. The general picture here is stable; the specifics of your account are worth checking with your email provider or web person.

The decoder ring: grey, green, and red

When you compose a message in Gmail, look next to the recipient's name. On a message you received, the icon sits up in the message header area — click it and Gmail tells you more. There are three versions, and they answer one question: was this message scrambled while it traveled?

Grey lock: scrambled on the road. The message is protected by TLS — encryption applied while the email moves between mail servers. This is the normal, everyday case for most business email. It does not mean nobody can ever read it (more on that below). It means nobody listening in along the way could read it.

Green lock: the strongest version. This means S/MIME encryption is active — the message is locked so that only the recipient's device can open it. You will only see this on a paid Google Workspace account where the extra encryption has been set up on both sides. Most small businesses never see the green lock, and that is normal.

Red open lock: traveling in the open. The other side's mail system does not do encryption, so the message goes as readable text. Google added this warning back in February 2016, and its advice then is still the right advice now: if you see the red lock while writing, do not send anything confidential — tax forms, contracts, bank details — to that address. If you see it on a message you received, that message arrived unencrypted; there is usually nothing you can do about it after the fact, but you now know not to reply with anything sensitive. The red lock is uncommon in practice, because the big providers (Microsoft, Yahoo, and the rest) all support encryption — which is exactly why you should pay attention on the rare occasion you see it.

One thing the lock never tells you: whether the sender is honest. A scammer's email can be perfectly encrypted. The lock is about the journey, not the sender. (The icon that questions the sender is the red question mark Gmail shows instead of a profile photo — our question-mark guide covers that one. Two icons, two different questions.)

What the lock actually protects: the road, not the post offices

Think of your email as a letter and the internet as the road between two post offices — yours (Gmail, Outlook, whoever hosts your business mail) and the recipient's. TLS is an armored truck on that road: while the letter is moving, it is scrambled, and anyone eavesdropping — say, someone sniffing the coffee-shop wifi — sees only gibberish.

But the armored truck only runs if both post offices use it. If the recipient's mail system does not support encryption, there is no truck, and Gmail shows you the red lock to say so. And here is the part nobody puts in the marketing: at each post office, the letter gets opened. Your provider decrypts your mail to store it, scan it for spam, and show it to you; the recipient's provider does the same on their end. So your provider and their provider can read routine email. That is not a hack or a scandal — it is how email works. The encryption protects against snoopers on the road, not against the companies carrying the mail.

This is also why encryption and authentication are two separate jobs. Proving a message really came from your domain (the SPF, DKIM, and DMARC checks) is about identity. Scrambling it in transit is about privacy. You want both, and neither replaces the other — our DMARC guide covers the identity half, and our Gmail-trust guide covers whether Gmail believes mail from your domain in the first place.

Confidential mode is a velvet rope, not a vault

Gmail's confidential mode sounds like encryption. It is not. What it does: the message expires after a time you set, you can revoke access early, and the recipient is blocked from forwarding, copying, printing, or downloading it through Gmail's normal buttons. Useful — for example, when you want a quote to stop floating around after the deal closes.

What it does not do: encrypt the message so nobody can read it. Google can still read confidential-mode mail. And every one of those forwarding restrictions is beaten by the oldest trick in the book: a photo of the screen with a phone. Use confidential mode for “please don't pass this around.” Do not use it for “nobody can ever see this.”

The real vault: S/MIME, and what it takes

There is one built-in way to get true end-to-end-style encryption inside Gmail, where not even Google can read the message: S/MIME — the green lock. But the price of admission explains why you have never seen it. You need a paid Google Workspace account (not free Gmail). Your Workspace admin has to turn S/MIME on. And both you and the recipient need valid security certificates that you have exchanged with each other — like swapping physical keys before you can use the lock.

That is a lot of setup, which is why S/MIME lives mostly in law firms, medical practices, and financial companies — businesses that routinely email contracts, tax documents, or sensitive client information and have an IT person to maintain it. If that describes your business, ask your web person about it directly (see the script below). If you mostly email quotes, invoices, and scheduling, the grey lock plus good habits is the proportionate answer — just know what the grey lock does and does not promise.

MTA-STS: telling the world “always use the armored truck”

There is one more layer, and almost nobody has it yet. MTA-STS is a small public policy your domain publishes that says: only ever deliver my mail over encrypted connections — no downgrading to the open road, ever. Think of it as DMARC's lesser-known cousin: DMARC tells the world how to handle fakes of your domain, MTA-STS tells the world to always encrypt mail bound for your domain.

Adoption is still tiny: about 0.7% of the million biggest domains published an MTA-STS policy in the January 2026 snapshot, up from 0.5% in 2025 and 0.3% in 2024. More than doubled over two years, from a small base — the direction is the point. The industry is moving toward always-encrypted mail, the way it moved toward always-encrypted websites years ago. Worth knowing it exists; worth asking your web person about when you next review your domain's DNS records (ideally in the same conversation as SPF, DKIM, and DMARC).

What to actually do

1. Respect the red lock. Before you send anything sensitive — tax forms, contracts, bank details, medical or legal documents — glance at the lock next to the recipient's name. Grey means the road is protected. Red means it is not, and the sensitive stuff should go another way (a secure portal, an encrypted file share, or a phone call).

2. Never email passwords or full account numbers, lock or no lock. The grey lock protects the road, not the post offices. Anything truly secret does not belong in email at all.

3. Lock the account door too. Two-factor authentication guards who can open your mailbox; the lock icon guards the message on its journey. Different doors — you want both. Our two-factor guide walks through turning it on.

4. Use confidential mode for what it is. Expiry dates and no-forwarding are genuinely useful for quotes, drafts, and anything you want to stop floating around. Just never mistake it for encryption.

5. Keep the identity half strong. An encrypted scam email is still a scam — our vendor-scam guide shows why you verify payment instructions by phone no matter what the icons say. And your own domain's SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records are what keep criminals from sending mail as you in the first place.

6. Back up, separately. Encryption protects mail in motion; backups protect mail at rest against loss, hacks, and accidents. Our backup guide covers that other half.

Four questions for your web person

1. “Can you confirm our business email uses encrypted connections to send and receive — and show me where I would see the red lock if it didn't?”

2. “We have SPF, DKIM, and DMARC — should we also publish an MTA-STS policy so the world always encrypts mail sent to us?”

3. “If I ever need to send truly private documents — contracts, tax forms, sensitive client data — do we have S/MIME set up, or what is our secure alternative to email?”

4. “Is two-factor turned on for every business email account, and does everyone on the team know that confidential mode expires messages but does not encrypt them?”

The bottom line

The lock icon is Gmail telling the truth in three colors: grey means the road was protected, green means the message itself is locked, red means it traveled in the open. None of them mean the sender is honest, none of them keep your provider from reading routine mail, and confidential mode is an expiry date, not a vault. Once you know what each one promises, the right move is usually simple: respect a red lock, never email secrets, and save the real vault — S/MIME — for the documents that truly need it.
