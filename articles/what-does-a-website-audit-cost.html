What does a website audit cost?

You got a quote for a website audit — or three wildly different ones — and now you're wondering what this thing should actually cost and whether anyone's telling you the truth. Here's the straight version.

The short answer: in 2026, a genuinely useful basic audit for a small business runs roughly $300 to $800, and a more comprehensive one — content, competitors, deeper technical work — runs $1,500 to $2,500. Those figures come from a July 2026 pricing guide on Medium. A separate September 2026 breakdown put freelance and small-agency audits at $500 to $2,500. Below all of that sit the free automated scans, and between them, the $0 to $300 “audit” PDFs agencies hand out. Which one you need comes down to a single question: how much actual verification is in the thing?

Why three quotes for "the same audit" can differ by 10x

“Audit” isn't a standardized product. As that July 2026 Medium guide put it, a five-minute automated scan and a hundred-hour expert-led deep dive are both sold under the same name — the real driver of cost is depth: how much manual review, competitive analysis, and strategic prioritization is actually included versus an automated export with a cover page. Three different things get sold as audits. Free automated scans — ours, Google’s PageSpeed Insights, SSL Labs, MXToolbox — real tools, real findings, but nobody prioritizes them for your business. Automated agency PDFs at $0 to $300 — a tool export with a logo on it; that September 2026 breakdown’s advice was blunt: skip it, it is a sales document. Human-verified audits — every finding checked against your live site and written so a non-developer can act on it.

What a real audit should actually include

The check count matters less than the shape of each finding: what's wrong, why it costs you customers, and what fixes it — with evidence attached. Ours is 40 checks. The areas that should never be missing: speed in seconds, not a score; email authentication — the SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records that decide whether your quotes land in spam; security — the padlock, the certificate's exact expiry date, the behind-the-scenes header instructions; mobile experience; broken links and missing images; the SEO basics — one clear headline, a real title and description; and accessibility basics — can everyone actually use the site?

Red flags — walk away when you see these

Findings with no verifiable evidence (“trust us, it's broken”). A 100-page PDF with a cover page where page 3 could belong to any business on earth. Findings with no business consequence — “missing meta keywords” in 2026 is theater. No named process for how the findings were verified — “measured, not guessed” isn't a slogan, it's the difference between a report and a sales document. And anyone who won't tell you exactly how many checks are included and what each one means before you pay.
