Why does my website say “Not secure”?

You opened your own site and there it is — “Not secure” where the padlock should be. Here is what the warning actually means, the three reasons it appears, and which one is yours.

That warning is Chrome telling your visitors one specific thing: the conversation between their browser and your site is not private. Either it was never encrypted, or the encryption has a problem. Your visitors see it — and most of them will not type their name, email, or card number into a page that carries it.

The three reasons it appears

1. No certificate at all. Your site was set up on plain http and nobody ever added the encryption certificate — the digital ID card that turns http into https. Old sites, DIY sites, and sites built on a host’s cheapest plan are the usual suspects. The fix is one certificate, usually free from your host.

2. The certificate expired. Free certificates last only 90 days, and the automatic renewal that was supposed to keep you covered quietly stopped — a changed email address, a moved DNS record, a host migration. The padlock you had on Tuesday becomes a warning on Wednesday, and you find out from a customer, not from your host. Knowing the exact expiry date is the cheapest insurance in this whole guide.

3. Mixed content. Your page itself loads over https, but something inside it — an image, a script, a video — still loads over plain http. The browser sees the hole in the armor and downgrades the whole page. This one is the sneakiest: your site looks finished, your host says everything is fine, and the warning persists because of one image file.

What it costs you

It costs you twice. First, the visitor who was about to fill in your quote form sees the warning and closes the tab — nobody hands personal details to a site their browser flagged. Second, when the certificate is expired or misconfigured, browsers can escalate the warning to a full-page stop sign your visitor has to click past. Either way, the visitor who leaves never tells you why.
