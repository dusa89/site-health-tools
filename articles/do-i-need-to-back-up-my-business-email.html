Do I Need to Back Up My Business Email?

The honest answer: yes - because the company that hosts your email is not keeping a spare copy of it. Google and Microsoft will keep the lights on: their servers stay up, the mail keeps flowing. But if a message is deleted, an account is closed, or someone hostile gets into your mailbox, the copy you lose is on you to have kept. Both companies say so in writing - it is called shared responsibility, and your half of it is the data. The good news: you do not need an IT department. The built-in safety nets are real, they are just time-limited, and a genuine backup for a small team costs less than a pizza per person per year. Below: who is responsible for what, the actual recovery windows, why Vault is not a backup, what a real backup costs in 2026, and the four traps that feel like backups.

1. The rule nobody reads: they keep the platform, you keep the data

Cloud email runs on what the industry calls shared responsibility. Google protects the infrastructure - the data centers, the network, the uptime - and you are responsible for what happens inside your own accounts: your access controls, your permissions, and your data. That includes backing it up. The assumption that "it's in the cloud, so it's backed up" is exactly the one the backup vendors make their living on: Google keeps the platform safe, but deleted, corrupted, or stolen data is your problem, not theirs (thehackernews.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Microsoft's side is the same structure: the platform is theirs, the data is yours, and after a few weeks they do not take responsibility for recovering lost mail (backupvault.co.uk; crawled 2026-10-10). None of this is hidden - it is just never said out loud until the day something is gone. (If the question underneath is really "is 2FA enough?", the 2FA article is the prevention half; this article is the recovery half - what saves you after something gets deleted.)

2. The safety nets are real - and every one of them has a countdown

This is the part most owners have half-right. There are built-in recoveries. They are just clocks, and after the clock runs out even Google's own support team cannot help you. Gmail trash: 30 days. Deleted mail sits in Trash for 30 days, then Google empties it automatically - permanently (kaseya.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Workspace admin restore: 25 days. An admin can restore a user's permanently deleted Drive or Gmail data for up to 25 days after it left the user's trash - after that, "the data cannot be restored, even if you contact technical support" (workspace.google.com data-recovery whitepaper; crawled 2026-10-10). Deleted user account: 20 days. An admin can restore a deleted account for up to twenty days; after that the account - and its data - is permanently deleted (workspace.google.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Microsoft 365: 14 days by default, 30 max. Deleted mail goes to Deleted Items, then to the hidden Recoverable Items folder; the default window is 14 days and an admin can extend it to at most 30 (learn.microsoft.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Note the fine print Microsoft itself documents: if someone hard-deletes a folder, the messages can come back but the folder tree itself cannot - you get a pile of mail, not your organization (dev.to adminpackstudio, ~2 days old; crawled 2026-10-10). Every one of these windows assumes you notice in time. Realize six weeks later that the quotes folder is gone, and the clock already beat you.

3. Why Google Vault (and Microsoft's eDiscovery) is not a backup

Ask a web person about backup and many will say "we have Vault" - and believe it. Google Vault is the feature most often confused with backup, and it is built for something else entirely: retaining and searching mail for lawsuits and compliance. It does not offer point-in-time recovery, and - the part that surprises people - the retained data lives inside Google's own environment, so a compromise or malicious change inside your Workspace can affect the Vault copy at the same time (kaseya.com, updated ~17 days ago; crawled 2026-10-10). Put bluntly by an education-sector breakdown: Vault "is not a backup tool, and data in Vault cannot be restored to Google Workspace" (edtechmagazine.com; crawled 2026-10-10). There is no secondary copy independent of Google - a single point of failure - and restoring from Vault means exporting and rebuilding mail manually (backupify.com; crawled 2026-10-10). Microsoft's eDiscovery is the same shape: it can find and export deleted mail for legal purposes, not put your inbox back the way it was. Vault and eDiscovery are for proving what happened, not for getting it back.

4. The moves that feel like backups - Takeout, exports, forwarding

A few common habits look like backup and are not. Google Takeout is a manual export: someone has to remember to run it, store the downloaded file safely, and manually reconstruct mail in a crisis - it is a snapshot, not a restorable system (n-able.com, updated 3 days ago; crawled 2026-10-10). Running it once a year means losing up to a year of mail if disaster strikes on the wrong week. Auto-forwarding a copy to a personal inbox gives you a duplicate pile, not an organized restore: it carries no folder structure, no calendar or contacts, and if the intruder deletes from both boxes it deletes from the spare too. Archiving policies (Google's default retention, Microsoft's auto-expanding archive) are about keeping mail longer, not about having a separate copy outside the platform. And exporting to a PST file once is another snapshot that starts going stale the day after you make it. The test of a real backup is simple: can you hand someone a date - "put my inbox back the way it was on March 3rd" - and have them do it in an afternoon? None of the above can.

5. The five ways small businesses actually lose their email

None of these are unusual - they are the scenarios backup vendors keep warning about because small businesses keep living them. One: the accidental delete after the window. Someone mass-deletes old mail to free up space, empties Trash, and realizes six weeks later a client's contract thread was in there. The 30-day clock already ran. Two: the departing employee. A fired or quitting staffer deletes their mailbox on the way out, or the admin deletes the account to save the license fee before anyone asks whether that mailbox mattered - and the 20-day restore window is long gone by the time the lawyer calls (the employee-exit article covers the handoff you should have made). Three: the compromise. A phishing login gives an attacker the account; they read, then delete or corrupt, and the damage spreads faster than anyone notices. Four: the host collapses. Email that came with cheap web hosting dies with the host - accounts closed, data gone, no Google-style safety net at all (the hosting-email article is the full warning). Five: the single basket. Everything - invoices, contracts, customer threads, passwords - in one mailbox with no copy anywhere else. One lost account and the business's memory goes with it. Note what all five share: in every case, the platform worked exactly as designed. Nobody broke Google. That is why nobody at Google is going to fix it.

6. What a real backup costs in 2026 - and what it buys

A dedicated backup is a separate service that copies your mail (and usually Drive, calendar, contacts) on a schedule, stores the copy outside your provider's walls, and restores it - one message, one folder, one whole mailbox, to a date you pick. The 2026 prices are small-business friendly, all reported figures from the vendors and reviewers (crawled 2026-10-10): CubeBackup - $5 per user per year for businesses ($2 for education/nonprofits), backups run hourly and incrementally, and the copies live on storage you control (cubebackup.com). SysCloud - $4 per user per month or $38 per user per year (zenphi.com, updated ~16 days ago). Spanning Backup - around $48 per user per year (via capterra.co.il). Backupify - priced per user per month or per year (backupify.com; zenphi.com). Microsoft also sells its own Microsoft 365 Backup as a separately licensed add-on that extends recovery past the default 14-to-30-day windows - but you have to have bought and configured it; a tenant that hasn't is still inside those windows (pktech.net, updated ~22 days ago). What the money buys is the thing the built-ins never give you: a second copy, in a second place, restorable to a point in time, long after the built-in clocks have run out - including departed users' mailboxes whose licenses you already gave back. (And if you are choosing platforms in the first place, the suite-decision article compares what each gives you out of the box.)

7. The four traps

Trap one: "it's in the cloud, so it's backed up." The most expensive sentence in this article. The cloud protects the building; you protect what is inside your room. Trap two: counting Vault as backup. Vault is your lawyer's tool, not your safety net - it proves what was there, it does not put it back (see section 3). Trap three: the once-a-year export. A Takeout from January is worthless in October; a snapshot is only as fresh as the day it was taken, and backups only work if they run on a schedule you never have to think about. Trap four: archiving as a substitute. Retention policies keep mail longer inside the platform - they do not create the independent second copy that survives a deleted account, a compromise, or a dead host. And the quiet one underneath all four: nobody ever tests the restore. Ask your web person to recover one deleted message from three months ago and time how long it takes. If the answer is a shrug, you do not have a backup plan - you have a backup hope.

8. What to tell your web person

Send your assistant or web person this: "I want a real backup of our business email, separate from Google/Microsoft. Four asks. First, confirm our platform and its real recovery windows: Gmail trash 30 days, admin restore 25 days, deleted accounts 20 days - or on Microsoft, deleted-item retention 14 days default, 30 max - and tell me in writing what we could and could not get back today. Second, pick a backup service - CubeBackup at about $5 a user a year, SysCloud, Spanning, or Backupify - daily automatic backups of mail plus drive, calendar, and contacts, with the copy stored outside our email provider. Third, set it to keep departed employees' mailboxes restorable even after we delete their accounts and licenses. Fourth, do one test restore of a single message and show me it worked - I don't want to discover a broken backup on the day we need it. Plain English, and no 40-page IT proposal - most of these are self-serve signup products, so budget an afternoon, not an IT project." The proof is one recovered message from a date you pick.
