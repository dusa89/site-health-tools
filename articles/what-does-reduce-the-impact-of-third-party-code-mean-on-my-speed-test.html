What Does 'Reduce the Impact of Third-Party Code' Mean on My Speed Test?

The honest answer: that line is about code running on your site that you didn't write and don't control - analytics, ad pixels, chat widgets, booking tools, social buttons, embedded videos. Your speed test fails it when that other-people's code blocks your visitor's phone for more than a quarter second during load - and the real sting is that the weight is only half the story. These scripts hog the phone's single processing lane and keep running for the whole visit, so a site that looks light can still feel sluggish. The median mobile page now ships more third-party JavaScript than its own code. Below: what counts as third-party, the 250-millisecond rule, why it's worse than the bytes suggest, the four fixes in order, and what to ask your web person.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your site.

What the line actually says

Your speed test (Lighthouse, the engine behind PageSpeed Insights) lists a line called “Reduce the impact of third-party code”. It fails that line when code from other companies' servers blocks your visitor's phone for 250 milliseconds or more during the page load. The report lists each offender by company name with how much time and data it cost. In the newest Lighthouse version the standalone line moved into a renamed section - same rule, same 250-millisecond tripwire. Think of this line as a table, not a score: it names who is spending your visitor's time.

What counts as “third-party”

Anything that loads from a domain that isn't yours. The usual suspects on a small-business site: Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager, chat widgets, booking widgets, review widgets, Facebook and ad pixels, Instagram feeds, embedded YouTube videos, cookie banners, and A/B-testing tools. Even the “friendly” ones count - none of them are free. For each, see our pieces on chat and booking widgets, tracking scripts, and review widgets.

Why it's worse than the byte count suggests

Two reasons, both invisible in a file-size readout.

Everything runs in one lane. A phone has one main processing thread - the lane your page uses to draw, to scroll, and to respond to taps. Every third-party script runs in that same lane. While it's busy running someone's chat widget code, your visitor's tap waits. The median mobile page's total blocking time sits around 1.9 seconds - time the phone simply couldn't respond. Your own code's dead weight is a separate line on the test; this line is everyone else's.

They don't stop at load. These scripts keep working for the whole visit: analytics phones home, ad scripts refresh, chat widgets poll for new messages. So the slowdown isn't a one-time tax at page open - it's background noise competing with every scroll and tap for the rest of the session.

The scale: the median mobile site loads 21 third-party resources totaling about 450 KB, and carries 375 KB of third-party JavaScript against just 168 KB of its own code - nearly double the code from other companies. They're also the unstable half of your score: the number wanders between tests partly because these scripts behave differently each run.

The four fixes, in order

1. Inventory what you're carrying. Most of this stuff arrived via Google Tag Manager, which hides the real cost: one container can quietly load a dozen tags, added over time by different people, with nobody checking the bill. List every third-party on the site and ask who still needs it. The ones nobody claims get removed - they were taxing every visit for nothing.

2. Delay or disguise the heavy ones. Scripts that aren't needed for the first paint should load after it (deferred loading - ask for this by name). For heavy embeds like YouTube, use the facade pattern: show a thumbnail with a play button, and only load the full player when the visitor clicks. The page gets the look of the video immediately; the expensive part is created only on demand.

3. Cut ruthlessly. A tracker that once answered one question and now answers nothing is pure cost. One business's rule of thumb: for every new third-party you add, remove two old ones.

4. See the page without them. Chrome's developer tools can block a third-party host and reload the page - one click shows you your site as if that company never existed. If it suddenly feels fast, you've found your culprit.

What to ask your web person

Four questions, and note the answers: (1) “Which third parties load on the homepage, and which does each one do for the business?” (2) “Which of them can be deferred until after the page shows?” (3) “Can the YouTube embeds become click-to-play facades?” (4) “What does the third-party line show in milliseconds now, and after you finish?” That last one is the only number that matters on this line: under 250, you pass.
