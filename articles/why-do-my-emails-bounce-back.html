Why Do My Emails Bounce Back?

You hit send on a quote, a contract, an invoice — and a few minutes later an email comes back saying it could not be delivered. That returned message is called a bounce, and it is actually good news wearing a scary costume: unlike email that silently slides into a spam folder (covered in the spam article), a bounce tells you what went wrong. There is one skill to learn — finding the 3-digit code near the top — and after that, almost every bounce falls into one of five families, each with its own fix. This article is the whole map.

1. What a bounce actually is

A bounce is your email being returned to sender, with the reason stamped on it. Think of it like the post office bringing your letter back with a sticker: "no such address," "mailbox full," "package too heavy." The returned message usually comes from an address like MAILER-DAEMON or "Mail Delivery System" — that is a robot, not a person, so do not reply to it. The information you need is in the first two or three lines; everything below that is technical wrapping you can ignore.

Three outcomes, three different problems. Delivered means it arrived (whether they read it is another question). Bounced means it came back with an error — loud and readable, this article. Silently missing means no error came back and the customer never saw it — that is the spam-folder problem, and the spam article covers it. Owners waste hours treating all three the same way. The first question is always: did anything come back?

2. The one skill: find the 3-digit code

Open the bounce and look at the top for a number like 550 or 452. Email servers speak in 3-digit codes (this is part of the email standards, RFC 5321 — the same rules every provider has used for decades), and the first digit is the whole story:

Codes starting with 5 (500-599) are PERMANENT. "Stop and fix something — sending it again changes nothing." A 550 will never succeed on retry. Read the words after the code; they name the fix.

Codes starting with 4 (400-499) are TEMPORARY. "Something is wrong right now, we will try again." Your mail server automatically retries these — Microsoft's own mail protection, for example, retries a deferred message every 15 minutes for a full day (Microsoft Learn, verified Oct 2026). You do nothing. You only act if a second message arrives a day later saying delivery finally failed.

"Delivery delayed" is NOT a bounce. It is a warning that the server is still trying — the 4xx family doing its job. File it, forget it, and only investigate if the final failure notice follows. Panicking at the warning is the most common false alarm in business email.

3. The five bounces every owner meets

Bounce one: 550 5.1.1 — "user unknown" / "mailbox unavailable." The most common bounce in business life. It means the address you sent to does not exist at that domain — misspelled, a typo in your contacts, or the person left the company and their mailbox was deleted. The fix is embarrassingly simple and almost always skipped: read the address back letter by letter. One transposed letter (gmial instead of gmail) produces exactly this bounce. If the address looks right, the customer changed jobs — call them and ask for the current one. The missing-quote article is the cousin of this bounce: no error, no delivery, same first step.

Bounce two: 552 5.2.2 (or 452) — "mailbox full" / "over quota." This one is not about you at all: the recipient's inbox is full, so their server refused your email. There is nothing to fix on your side — no setting, no record, no upgrade. The fix is a phone call or a text: "your inbox is full, my quote bounced." Small businesses hit this constantly with customers on old hosting-company email plans with tiny storage. Do not keep resending; the bounce will keep coming until they clear space.

Bounce three: 552 5.3.4 — "message too large." Your attachment (or the whole email) exceeded the recipient's size limit — and sometimes your own provider's. Two facts make this bounce confusing. First: Gmail's 25 MB limit is the entire message, not the file on your disk (mailjerry.com, verified Oct 2026) — the email body, the signature images, and the file all count. Second: attaching a file makes it grow about a third bigger in transit (the encoding email uses), so a 20 MB file arrives as roughly 26-27 MB — over the 25 MB line even though the file itself looked fine (mailjerry.com; Microsoft's own 365 blog, both verified Oct 2026). Microsoft 365 allows up to 150 MB if configured, but defaults to 35 MB send / 36 MB receive (Microsoft Learn, verified Oct 2026) — and most small-business hosts set theirs far lower than Gmail's 25. The fix is not a smaller file; it is no file at all: put it on Google Drive, Dropbox, or OneDrive and send the link. Contractors sending job photos and contracts hit this bounce weekly; the link habit ends it permanently. Zipping rarely helps — photos and PDFs are already compressed, so the zip barely shrinks.

Bounce four: 550 5.7.1 — "blocked" / "rejected due to policy." The recipient's server does not trust your mail. This is the bounce that points at your setup: your domain or sending address is on a blocklist, or your authentication is failing so you look like a forger. It is also the bounce where the other articles in this family earn their keep — the blacklist article for the blocklist case, the authentication article for the SPF/DKIM/DMARC case, and the deliverability article for the 10-second diagnosis. Unlike the first three bounces, this one is never the customer's fault. If every address at one customer's company bounces 5.7.1, though, their server may simply distrust your provider — rare, and a call to their IT person resolves it. Gmail's own version of this rejection — the "your mail looks suspicious" verdict — is common enough to have its own article.

Bounce five: 550 5.4.5 — "daily sending quota exceeded." You sent too many emails today and your provider cut you off until tomorrow. Gmail allows 500 a day on a free account and 2,000 a day on Google Workspace (Cornell IT's published Workspace limits, verified Oct 2026). Owners hit this the day they send a newsletter or a batch of quotes from their main business address — the newsletter article explains why that is the wrong tool for the job. The fix: wait 24 hours, then move bulk sending to a proper mailing service and keep the business address for one-to-one mail.

4. The mistakes that make bounces worse

Mistake one: retrying a 5xx bounce. A permanent failure will fail forever. Resending the same email to the same address ten times produces ten bounces and zero deliveries. Read the code, fix the cause, then send once.

Mistake two: deleting the address in a panic. A 5.1.1 bounce does not always mean the customer is gone — it often means you typed it wrong once. Confirm the address before you delete the contact; the number of "lost customers" who were actually typos would fill a filing cabinet.

Mistake three: replying to the bounce. It came from MAILER-DAEMON, a robot. Your reply goes nowhere. Forward the bounce to your web person or IT helper instead — the code at the top is exactly what they need.

Mistake four: treating a bounce like a spam problem. Bounces are errors with answers; spam-foldering is silent and needs authentication work. If the bounce says 5.7.1 (blocked), then the authentication articles apply — but a 5.1.1 wrong-address bounce needs no authentication work at all, and owners who "fix their SPF" for a typo'd address are fixing the wrong thing.

5. The questions owners always ask next

"The bounce is full of gibberish — headers, IP addresses?" Ignore all of it. The one line that matters is near the top: the 3-digit code and the plain words right after it ("user unknown," "mailbox full," "message too large," "blocked"). Everything else is the servers talking to each other.

"It only bounces when I email one company." Then it is their server and their rules, not your email. One company's filter can be stricter than the rest of the world's. Call someone there — ideally their IT person — and ask what their filter flagged. Nine times in ten it is their full mailbox or an overzealous filter, and a human resolves it in minutes.

"It bounces from my phone but not my laptop?" Then the problem is the phone's setup, not the recipient — the phone article covers the sending-settings side of exactly this.

"Should I worry about one bounce to one address?" No. Single bounces are typos and full inboxes — routine. Worry when the pattern appears: the same code to many addresses (your setup has a problem — start with the diagnostic), or bounces on addresses that worked last month (something changed — a recent provider move breaks authentication records if they were not rebuilt).
