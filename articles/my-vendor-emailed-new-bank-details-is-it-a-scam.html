My Vendor Emailed New Bank Details — Is It a Scam? The Payment-Redirection Trap

The email looks right. It sits in the middle of a real thread about a real invoice from a vendor you actually pay — and it says the bank details changed, so please send this month's payment to the new account. Maybe your vendor really did switch banks. Or maybe you are one click away from wiring this month's payment to a criminal's account, and by the time you find out, the money is gone. This is the most expensive email fraud aimed at small businesses, and it works even when your own email is perfectly secure — because the trick is not in your inbox. It is in your payment process.

First, where this guide sits among its siblings: this is not about your customers receiving mail that looks like it came from you — that guide is here — and it is not about your own mailbox being hacked (that one). It is also not a deliverability problem (that guide is here); your email authentication passing or failing is beside the point, because a compromised vendor's real mailbox passes SPF, DKIM, and DMARC just fine. This guide is about one email and one wire transfer: somebody asked you to change where the money goes, and everything else hangs on whether you believe them.

The short answer: treat every bank-details change as fraud until proven otherwise. The proof is one phone call — made to a number you already had, not one from the message — asking a person you know to confirm the change and read the new details back to you. That call takes two minutes and defeats all three fraud routes below. Everything else in this guide is how to tell the routes apart, what the warning signs are, and what to do if the money already left.

The three ways this fraud works

Route 1 — The spoofed vendor. The criminal registers a lookalike domain (an added hyphen, a swapped letter, a different ending) and emails you posing as your vendor. The thread may even look continuous, because faking a forwarded history is easy: in August 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence documented a campaign of more than 1,000,000 emails — 87.7% aimed at US organizations — each asking for an ACH transfer of around $50,000 against a fake annual ServiceNow subscription invoice, complete with a fabricated forwarded thread in which the impersonated CEO appeared to have already approved the payment. The lookalike domains were registered three days before the campaign started.

Route 2 — The compromised vendor. The criminal is actually inside your vendor's real mailbox. The email comes from the real address, inside the real thread, with the real invoice history — because they read the thread first. Abnormal AI's 2026 data found about 61% of email attacks in their dataset involved vendor email compromise, with billing-change schemes as the leading BEC variant at 26.5%. No authentication check on your end can catch this: the message is genuinely from the vendor's account. Only the process — the callback — catches it.

Route 3 — The mid-thread change. The invoice is real, the conversation is real, and then the "new banking details" message arrives inside it. This is the version that defeats the owner's instinct to check, because every instinct says the conversation is legitimate. The instinct is right about the conversation; it is the bank details that changed. The scale is why criminals keep doing this: the FBI's 2024 Internet Crime Report recorded $2.77 billion in business email compromise losses across 21,442 complaints — the seventh most reported crime to the IC3, but the second-highest by dollar loss — and the 2025 report put it at $3.05 billion. The Association for Financial Professionals found 63% of organizations experienced BEC in its 2025 survey.

Why it works: urgency plus authority, minus the walk-over

Every version of this fraud runs the same psychology: urgency ("pay today or the order stalls") plus authority ("the boss approved it," or simply the vendor's trusted name). The FBI's guidance calls it out directly: be especially wary if the requestor is pressing you to act quickly. And the check that used to be easy — walking over to the person who supposedly sent the request — is exactly what remote work took away. When the check is a call you have to make, a fabricated forwarded thread showing the CEO already approved it is designed to make skipping the call feel reasonable. The fraud counts on the check feeling like overkill for a vendor you know. That is the whole con: the vendor you know is the reason you don't check.

The one control: the callback, on a number you already had

The FBI's business email compromise guidance directs organizations to verify payment requests and account changes through a known, trusted contact channel — and its BEC page says it plainly: verify any change in account number or payment procedures with the person making the request, in person if possible or by calling. The IC3 version sharpens the one rule that matters: call a number already known to the business, taken from internal records, never one supplied in the message. In practice:

1. Stop the payment run for that supplier. Nothing goes to the new account until the change is confirmed. Urgency should accelerate the review, never bypass it.

2. Call the supplier on a number you already hold. The vendor master file, a signed contract, a past verified email, or the company's own website. The FBI: look up the company's phone number on your own — do not use the one a potential scammer is providing. Never the number in the suspicious email. The call is the control; everything else supports it.

3. Speak to a person you know there. Ask them to confirm the change and read the new account details back to you. Two minutes. If they didn't send it, their mailbox or yours may be compromised — both sides should change passwords and check for forwarding rules (criminals quietly forward incoming mail so the real thread stays visible to the owner while copies go to them).

4. Check the sender's domain character by character. Compare it against earlier, genuine emails. Look for swapped letters, an added hyphen, a different ending. Modern fraud messages are polished; excellent grammar is no longer proof of anything.

5. Use your bank's name check. Many banks show the account holder's name before you confirm a new payee. If the name doesn't match the supplier, do not pay.

6. Put a second pair of eyes on it. One person updates the vendor record, another approves it after seeing the callback note. If two-person approval is impossible in your shop, require the callback plus a written record: who you called, on which number, when, and what they said.

7. Write the rule down now, before it happens. Decide in advance how an urgent payment request gets confirmed, so nobody has to invent a process under pressure on a Friday afternoon. New payee or changed details → callback to an internal-record number, every time, regardless of what any forwarded thread appears to show. The same rule applies if the request arrives by text, Teams, Slack, or a video call — a live face is a channel, not a verification.

If the wire already left

1. Call your bank's fraud or wire desk immediately. Recovery is a race against the clock — recall success drops fast as the money moves through accounts. Do not email; call.

2. File a complaint with the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov. Include every detail: the fraudulent email, the account numbers, the amounts, the timeline. IC3 complaints feed the investigations and the bank-recovery coordination.

3. Check your own mail for forwarding rules. If the criminal had any presence in your mailbox, they may have set rules quietly copying or deleting mail so the real thread stayed visible. Remove them and change the password.

4. Warn the real vendor. If their mailbox was the compromised one, they need to lock it down before the next customer pays the wrong account. They may not know.

The bottom line

A "bank details changed" email is never an email problem — it is a payment problem wearing an email costume. The three routes (spoofed vendor, compromised vendor, mid-thread change) all lose to the same control: a two-minute callback to a number you already had, asking a person you know to read the new details back to you. That call is the cheapest insurance in business finance. And write the rule down before Friday afternoon, not during it — the fraud counts on the check feeling like overkill for a vendor you know, and the vendor you know is the whole reason to check.
