What that padlock in your browser actually means

That little padlock next to your website’s address is a promise to every visitor: nobody between you and them can read or alter what’s passing through. Here’s what powers it, how it breaks, and the deeper protections most small-business sites are missing.

The padlock means one specific thing: the connection between your visitor and your site is encrypted. The encryption runs on a certificate — a digital ID card for your domain, issued by a trusted authority, with an expiry date printed on it. When it lapses, browsers don’t show a gentle reminder; they show visitors a full-page “not secure” warning, and most of them leave on the spot.

Why expiry sneaks up on busy owners

Certificates from free providers last only 90 days. Ninety days is short enough that the renewal your host set up two years ago can quietly stop working — a changed email address, a moved DNS record — and you find out from a customer, not from your host. Knowing your exact expiry date is the cheapest insurance in this whole guide.

Beyond the padlock: the instructions your site sends

With every page, your site also sends a set of behind-the-scenes instructions called security headers. They tell browsers things like “always use the encrypted connection, even if someone tries to downgrade it,” “never let another site embed this page invisibly,” and “only load code from sources I approve.” Mozilla’s HTTP Observatory grades all of this on an A+ to F scale — a building inspector for your website: it doesn’t just check the front door, it checks the locks on every window.

Most small-business sites land in the C to F range. Not because the owner did anything wrong, but because the platform or host ships weak defaults and nobody ever tightened them. The fixes are usually small configuration changes, not rebuilds.
