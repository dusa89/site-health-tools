Do broken links hurt my Google ranking?

The SEO scan email says it every time: “Your site has 47 broken links — this is hurting your Google rankings!” Half of that sentence is true. Here is the half that is, straight from Google’s own people.

Let’s start with the part that surprises most owners: broken links are not a direct Google ranking factor. Google has said this plainly, more than once. Google’s John Mueller wrote on LinkedIn in August 2025: “404s are fine. Having pages on a site that return 404 or 410 is perfectly fine with regards to SEO,” he said — even if a site has hundreds of millions of them. Years earlier he confirmed the same point the long way: 404 errors do not affect the ranking of your other pages. A dead link here and there is normal internet housekeeping, and Google does not punish you for it.

So why do the SEO scanners scream about them?

Because broken links still cost you in three indirect ways — and “indirect” matters less to a customer who just hit a dead end.

1. Visitors who hit a dead link leave

This is the big one, and it has nothing to do with Google. A customer clicks “Book now” on your services page and lands on a “page not found” screen. Most people do not hunt for the right page — they bounce, and a chunk of them do not come back. Dead links are a customer problem wearing an SEO costume. (We wrote the full customer-side case here: how broken links cost you customers.)

2. They waste Google’s visits to your site

Every time Google’s crawler follows one of your broken links, it spends a visit learning nothing. On a small site this is minor — Google will get around to your good pages anyway — but a big pile of dead links slows down how fast your real pages get seen and re-seen. Think of it as the crawler knocking on empty rooms while your open rooms wait.

3. The one that actually hurts: soft 404s

Here is the exception that really does damage, and it is sneakier than a dead link. A soft 404 is a page that looks fine to you — it loads, it has your header on it — but contains no real content, while telling browsers “everything is OK.” When Google catches the mismatch, it treats the page as a 404 and quietly removes it from the index. Your page simply stops appearing in search, with no warning. Google refined how it detects these in 2026 — it now evaluates them separately for desktop and mobile crawls — so a page that looks right on your phone can still be flagged from the other crawl. Broken links and soft 404s are different problems, but both come from the same disease: pages nobody has checked in a while.

What “404” and “soft 404” mean, in 20 seconds

A 404 is the internet’s “this page doesn’t exist” — you deleted the page, renamed the URL, or typed the link wrong. A soft 404 is a page that says “all good” while showing nothing worth reading. One is honest and harmless; the other quietly erases you from Google. If you are going to fix one, fix the soft 404s first.
