Why Does Gmail Stop Me From Sending After 500 Emails?

The honest answer: it is not a glitch - it is Gmail’s anti-spam ceiling, and every free Gmail account has one. A free Gmail address can send at most 500 emails a day. Hit the ceiling and Gmail stops your sending for up to 24 hours with an error saying you have reached a limit - while your inbox keeps receiving normally. Paid Google Workspace accounts get 2,000 a day, and brand-new or trial accounts sit at 500 too. Below: the exact numbers, the traps that burn through the limit without you noticing, and what to do when your business needs more.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your account - Google can change them without notice.

The verdict first: the ceiling is real and it is 500

Think of your Gmail account like a phone plan with a data cap. Gmail limits free accounts to 500 emails per day, counted on a rolling 24-hour window - not midnight to midnight, but 24 hours from your first send. Paid Google Workspace accounts get 2,000 a day. Trial accounts, and Workspace accounts with fewer than six billing cycles of history, sit at the same 500 as a free account.

Two catches on the fine print. First, Gmail counts recipients, not messages: one email sent to 50 people uses 50 of your daily 500. Second, the per-message cap shrinks when you send through a mail program instead of the browser - about 100 recipients per message via SMTP, versus 500 through the Gmail website.

The traps that burn through the limit silently

Trap one: the newsletter. A customer list of 400 names sounds small - until you learn that the 400-person send, plus your normal replies and forwards that day, plus the auto-responder you set for the holiday weekend, all draw from the same bucket. Replies, forwards, and auto-responders all count toward the limit, and they add up fast on a busy day. This is why running a newsletter straight out of Gmail keeps breaking - it is the exact wrong tool for bulk mail, and our newsletter-from-business-email article covers the right setup.

Trap two: the BCC blast. Sending one big email with 300 people in BCC feels like one send. Gmail sees 300 recipients. Add the morning’s back-and-forth with a client and you are suddenly at the ceiling by lunch - which is also why cold outreach from your main address is a bad idea on multiple levels, as our cold-email article explains.

Trap three: the rolling clock. Because the window resets 24 hours after your first send - not at midnight - you can be locked out through most of the next morning without realizing the pattern. There is no visible countdown; the first warning is the error itself.

What hitting the limit looks like - and what it is not

When you hit the ceiling, Gmail shows an error along the lines of “You have reached a limit for sending emails.” Your sends stop for anywhere from 1 to 24 hours, depending on the account. You can still receive email the whole time - only sending is locked. That distinction matters: this is a hard quota, not a spam filter. If your emails are landing in customers’ spam folders but still going out, that is a different problem - our sudden-spam article covers it - and if individual messages are being bounced back, see our Gmail-rejection article.

What to do when your business needs more

Option one: stop using Gmail as a bulk sender. Newsletters and customer blasts belong on a real email-marketing service (Mailchimp, ConvertKit, and their competitors), which sends from infrastructure built for volume and handles the unsubscribe rules the law requires. Your Gmail goes back to one-to-one business mail, where 500 a day is plenty.

Option two: split big sends across days. If you must send 800 individually-personalized messages, send 400 today and 400 tomorrow. Crude but effective.

Option three: upgrade the account. Paid Google Workspace raises the ceiling to 2,000 emails a day - and it is one more reason the Workspace-vs-Microsoft 365 decision matters. Note that the free-address habit itself has costs beyond the cap, which our free-Gmail-for-business article breaks down.

The real lesson: the limit is a signal. If you are routinely brushing up against 500 sends a day, your business has outgrown free Gmail as its email system - and the authentication checks (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) that keep a growing sender’s mail landing in inboxes are one of the checks inside our full audit.
