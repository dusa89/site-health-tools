Why Does Google Still Show My Deleted Pages?

You deleted the page two weeks ago. The old pricing, the discontinued service, the page from the redesign — gone from your site. But type your business name into Google and there it is, sitting in the results like nothing happened. A customer clicks it and lands on your 404 page. You are not doing anything wrong. Google is showing them a snapshot of your site from the past, and the past takes a while to catch up.

If you have already read our guide on why links break in the first place and what your 404 page should say when they do, this is the angle those two do not cover: the search engine in the middle. The broken link is not on your website at all — your site is fine, the page is gone. The broken link lives inside Google's memory of your site. Fixing it means understanding how that memory works.

The short answer: Google does not look at your live site every time someone searches. It keeps a copy — an index — built by visiting ("crawling") your pages on its own schedule. When you delete a page, Google has no idea until its crawler comes back, finds the 404, and updates the copy. That return visit can take days or weeks. Until then, the old listing stays, and every customer who clicks it lands on a dead page. There are three fixes, in order of importance: redirect the old URL to the closest live page, do not block the deleted page in robots.txt, and use Search Console's removals tool for anything urgent.

The snapshot, not the site

Think of Google's results page as a photograph of the web, not a live feed. Google's crawler walks your site periodically, takes notes on every page it finds, and files those notes away. The results customers see are drawn from the notes, not from your site in real time. This is why a change you made this morning — new hours, a deleted page, a renamed service — shows up on your site instantly but on Google eventually. The system is working as designed; it is just not designed to be instant for every site. Big news sites get re-crawled constantly because they change constantly. A small-business site that changes a few pages a month gets visited on a much lazier schedule.

So when you delete a page, here is the actual sequence: the page disappears from your site today. Google's copy of it stays. Sometime in the coming days or weeks, the crawler returns, knocks on the old URL, gets your 404 page, and eventually removes the listing from the results. "Eventually" is honest vagueness — there is no published timetable, and anyone who promises you an exact number of days is guessing. What you can do is make each step of that sequence behave correctly, so the process does not stall.

The trap: the "not found" page that isn't

Here is the failure mode that keeps deleted pages in Google's index far longer than they should be. Some websites — especially ones where the 404 page is a nicely designed template — serve their "page not found" page with an HTTP 200 status code. That code means "everything is fine, here is a normal page." Google's crawler cannot read; it reads the code. A "sorry, this page doesn't exist" page that returns 200 is, to Google, a page that exists with some odd content — and the URL stays indexed. Google even has a name for this: a soft 404.

You cannot see the status code by looking at the page, so this one hides in plain sight. The check is simple: ask whoever manages your site (or your hosting company) to confirm that deleted URLs return a real 404 — or better, a 410, the code that specifically means "gone on purpose." A 410 is the strongest signal you can send that the page is not coming back, and Google treats it accordingly. If your 404 page returns 200, nothing else in this guide works properly until that is fixed.

Fix 1: redirect the old URL to the closest live page

In most cases, you should not let the deleted URL sit there 404ing at all. A 301 redirect is a permanent forwarding instruction: anyone — customer or crawler — who visits the old address is sent to the address you name. It is the "we moved" sign of the web. The deleted pricing page redirects to the current pricing page; the discontinued service redirects to the services overview; the old blog post redirects to the closest live article.

Two rules. First, redirect to the closest match, not to the homepage — a customer who clicked "spring furnace tune-up special" and lands on a generic homepage feels misdirected; one who lands on the current specials page feels helped. (The closest-match rule also matters for search rankings: a redirect to an unrelated page passes along almost none of the old page's standing, as our SEO-and-broken-links guide explains.) Second, a redirect is forever-ish: keep it in place as long as the old URL might still be floating around in Google's index, bookmarks, or old emails — which means at least a few months. This single fix solves the customer-facing half of the problem immediately, regardless of how long Google takes to update its copy.

Fix 2: do NOT block the deleted page in robots.txt

This is the mistake that turns a two-week annoyance into a months-long one, and it feels like the right thing to do. Robots.txt is the file where you tell crawlers which parts of your site to stay out of. When a page is deleted, plenty of owners — and some web people — add the old URL to robots.txt, reasoning that Google should stop looking at it.

The logic runs exactly backward. If Google's crawler is blocked from visiting the deleted URL, it can never come back and discover the 404. It cannot update its notes. So the old listing sits in the results indefinitely, propped up by the very file meant to clean it up. The rule: never block a URL in robots.txt that you want Google to forget. Let the crawler visit it, let it see the 404 or follow the 301, and let the index update itself. (Blocking URLs you want kept out of the index entirely — admin pages, internal tools — is a different job, and robots.txt is fine for that. The distinction is whether you want Google to re-examine the page or never see it.)

Fix 3: the urgent cases — Search Console removals

Sometimes you cannot wait out the crawl cycle: the deleted page showed old prices a competitor is undercutting, a discontinued service you are still getting calls about, or something embarrassing from the old site. For those, Google provides a removals tool inside Search Console (the free tool where you verify ownership of your site). It temporarily hides a URL from Google's results — about six months — which buys time while the re-crawl catches up and the redirect or 404 does the permanent work.

Note the word temporarily: removals is a bandage, not the cure. If the page is still live (or still returning a soft 404) when the hiding period ends, the listing comes back. Always pair a removal request with Fix 1 or Fix 2 — the redirect or the honest 404 — so the underlying state is correct when the bandage comes off.

Also check: your own site's links to the dead page

While you are at it, search your own site for links pointing to the deleted URL. Menus, footers, old blog posts, and sidebar widgets keep linking to pages that no longer exist long after the page is gone — and every one of those is a broken link you control, no crawl cycle involved. Update them to the replacement page (or remove them) as part of the same job. The bottom line

Google shows your deleted pages because it is showing a photograph, not the live site. Make the photograph update itself: serve a real 404 (or 410) on URLs that are truly gone, redirect deleted URLs to their closest live match, never block those URLs in robots.txt, and use Search Console removals for the urgent ones. Do all four and the stale listings fade on their own — and in the meantime your customers land somewhere useful instead of nowhere.

No statistics are cited in this guide: re-crawl timing varies by site and no honest industry-wide figure exists, so timelines are described as mechanics ranges, not promises. Soft 404s, 301 redirects, the robots.txt re-crawl trap, and the Search Console Removals tool are documented Google behaviors, described here as mechanics. The fixes are maintenance judgment, not measured prescriptions — check your own deleted URLs with the steps above and let what you find set your priority.
