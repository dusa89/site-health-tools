Why isn't my business showing up on Google?

You typed your trade and your town into Google — “plumber in Henderson” — and scrolled. Competitors. A map with three pins. No you. The instinct is to blame your website. The website is usually the third or fourth reason, not the first.

Here is the scene almost every local business owner has lived through. You pull out your phone, search the exact words a customer would use, and your business is nowhere — not in the map pins, not on the first page, not anywhere. Your first thought is that your website is broken or invisible. That is possible, but it is rarely the actual gate. Something bigger decides who shows up for local searches, and your website is only a small part of it.

Your website is not what customers see first

For a search like “plumber in Henderson,” Google shows a map with three business pins before the regular results. A large study of 53,900 local search pages (Synup, September 2026) found that three-business map pack appears on 91 to 97 percent of local search pages — it is the most reliable thing on the whole page. Here is the detail that flips the usual thinking: around 3 in 10 of those map listings had no website link at all. Read that again. You can rank in the map pack without a website. Which means the absence of your website is not why you are missing — the map runs on something else.

The 46% number, honestly

You will see a stat everywhere: 46 percent of Google searches have local intent. The honest footnote is that the figure comes from a Google presenter’s talk in 2018 — nobody has published an official update since, and serious roundups in 2026 flag it as recycled. What has been measured recently is how people behave: BrightLocal’s 2025 research found 46 percent of consumers always or often add “near me” to the query, and 73 percent of those local searches start on a phone.

There is a second number that matters more for you: SparkToro and Datos’ clickstream study (May 2024) found 58.5 percent of U.S. Google searches ended without a click to any website. Most searches never produce a website visit at all — Google answers from the business listing itself. For a local business, that means the fight is not really for the click. It is for the listing.

The three real reasons, in order

1. Google does not know you are there. The map pack runs on your Google Business Profile — the listing that shows your name, address, hours, phone, photos, and reviews. The single most common problem we see is a profile that was never claimed, or was claimed years ago and left half-empty: wrong category, no services listed, old hours, no photos. BrightLocal’s 2025 survey of small-business owners found only 35 percent even have a Google Business Profile. If the profile is missing or thin, Google simply picks the competitor who gave it the facts.

2. Google is not convinced. When several businesses could fill a map pin, Google leans on trust signals — and reviews are the loudest. The Synup study found that “best [trade] in [town]” searches skew toward higher ratings: packs on “best” queries averaged 4.79 stars, and the share of pin businesses rated 4.8 or above jumped from 53 to 67 percent. A 4.2 with 12 reviews loses to a 4.8 with 200 reviews before your website ever gets a look. And one more figure worth sitting with: 62 percent of consumers say they will disregard a business they cannot find online at all (sagapixel, September 2026).

3. Your website does not say the right thing. This is where the website finally matters — but not the way owners think. You do not need the words “near me” in your title tags; Google already knows where the searcher is from your Business Profile address. What the site needs is one page that is actually about the town and the job, in plain sentences: the trade, the town, the service. Your business name, address, and phone must be identical everywhere — your site, Yelp, Facebook, directories, all of them. Conflicting listings make Google unsure you are one business at all.

The honest check you can run today

Search your trade and town in a private or incognito window on your phone — your normal results are personalized toward you, so your own searches lie. Then open Google Maps and search the same thing: 88 percent of consumers use Google Maps versus about 12 percent for Apple Maps (sagapixel, September 2026), so the Maps result is the one that matters. Ask a friend in a different zip code to run the same search, because results shift by location. And check the new front door: AI answers now sit on top of Google results and assistants get asked the same recommendation questions. Four fixes, in order of leverage

1. Claim and complete your Google Business Profile. Right category, real services, real hours, real photos — and reply to every review, because replies are extra text attached to your name that both Google and AI assistants can read. Cost: zero. This is the single highest-leverage hour you can spend.

2. Ask every happy customer for a review. Not a campaign, a habit: the ask goes out after every finished job. Volume and recency both count, and a steady trickle of fresh reviews beats one burst from two years ago.

3. Write the town page. One page per service-and-town, plain sentences, trade and town in the first paragraph. This is also the page an AI assistant is most likely to quote, so you get two wins from the same work.

4. Make your name, address, and phone identical everywhere. Directories, Yelp, Facebook, your site — same spelling, same punctuation. Free, boring, and disproportionately effective.
