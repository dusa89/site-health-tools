Do My Website's Videos Work for Deaf Customers?

Some of your customers will never hear a word of your videos. Deaf and hard-of-hearing customers read videos the way blind customers listen to them — and most small-business sites hand them a video player with the captions off, buried, or wrong. This is not a niche problem and the fix is not a rebuild. Here is what your videos look like to customers who read instead of listen, and how to cover them in a weekend.

First, what this guide is not: it is not about whether blind customers can use your site (see our screen-reader guide), whether a customer can work your contact form without a mouse (see our keyboard-navigation guide), your photos' descriptions (see our alt-text guide), or whether your videos slow the site down (see our video-speed guide). This guide is about one thing: whether the customers who cannot hear can understand your videos.

The short answer: if your videos have accurate captions turned on and visible, you are covered. If they have auto-generated captions nobody reviewed, captions buried in the player settings, or no captions at all, a whole category of customers is locked out of your message. The fix is a weekend of caption review, not a rebuild.

Your videos are silent films to some customers

Picture a customer researching your service. They click the two-minute video on your homepage — the one where you explain your process, name your prices, and tell them why to call you. The picture plays. The words play too, if you can hear them. For a deaf customer, the words simply do not exist. They watch your mouth move and guess.

This is not an edge case you can discount. Hearing loss is one of the most common conditions in adults, and it is invisible: the customer never tells you they could not hear your video. They just leave, and you never know why. Every video without captions is a page of your sales pitch written in invisible ink.

Auto-captions are a half-fix (the auto-caption trap)

Most platforms offer automatic captions now, and they feel like a solution. They are half of one. Auto-captions regularly get brand names wrong, mangle technical terms, and drop punctuation — the exact things that carry meaning. A caption that reads “we do air condishing for fortee eight hours” technically has captions. It does not technically communicate.

Worse, owners assume the captions are fine because the platform generated them. The honest version: auto-captions are a rough draft. Somebody — you, once — reads through them, fixes the brand names and the prices, and clicks save. Thirty minutes per video is typical. That one pass turns a rough draft into real access.

The caption toggle nobody can find

Even when captions exist, they often default to off, and the toggle lives three clicks deep in the player's settings gear. A customer who reads videos has to discover the setting exists, find it, and turn it on — on every video, on every site, forever. Most do not bother. They assume you did not caption it and move on.

Check your player settings once: where your platform lets you default captions on, turn them on. Where it lets you label the video as captioned, label it. The customers who need captions should not have to go hunting for them, and the customers who do not need them can turn them off in one click.

Transcripts: the quiet sibling

Not everything with sound is a video. Podcast episodes, interview audio, recorded webinars — if the content is audio-only, there is no player caption to toggle. The fix is a transcript: the words of the episode as text on the page, which every customer can read, skim, and search.

Do not leave the transcript as a second-class citizen. Put it below the player where a customer finds it without hunting, or link it clearly: “read the transcript.” For video too, a short text summary under the player covers the customers on slow connections and the ones who prefer reading — the same words you would put in captions, doing double duty.

Autoplay with sound is hostile (and not just to deaf customers)

A video that starts playing with sound the moment the page loads is hostile to everyone in a waiting room, an office, or bed at 1 a.m. — and to deaf customers it is pointless noise that adds nothing. If any video on your site autoplays, mute it. A muted autoplay video with captions is the polite version: motion catches the eye, words are available, nobody's speakers ambush them.

Check every page, not just the homepage. Testimonials widgets, hero sliders, embedded social videos — autoplay-with-sound hides in all of them. The phone test works here: open your site in a quiet room, scroll, and see what starts shouting.

The weekend fix

1. List your videos. Homepage, service pages, about page, embedded social posts. Most small sites have fewer than ten — the whole inventory fits on a napkin.

2. Review the captions on each. Play each video with captions on, read them, fix brand names, prices, and technical terms. Save. This is the highest-value hour in the whole project.

3. Default captions on where the platform allows. YouTube, Vimeo, and most players let you set the default; check yours.

4. Publish transcripts for audio-only content. One page per episode, linked from the player. Pasted text counts — it does not need to be pretty.

5. Kill autoplay with sound everywhere. Mute or click-to-play, site-wide. Check the widgets and embeds, not just the videos you uploaded yourself.

The bottom line

Your videos are doing two jobs: showing and telling. Without accurate, visible captions, the telling part only reaches the customers who can hear — and the rest leave without a word, literally and figuratively. The fix is not a redesign and not a new tool. It is one review pass per video, a settings toggle, and transcripts for the audio. A weekend, no budget, and a whole category of customers finally gets the whole message.
