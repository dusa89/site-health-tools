Why does my email say “via” next to my business name?

Your customer’s inbox shows “via mailchimp.com” or “via gmail.com” under your business name. That little tag is their inbox saying your email wasn’t fully authenticated as yours. Here is what causes it, why it costs you trust, and the fix — in leverage order.

You sent a quote to a customer. They open it, and next to your business name their inbox shows something like “via mailchimp.com” or “via gmail.com” — in Gmail it sits right under your name, in small gray text. Your email got there, nothing broke, but your business name is wearing someone else’s luggage tag. What is that? It is your customer’s inbox telling them, politely, that your email claims to be from your business but was actually mailed by someone else’s servers. Here is what causes it, why it quietly costs you trust, and how to make it disappear.

What the “via” tag actually means

Every email has two identities. The one your customer sees — the From line with your business name and address — and the one the mail servers check: which server actually sent it, and whether that server is authorized to speak for your domain. When those two identities do not match up, Gmail and Outlook show the “via” tag to flag the mismatch. It is the provider’s way of saying: this message says it is from acmeplumbing.com, but the envelope says it was mailed by mailchimp.com — proceed with your eyes open.

It is not an error, and it is not a block. But it is a tell. Customers who notice it learn, consciously or not, that your email was not fully authenticated — and phishers are exactly the people who mail “from” domains they do not own. You are not being accused of anything. You are just carrying the same visible mark the real scammers carry.

The three ways small businesses earn the “via” tag

1. Your email marketing tool, un-authenticated. This is the most common cause. You send newsletters or promotions through Mailchimp, Constant Contact, or similar — but you never completed the tool’s “verify your domain” step. So the tool signs the mail with its domain instead of yours. The fix the tool offers is free: it gives you two or three DNS records to paste into your domain’s settings, which authorizes it to sign mail as your business. Owners skip this step because the tool works fine without it — it works, but with the tag.

2. Gmail’s “send mail as” shortcut. You have a proper business address like you@acmeplumbing.com, but you send from inside your personal Gmail using its “Send mail as” feature, routed through Gmail’s servers. To the recipient that reads “you@acmeplumbing.com via gmail.com” — Gmail is telling the truth about who mailed it. The clean version is to send through your own domain’s outgoing mail server (Gmail’s settings let you add your domain’s SMTP credentials), or just use your domain’s actual mailbox. Our companion guide on why Gmail rejects business email covers the other half of this problem: Gmail has been tightening what it accepts from senders since 2024.

3. Your booking, CRM, or invoicing tool. Appointment reminders, invoices, and confirmations often go out “from” your address but through the software company’s servers — Housecall Pro, Square, your CRM — with no authentication set up. These are the emails that most need to look trustworthy, and they are the ones most often wearing the tag.

Why it is worth fixing (beyond looking professional)

The tag is a symptom of the same machinery that decides whether your email lands in the inbox at all. The DNS records that remove the tag — SPF and DKIM, authenticated to your domain — are exactly the authentication checks Gmail, Yahoo, and Outlook weigh when they decide spam or inbox. Since the industry-wide bulk-sender requirements took effect in 2024, mail that cannot prove it comes from the domain it claims gets filtered harder, and unauthenticated mail is rejected outright in many cases. The “via” tag is the part of that system your customer can see. The rest of it happens silently, which is why it pays to verify what your setup actually passes — see the free check below.

One honest caveat: provider wording changes. Gmail and Outlook phrase and display this annotation differently, and they tweak it over the years. The stable fact underneath is the mechanism: if the From address and the authenticated sending domain do not align to your domain, something in the recipient’s inbox says so. That has been true for years and is not going away.

The fix, in leverage order

First, authenticate your domain in every tool that mails for you. In Mailchimp it is called “verify a domain”; in Constant Contact, “authenticate your domain”; in SendGrid, “domain authentication.” Same job everywhere: the tool hands you DNS records, you paste them into your domain’s DNS settings, you confirm. The tag disappears once the tool signs mail as your domain instead of its own. This one step fixes most cases.

Second, check what your customers actually see. Send a test email to a Gmail address you own and look at it the way a customer would. In Gmail, open the message, click the three dots, then “Show original” — you will see exactly which domains signed and authenticated the message (“mailed-by” and “signed-by”). If your domain is not on both, the tag stays.

Third, finish the authentication trio. SPF and DKIM get your domain signed and authorized; DMARC is the record that tells Gmail and Outlook what to do with mail that fails those checks — it is the difference between a quiet cleanup and the same unauthenticated mail still landing. If you have not set these up at all, our guide What is DMARC walks through the three policy settings in plain English.
