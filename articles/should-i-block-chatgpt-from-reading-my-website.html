Should I Block ChatGPT From Reading My Website?

The worry is real: you heard ChatGPT reads websites, and now you are wondering whether it is reading yours — and whether it should be. Some web people paste a “block all AI bots” rule into robots.txt and call it done. That one rule can do two opposite things at once: it can keep your words out of AI training, and it can delete you from the AI answers your customers are reading. Whether that is good or bad depends entirely on which bot you blocked — because there is not one ChatGPT crawler. There are three, and they do very different jobs.

If you are new to how AI answers learn about your business at all, read our guide to getting recommended by ChatGPT first — it covers the sources AI reads. And if AI has never mentioned you, our guide to being invisible to AI walks through the usual causes. This guide is about the specific question: should AI crawlers be allowed on your site, or blocked?

The short answer: most small businesses want both things at once — to be read by AI (that is how you get recommended in answers) and to not be trained on (your words not baked into future models). You can have both. Block the training crawlers, allow the search crawlers. Blocking everything makes you invisible to AI-using customers; allowing everything lets your words be used to train models. Neither choice affects your Google ranking. There is not one ChatGPT crawler — there are three

This is the part the “block all AI bots” snippets get wrong. OpenAI runs three separate crawlers, each with its own name and its own rule in your robots.txt file. Blocking one does nothing to the others:

  • GPTBot — the trainer. This is the one most people picture when they worry. It crawls websites so OpenAI can train its AI models. If your instinct is “I do not want my words feeding AI training,” this is the crawler you block.
  • OAI-SearchBot — the reader. This one builds the index behind ChatGPT’s search answers — it is how your business gets found, cited, and linked inside ChatGPT answers. Block this one and you drop out of ChatGPT search results. It has nothing to do with training.
  • ChatGPT-User — the fetcher. This one does not crawl on a schedule at all. It fetches your page because a real person is in a conversation right now — they pasted your link, or ChatGPT decided to open your page while answering them. A visit from it means a person’s question led to your door.

So “block ChatGPT” is three different decisions, not one. The rule that keeps your words out of training is not the same rule that keeps you out of answers — and mixing them up is the expensive mistake.

Everyone else split the same way

OpenAI is not special here — the whole industry made the same split:

  • Google: plain Googlebot does search indexing (leave it alone). Google-Extended is the training opt-out — it is not even a real crawler, just a signal that says “do not use this for Gemini training.” Blocking it costs you nothing: Google says it does not affect your Google Search ranking, your indexing, or AI Overviews (those come from the regular search index).
  • Anthropic (Claude): ClaudeBot trains, Claude-SearchBot cites. Same deal — you can block one and allow the other.
  • Perplexity: PerplexityBot does both jobs at once, so blocking it is an all-or-nothing call.

The common mistake: the “block all AI bots” snippet

Copy-paste robots.txt snippets that disallow every AI crawler are everywhere now. Here is what they actually do to a small business: they protect you from training and erase you from AI answers at the same time. A December 2025 study of 100 top news sites found 79% block at least one AI training bot — and 71% also block at least one retrieval bot, meaning a lot of publishers may be accidentally cutting themselves off from AI search answers (that is the study’s conclusion, stated as such). For a local business, the AI answer is increasingly where the customer meets you first. The snippet that was supposed to protect you can be the thing that makes AI tell your customer about your competitor instead.

The copy-paste rule most small businesses want

This goes in your robots.txt file (the little file at the root of your website — your web person or host can add it in minutes). It says: no training crawlers, yes search crawlers.

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: Google-Extended
Disallow: /

User-agent: OAI-SearchBot
Allow: /

One technical gotcha, worth one sentence: a rule naming a specific crawler replaces your general rules for that crawler — so if your robots.txt already has a “User-agent: *” section with private pages blocked, repeat those lines under each named group, or the named crawler will not see them. And changes take roughly a day to be picked up (per OpenAI’s own documentation).

The honest limits (read this before you relax)

  • robots.txt is a request, not a lock. Well-behaved crawlers honor it; bad actors ignore it completely. It is not security — anything you do not want seen should not be on the public web at all.
  • Blocking does not un-train. It stops future crawling. It does not reach back and remove what is already inside a trained model.
  • ChatGPT-User is the fuzzy one. Because it fetches on behalf of a real person mid-conversation, a block rule may not stop it the same way it stops a scheduled crawler.
  • Blocking everything is a real option — with eyes open. If you genuinely want AI to leave you alone (no training, no answers), block the search crawlers too. Just know the price: when a customer asks AI “who does this near me,” you will not be in the answer. For most small businesses, that is too high a price for the privacy gained.

The bottom line

Do not ask “should I block ChatGPT?” Ask “which ChatGPT?” Block the trainers, allow the searchers, and keep Google-Extended blocked — it costs you nothing in search. And before you decide anything, find out what AI is actually saying about you right now: that is the real input to this decision, not fear and not hype.
