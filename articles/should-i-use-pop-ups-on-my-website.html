Should I use pop-ups on my website?

Pop-ups genuinely convert — the average is 3–5% and the best ones far exceed that. But a full-screen pop-up on a mobile search landing page can get the page demoted by Google, and it trains visitors to close your brand on reflex. Here are the numbers, the rules, and what to do instead.

Every small-business owner has been sold the same pitch: “just add a pop-up and watch your email list grow.” And honestly, it is not a lie — pop-ups really do convert. But the pop-up your vendor installed is a full-screen demand that slams shut in front of every visitor the second your page loads on their phone, and that pop-up is quietly doing two kinds of damage: Google can demote the page for it, and the visitors you want most — the ones arriving from a search, phone in hand — close it on reflex without reading a word.

Yes, pop-ups work — the benchmarks

The numbers are not shy. An analysis of 2 billion pop-up impressions (citing the widely-quoted Sumo benchmark) put the average pop-up conversion rate at 3.09%, with the top-performing pop-ups converting 28% of viewers. That is comfortably above the average email click-through rate across industries (~2.61%, per Mailchimp) — which is exactly why vendors love the pitch.

More recent data confirms the range, with a methodology caveat. HelloBar’s current benchmarking puts average pop-up conversion at 3–5%, the top 10% at 9% and above, and the top 1% past 12–15% — but notes it depends entirely on what the pop-up asks for: an email-for-10%-off pop-up can run 5–10%, while exit-intent pop-ups usually land at 2–4%. A 2026 roundup of vendor studies reported top-10% figures of 57.7% (Wisepops) and 42.35% (OptiMonk). The spread across studies is not a contradiction; it is a methodology difference — some count every display, some count only pop-ups that received engagement. Do not benchmark yourself against any single number. Benchmark against your own previous results for the same pop-up type.

The catch: Google penalizes the wrong kind

Google launched its mobile intrusive-interstitial demotion in January 2017, and it is still active in 2026. It is not a penalty in the remove-your-site sense — it devalues the page so it ranks worse. The target is specific: a full-screen pop-up that covers the main content immediately when a visitor arrives from a mobile Google search, a standalone interstitial they must dismiss before seeing anything, or a layout that looks like the whole page is the pop-up with the real content shoved underneath. Some sites reportedly dropped ten-plus spots in a day when it first went live.

Two things make this urgent for a local business. First, mobile-first indexing means Google judges the mobile version of your site — and your customers are overwhelmingly mobile. Second, the demotion applies to the page people land on from search results, which for a small business is usually the most valuable page it owns: the homepage or the service page that was supposed to ring the phone.

The exemptions are narrow: cookie consent notices, age gates, and login dialogs on content that is not publicly indexable are fine. Everything else that blocks the screen on arrival is a risk. Industry guidance distilled from Google’s rules: if you need something on the landing page, use a banner that takes up a reasonable amount of screen space (Search Engine Journal suggests a maximum of about 15%) instead of a full-screen overlay.

The honest caveat: the pop-up is not the problem, the ambush is

Here is what the data actually says when you read past the pitch. The gap between a 2% pop-up and a 10% pop-up is almost never the design — it is the targeting plus the offer. A pop-up that appears after someone has read half your page, offering something specific (a seasonal maintenance checklist, a first-visit discount), outperforms an instant full-screen demand every time. The best-performing pop-ups in every study share the same traits: right context, right timing, one clear offer, one ask, easy to close.

So the real cost of the default full-screen pop-up is not the conversions it misses — it is the visitors it trains. A customer arriving from search gets your pop-up before your content, closes it on reflex, and now associates your name with an interruption. You converted 3% and annoyed 97%, on the page that was supposed to make you look like the professional choice.

The fix, in leverage order

First, kill the load-time full-screen on mobile. This is the one Google can demote you for and the one visitors hate most. If a vendor set it to fire instantly for everyone, change it or disable it — this single change costs you nothing and removes the ranking risk.

Second, move to delayed or exit-intent triggers. Let people actually see your content first: fire after 30–60 seconds, after 50% scroll, or when they are about to leave (exit-intent typically converts 2–4%, but on visitors who were already leaving, so it is pure bonus). These patterns are low-risk in practice and still capture the people who were engaged enough to stay.

Third, one pop-up, one ask, one offer. “Get 10% off your first service — enter your email” beats a wall of three newsletter forms, a survey, and a coupon stack. And on mobile, use a slim banner, not a screen-filler — it sidesteps the interstitial rules and does not fight the visitor’s thumb.

Fourth, measure against yourself, not a vendor’s brochure. Track each pop-up’s conversion rate and your bounce rate on the same pages. If a pop-up converts 4% but the page’s bounce rate climbed 10 points, do the math on which one bought the phones ringing.
