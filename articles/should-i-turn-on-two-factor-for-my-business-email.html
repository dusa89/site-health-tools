Should I Turn On Two-Factor for My Business Email?

Your business email holds more than messages. It holds your quotes, your invoices, your customers' contact details — and the password reset links to nearly every other account you own. Most owners guard all of it with one password. Here is what the people who track account break-ins for a living say about that habit, the one setting that fixes it, and the two traps to avoid when you turn it on.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about your business email: the password is probably the weakest part of it. Not because it is short or weak — because it is one thing. One thing that can be phished, guessed, leaked in a breach somewhere else, or typed into a lookalike login page. And once someone has it, they do not just read your email. They reply as you, they see your quotes and invoices, and they can reset the password of anything that sends its recovery link to your inbox.

What the people who track break-ins say

Microsoft tracks more than 30 billion login events a day across its accounts, and its identity-security team has published what it sees. Their finding, from the director of identity security's RSA presentation: 99.9% of the compromised accounts they track each month did not use multi-factor authentication — and MFA stopped 99.2% of automated attacks. Those are not marketing words. They are the people who watch the attacks arrive saying, in effect: the accounts that fall are almost always the ones with only a password.

The FBI tells small businesses the same thing from the fraud side. Its standing advice against Business Email Compromise — the scam family that runs on hijacked real mailboxes — starts with: use multi-factor authentication, and verify account-change requests with a second pair of eyes. The Internet Crime Complaint Center logged 24,768 BEC complaints with $3.046 billion in reported losses in its 2025 report. Most of those scams needed exactly one thing to get started: somebody's mailbox. The fake-bank-details scam is the shape those takeovers take when they reach you — often from a real account, inside a real thread, with nothing for a filter to catch. Two-factor is the defense at the door the filter cannot guard.

What two-factor actually is (no jargon)

Two-factor — also called two-step verification, or MFA — is just a second question at login. First question: the password. Second question: something only you have right now. Usually it is a six-digit code from an app on your phone (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, or any free authenticator app), or a text message with a code, or a prompt on your phone that says "is this you trying to sign in?"

You already do this for your bank. The point is simple: a thief can steal your password from across the country, but they cannot also be holding your phone. Two questions, answered in two different places — and the attack that works on one password does not work anymore.

One preference matters: the app's codes are better than text-message codes. Security agencies like CISA recommend phishing-resistant methods where you can, and the practical version of that advice is this — phone numbers can be stolen (SIM swapping), while an authenticator app stays on the phone you hold. If your provider offers both, pick the app.

Why business email is the account that needs it most

Think about what lives in your inbox. Every quote you sent this month. Every invoice, paid and unpaid. Your customers' phone numbers. And — the part owners underestimate — the password-reset emails for your bank, your hosting, your registrar, your accounting software. An attacker who gets into the mailbox does not need to hack anything else. They can search "password reset" and walk through your whole business life one recovery email at a time. The compromised-mailbox guide covers how to spot a takeover in progress; the point here is that two-factor is what keeps the takeover from happening at all.

This is a different check from the email authentication work. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC prove to the world that mail claiming to be from your domain really is yours. Two-factor proves to your mailbox that the person typing the password really is you. The scam industry attacks both ends; close both.

The ten-minute setup

Google / Gmail for business. Open your Google account settings → Security → 2-Step Verification, and follow the prompts. It offers the phone prompt, the authenticator app, or backup codes.

Microsoft 365. The owner or admin turns on multi-factor in the admin security settings (it is called MFA or per-user MFA); each person then sets up the Microsoft Authenticator app on their own phone.

Email that came with your web hosting (the hosting-email guide explains that setup). Look in your mail or security settings for "two factor" or "two step" — most control panels (cPanel and its equivalents) have it now. If yours does not, that is itself information about the quality of the service.

Start with yourself and any employee who can send money, approve invoices, or manage customer data. The weakest account on the team is the one without it — the takeover only needs one unlocked mailbox.

The two traps

Trap 1: the lost phone. Your phone is now one of your two factors, so losing it without a backup is a lockout. When you set up two-factor, you get backup or recovery codes — a short list of one-time codes. Print them. Keep them where your business records live, not in the phone's notes app. One sheet of paper ends this trap permanently.

Trap 2: "I'll do it after the busy season." The takeover does not wait for the busy season. It takes about ten minutes, it is free on every major provider, and the people who see the attacks every day keep saying the same thing: almost every account that falls was missing this one setting. There is no version of "later" that the scammers respect.
