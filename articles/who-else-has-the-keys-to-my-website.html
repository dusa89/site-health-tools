Who Else Has the Keys to My Website?

The uncomfortable answer: probably more people than you think. Your old web developer, the employee who left last year, the default “admin” account that came with the installer - every one of them may still hold a working key to your website. Research on employee offboarding finds nearly nine in ten former employees keep access to at least one company system after they leave, and one in five businesses has suffered a breach linked to an account nobody remembered to close. Below: how the keys pile up, what the numbers say, and the 20-minute audit that takes them back.

All numbers are reported figures from the cited sources, not promises about your situation - access habits differ business to business. The facts below describe how the key problem works in general; your own login list is the verdict on your business.

The keys you forgot you handed out

Think of every login that can change your website as a key: the hosting account, the domain registrar, the website's own admin area, the business email, the social accounts, the Google Business Profile. Now list who holds each one. For most small businesses the list is longer than expected: the web developer from three years ago who you do not talk to anymore. The employee who left last spring. The default “admin” account the installer created and nobody ever deleted. The one password the whole office knows because it was easier than setting everyone up properly. None of these people broke in. You - or someone who worked for you - handed them a key, and nobody ever asked for it back.

This is different from deciding whether to share access going forward - our web-developer-passwords article covers that decision. This is the audit of the keys that are already out there.

What the research says

The numbers are blunt. A 2026 offboarding report from Zimyo found that 89% of former employees retain access to at least one company application after their departure - and one in five businesses has suffered a breach linked to an orphaned account nobody remembered to close. A separate industry survey found 83% of employees kept access to at least one account from a previous employer, and 47% admitted to using old company passwords after leaving, some of them more than two years later.

The cleanup side is just as weak. BetterCloud's 2026 report on business software found only 27% of organizations automate offboarding at all - companies put more effort into setting someone up than into locking them out - and 18% of enterprises suffered a breach tied directly to an un-offboarded former employee in the past twelve months. Gartner's research puts a number on the lag: only 44% of companies revoke all access rights within 24 hours of someone's departure. The Ponemon Institute found roughly one in five data breaches involves a former employee accessing systems within six months of leaving.

And the famous case makes the mechanism concrete: the 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack - the one that shut down fuel delivery across the US East Coast - started through an old, inactive VPN account that had no second login step. Not a sophisticated break-in. A door that was simply never closed.

One honest note from the research: this is rarely a story about a vengeful ex-employee. Most former employees never touch the old access again. The risk is that nobody can say for certain that they will not - because nobody owns confirming the access is actually gone.

Why small businesses are worse at this

Big companies have an IT department and an offboarding checklist. You have a phone full of texted passwords and a web person who was a freelancer with no handover document. When someone leaves a small business, the “offboarding” is usually a farewell lunch - nobody sits down with the list of logins. Shared passwords make it worse: if the whole office knows the hosting password, one departure means the password is compromised and nobody changes it, because changing it means telling everyone the new one. Our responsibility article breaks down whose job each layer of this really is - the short version is that the key list is yours, not your host's and not your old developer's.

The 20-minute key audit

Do this once, then repeat it every time someone with access leaves. It takes about twenty minutes.

1. Write down every login that can change the site. One page: hosting account, domain registrar, website admin, business email, social accounts, business profile. Next to each, write who has the credentials today. You cannot revoke what you have not listed.

2. Remove the people who are gone. Delete or disable their accounts - do not just change the password and leave the account sitting there. A disabled account cannot be logged into, full stop. Start with the old web developer and every ex-employee.

3. Kill the defaults. If your site still has the “admin” account that came with the installer, delete it or rename it after creating a properly named administrator account. That default name is one of the first things every automated attack tries - our login-attempts article explains the knocking you are probably already hearing.

4. End the shared passwords. Anything more than one person knows is not a secret - it is a rumor. Give everyone their own login so that the next departure is one account to disable, not a password the whole office has to relearn.

5. Put the second login step on the important ones. Hosting, domain registrar, and business email at minimum. A stolen password alone should never be enough - our two-factor article walks through what that second step looks like in plain English.

One chain to check while you are at it: if someone can get into your business email, they can reset the passwords for nearly everything else. Our email-to-website-takeover article shows exactly how that chain works - which is why the email account sits at the top of the key list, not the bottom.

What to ask your web person

Four questions that get a straight answer: (1) “List every admin-level account on my website and tell me who each one belongs to.” (2) “Which of these belong to people who no longer work with us - and can you remove them today?” (3) “Is there still a default admin account from the original install, and can we delete it?” (4) “The next time someone with access leaves, what is the ten-minute checklist so no key gets left behind?” If the answer to the first question takes more than a day to produce, that is itself the finding - and our already-hacked article covers how to check whether any of those old keys were used while you were not looking.
