Your Website Links to Other Businesses. What Happens When They Move?

You have checked that your own pages work. But your site also links to other people's sites — a supplier's catalog, a review page, the city's permit form — and the internet does not sit still. Half of the links cited in U.S. Supreme Court opinions no longer point where they were supposed to. Here is what happens to the links leaving your site, and the 15-minute check that catches them.

One clarification before we start: if the problem is your own pages being dead — links on your site that lead nowhere — that is covered in our guide to why your links die and our 404-page guide. This guide is about the links that leave your building: the ones on your site pointing at other people's websites. Different direction, different owner of the breakage — and the same angry customer.

The short answer: the internet quietly rearranges itself every year — sites redesign, pages move, businesses close and their domains get bought by strangers — and nobody notifies you when a link you published stops working. Your customer clicks a link on your site, lands on a dead page or something unrecognizable, and blames you, not the other site. The fix is a 15-minute click-through once a quarter, plus linking to stable pages in the first place.

The proof that nobody's links are safe

In 2014, researchers at Harvard (Jonathan Zittrain, Kendra Albert, and Lawrence Lessig) examined the web links cited in legal scholarship and in U.S. Supreme Court opinions — the most carefully maintained documents in the country. They found that 50% of the URLs cited in Supreme Court opinions no longer pointed to the information originally cited, and more than 70% in the law journals. Half. At the Supreme Court.

The point is not legal trivia. The point is that link rot is not a "messy website" problem — it is a property of the web itself. Addresses move. If half the citations in the highest court in the country decayed, the "partner links" page your web person built five years ago and nobody touched since is not fine. It is a museum of other people's moves.

The three ways your outbound links die

1. The other site redesigns. Your supplier rebuilds their site and the product catalog page you link to becomes a 404 — or worse, it lands on their new homepage, and your customer has to hunt for what you promised was one click away. Nobody emailed you. Why would they? They do not know you link to them.

2. The business closes and the domain gets bought. The manufacturer you link to goes under. Their domain expires. Someone buys it at auction — and now your link sends your customers to a page of ads, or a spammy "this domain for sale" notice, or occasionally something much uglier. Your site is now lending its credibility to a stranger, and your customer thinks you chose to send them there.

3. The page just moves. A city permit form, a review site, a professional association — someone reorganizes the site and the page you link to gets a new address. Your link 404s or lands on a generic "page not found." This is the most common version and the least dramatic: nobody's fault, but it is your customer who hit the wall.

Notice what all three have in common: the breakage happens over there, but the customer experienced it over here. They were on your site. Your site sent them somewhere broken. As far as they are concerned, that is your failure.

Why customers blame you (and they are not wrong)

Customers do not think in terms of domains and HTTP codes. They think: "I was on the plumber's site, I clicked their link to the water heater company, and it went to a page of garbage." You did not break anything, but you are the one who invited them to click. A link on your site is a recommendation — an implicit promise that what is on the other end is worth their time. Every broken outbound link spends a little of the trust the rest of your site built.

There is also the quiet version: a link that lands on a different page than the one you meant — a redesigned site where your "parts catalog" link now lands on a homepage with no catalog in sight. The customer does not report this. They just click around, get confused, and associate that confusion with your business, because your site is where the journey started.

The 15-minute outbound-link check (once a quarter)

This belongs in the same quarterly routine as checking your own dead links — our maintenance guide lays out the full 10-minute cadence; this is the extra 15 minutes for the links that leave your site.

1. Open your links page(s). Partner links, resources, "useful links," supplier lists — and any links embedded in your content (blog posts, FAQ pages, service descriptions). These are the usual suspects.

2. Click every one, and watch where you land. Not just "does it load" — does it land on what the link text promised? A link that says "manufacturer's warranty form" but lands on a homepage is broken, even though nothing 404'd. Count it.

3. Look for the hijacked. If any link lands on ads, a domain-for-sale page, or content that has nothing to do with the business you meant — pull it today. That one is actively damaging you; it is your site endorsing a stranger.

4. Fix or remove, the same day. Find the new address (Google the business name — it takes a minute) and update the link, or remove the link if the destination no longer serves your customers. The rule from our causes guide applies: a removed link is better than a lying link.

The fix ladder (so they break less often)

1. Link to stable pages, not deep pages. The deeper the page, the more likely it moves. Linking to a supplier's homepage instead of their "spring-2023-catalog.pdf" cuts your future breakage dramatically. Homepages almost never die; PDFs almost always do.

2. Open external links in a new tab. This is the one house rule: when a customer clicks away from your site, keep your tab open behind it. They come back to you instead of having to hit the back button through someone else's site — or losing you entirely. Your web person knows the setting; if they push back, insist: your site should be the one they return to.

3. Prune links that earn nothing. Every outbound link is a maintenance obligation and a small trust risk. The link to the trade association you joined in 2019 and never attend? The badge from an award program that no longer exists? Each one is a future 404 you are responsible for and a check you will have to do again. Keep the links that help customers; delete the decoration.

4. Re-check after your own redesigns. When your site gets rebuilt, outbound links get moved, dropped, or mangled in the migration. The week after a redesign is the highest-risk moment for links — yours and everyone else's — so run the 15-minute check then, not next quarter.

What not to do

Don’t add outbound links as favors. "Link to my cousin's business" is how sites accumulate a decade of dead recommendations. Every outbound link is a promise you are on the hook to keep — treat it like a referral, because that is what the customer thinks it is.

Don’t trust "link checker" tools alone. Automated tools tell you whether a link loads, not whether it lands where it should. A redesigned supplier homepage "loads" — but your customer was promised a catalog. The 15 minutes of human clicking is the check that matters; the tool just finds the ones that 404.

The bottom line

The internet rearranges itself constantly, and half the links in Supreme Court opinions could not survive it. Your outbound links are dying on the same schedule — the only question is whether you find them before your customers do. Fifteen minutes a quarter, stable pages over deep pages, and a link list that only keeps the links that earn their place. Your site made the recommendation; make sure it still deserves it.
