Why Do My Employees Keep Clicking Phishing Emails?

You trained them. You told them not to click. And last Tuesday your bookkeeper nearly wired money to a "vendor" whose bank was suddenly in a different state. Here is the part nobody in the training videos will say: your employees keep clicking because the emails are designed to be clicked — by people who do nothing else for a living, now writing the lures with AI. Verizon's 2026 breach report puts the human element in 62% of all breaches (up from 60%). This is not a hiring problem. It is a design problem — the attack's design, not your people's. And the fix is not another hour of training. It is a small set of rules that make clicking harmless.

1. The short answer, up front

They click because the email did its job. Modern phishing is not the Nigerian prince with broken English — that version is a museum piece. The emails arriving at your business are written cleanly, reference real people, arrive during the workday about work topics, and now (per the 2026 breach data) are routinely drafted with AI assistance. Clicking them is not a character flaw; it is the expected human response to a professional deception campaign aimed at a busy person with a full inbox. The businesses that fix this do not hire smarter people. They change the rules so that a click cannot become a breach.

2. The three hooks — what the click is really about

Every successful phish uses at least one of three levers. Learn to name them and you can spot most attacks in five seconds:

Authority. The email comes from someone the employee must obey — the owner, the boss, the vendor, the IT department, the bank. "Please process this today, I'm in a meeting." The 2026 breach data calls the heavyweight version of this pretexting — building a fake trusted relationship until the victim acts — and it reached 6% of all breaches, increasingly used as the way in to ransomware attacks. The vendor-bank-details wire-fraud email is pretexting with a dollar figure attached — and the mirror image, attackers impersonating your business to your customers, is the same playbook aimed outward.

Urgency. The email manufactures a clock — "account suspended," "payment due today," "review needed before 5pm." Urgency short-circuits the second look. The attacker's whole bet is that a busy employee will act before thinking. If an email says act now, that is the single most suspicious thing in it.

Familiarity. The email looks exactly like the real thing — your actual Microsoft login page, your actual bank's branding, an invoice layout matching a vendor you really use. AI-assisted attackers (the 2026 report documents threat actors using AI help in a median of 15 attack techniques) now personalize at scale: real names, real job titles, real recent events scraped from social media. The grammar test died in 2025. Assume every lure is well-written.

3. Why your small business — not just the big ones

"We are too small to be a target" is the belief that does the attacker's targeting for them. Attacks are automated and volume-driven; the phishing kit does not know your revenue. And small businesses are cheaper to hit: fewer defenses, one bookkeeper with wire access, no security team to call. The FBI's 2026 internet-crime report (2025 data) counted 1,008,597 complaints totaling $20.877 billion in losses — and business email compromise alone accounted for $3.05 billion across 24,768 complaints. That is the category your bookkeeper's near-miss lives in. The biggest check-fraud wave and the fake-invoice industry run on small businesses precisely because the big companies finally locked their doors.

Also: the click is moving to the phone. The 2026 breach data found that in phishing simulations, click rates over voice and text were 40% higher than over email — and Mandiant's 2026 threat report shows voice phishing up to 11% of intrusions while email phishing fell to 6%. The "boss" is increasingly a text message. Train for the phone, not just the inbox.

4. Why training-by-shame does not work

The most common "program" in small business is: send a fake phishing test, shame whoever clicks, maybe threaten a meeting with HR. This fails twice. First, it teaches people to hide mistakes — the employee who clicked will never report the real one, because reporting is how you get in trouble. The breach you never hear about is the expensive one. Second, it trains people to pass the test, not spot the attack — they learn your test vendor's style, and the real attack looks different.

The inversion that works: report, don't delete — and never punish a report. The employee who clicks and then immediately calls IT is your best sensor on the network. Every security team in the world wants a user base that reports fast. You build that by celebrating the reports and deleting the shame. A fake-test click should trigger a 30-second lesson, not a write-up. If the test has consequences, people will not report the real thing.

5. The 10-minute fix order

You do not need a program. You need five rules, written down, repeated once a quarter:

Rule 1: money moves get a second channel. Any instruction to send money, change bank details, or pay a new invoice gets verified by a second channel you already trust — a call to a number you already have, not the number in the email. This one rule kills the $3.05-billion category dead. Put it on the wall of the office that pays the bills. The vendor-bank-details checklist shows exactly how the scam reads.

Rule 2: the report button is sacred. Make reporting one click (most email tools have a report-phishing button — enable it) and make the rule explicit: no one is ever punished for reporting, even if the report is a false alarm, even if they clicked first. Report first, investigate second. This applies double to staff who read the inbox on your behalf — the assistant-access article covers the trusted-helper side; this rule covers the attackers targeting that helper.

Rule 3: multi-factor on email, everywhere. If a click does harvest a password, multi-factor authentication means the password is useless without the phone. The two-factor article covers the setup — it is the cheapest control on this entire list and it neutralizes most stolen credentials outright. (The mechanical side matters too: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC decide whether a spoofed version of your domain even reaches inboxes — the DMARC article explains the trio.)

Rule 4: test to teach, never to shame. If you run simulated phishing tests, the miss must trigger a short lesson on the spot and nothing else. Anonymous aggregates go to management; individual names do not. The metric that matters is report rate, not click rate — a high click rate with a high report rate is a winning security culture.

Rule 5: the 5-second check. Teach one habit, not twenty: before acting on any unexpected email, hover the sender address (not the display name) and ask two questions — "was I expecting this?" and "does the actual address match?" Five seconds. That is the whole technique. It catches the majority of real-world attacks because most attacks still need you to act on something unexpected.

6. What to ask your IT person (plain English)

"Is multi-factor turned on for every email account, including the ones nobody uses?" Orphaned inboxes are the back door — the employee-exit article covers the deprovisioning side.

"Do we have a report-phishing button in everyone's email, and does it actually work?" A button nobody was shown is decoration.

"If someone's email account was taken over today, how would we know?" This is the test of whether monitoring exists at all — the answer connects directly to the "my email is sending messages I didn't write" article, which is what the takeover looks like from the inside.

And the honest summary: the click is not the breach. The click with no second-channel check, no report button, no multi-factor, and a shame culture — that is the breach. Fix the environment and the clicking stops mattering. That is the whole job, and it takes an afternoon.
