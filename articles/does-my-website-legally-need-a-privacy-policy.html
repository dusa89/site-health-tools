Does My Website Legally Need a Privacy Policy?

That little link at the bottom of every website, the one nobody reads, the one your site might not have. Owners ask about it when something scares them: a competitor got fined, a customer asked "what do you do with my email," or their web person added it without explanation. Here is the honest answer, stripped of lawyer talk: if your website collects anything from visitors, you almost certainly need one, and the dangerous part is not being without it. The dangerous part is having the wrong one.

This article describes what privacy laws generally require of small-business websites in the United States. It is not legal advice, and it cannot tell you which laws cover your specific business. The summary of each rule is dated and sourced at the bottom, and the thresholds quoted are the ones in force now, not forever. When your situation involves children's data, health data, or financial data, talk to a lawyer before you rely on anything written here.

If your site collects anything, the answer is almost certainly yes

The word owners stumble over is "collecting." They picture a database of credit cards. The law pictures something much smaller. A contact form asking for a name and an email address is collecting personal information. So is a newsletter signup. So is an online order form, a booking form, a quote request, and the analytics tool quietly counting your visitors. In most of the rules below, "collecting" starts the moment a visitor types anything into your site, or the moment your site remembers them with a cookie.

This is why the question is nearly always answered yes. Most business websites collect something. A policy is the written answer to the question every visitor is entitled to ask: what are you taking from me, and what do you do with it? And note the distinction our doesn't-sell-anything article makes about security: not selling online does not exempt you. The same holds for privacy. A contact form alone is enough to trigger the duties below. (Spam hitting that form is a different problem with a different fix; our contact-form spam article covers that one.)

The three rules that actually reach small businesses

Rule 1: California's commercial-website rule. If your business operates a commercial website that gathers information from visitors, California law requires you to post a privacy policy conspicuously, linked from the site, describing what you collect and with whom you share it. This applies regardless of how small you are. If you take orders or inquiries from anyone in California, and nearly every site does, this rule is pointed at you.

Rule 2: the "say what you do" rule. The Federal Trade Commission is the main federal privacy enforcer, and its weapon is the law against deceptive practices. Saying one thing in your privacy policy and doing another is the classic violation: promising "we never share your information" while your mailing-list tool does exactly that, for example. Nobody needs a specific new statute for the FTC to act; an unfair or deceptive practice is enough. The takeaway for owners is simple and underrated: the policy must describe your actual practices, not the practices you wish you had. Lawsuits are the broader fear behind this article; our can-my-website-get-me-sued guide walks through the other ways a site can land you in legal trouble, beyond data privacy.

Rule 3: the state patchwork and its thresholds. California's big privacy law, the CCPA, applies only to for-profit businesses that clear at least one of three bars: roughly $26.6 million in annual revenue, personal information on 100,000 or more California consumers or households, or half of revenue coming from selling personal information. Most small businesses clear none of these, but there is a trap inside the 100,000 number: it counts devices and identifiers, not just customers. A site running ordinary analytics that gets a few hundred California visitors a day can cross that line in a year without the owner ever realizing it. Checking your analytics for California users over the last twelve months is the fast way to see where you stand. And California is not alone: as of 2026, nineteen US states have their own comprehensive privacy laws, with no single federal law to replace the patchwork. California also has a separate duty that kicks in after something goes wrong: telling customers about a breach. That is a different obligation from the standing policy, and our breach-notification article covers when and how it applies.

The mistake that causes more trouble than having no policy

Here is the part owners get backwards. The most common way to get a privacy policy is to copy one from a big company's website, change the name, and move on. Privacy specialists are blunt about this: a wrong or copied policy can cause more trouble than having none at all. A copied policy describes someone else's business. It promises practices you don't follow, names tools you don't use, and omits the ones you do. That mismatch is precisely what regulators look for under Rule 2. A short, honest policy written in your own words beats a long, impressive, borrowed one every time.

What a real policy covers, in plain words

You do not need lawyer language. You need to answer nine questions in words you would use at the counter:

1. What do you collect? Names, email addresses, phone numbers, order details, whatever your forms ask for.

2. Why do you collect it? To reply to inquiries, to ship orders, to send the newsletter they signed up for.

3. How do you collect it? Through the contact form, the checkout, cookies, your analytics tool.

4. Who else sees it? Your email service, your payment processor, your web host. Name the categories. Payment data deserves its own warning: our storing card numbers article explains why keeping the actual card numbers yourself is a separate, much heavier burden than processing payments.

5. How long do you keep it? Indefinitely, a year, until they ask you to delete it. State the rule you actually follow.

6. How do you protect it? Describe the real steps: passwords, limited access, your host's security. Plain terms.

7. What choices do visitors have? Unsubscribing, asking for their data, asking you to delete it.

8. How can they reach you about it? An email address that someone actually monitors.

9. When did this take effect? The date, updated whenever your practices change.

The cookie banner is a separate job

Many owners treat the little cookie-consent popup as the whole project. It is not. The banner handles one question: permission to store cookies. The privacy policy handles everything else: what you collect, why, and what happens to it. You need the policy whether or not you have a banner, and the banner does not substitute for the policy.

When this stops being a do-it-yourself page

Three situations move you out of "write it yourself" territory and into "get a lawyer": you collect information about children under 13 (a separate federal law, COPPA, with its own strict rules), you handle health information in a medical or wellness business, or you handle financial information beyond ordinary payment processing. Those areas have their own statutes, their own regulators, and penalties that make the general rules look gentle.

What to do this week

Write it in your own words. Describe what your site actually collects and does, using the nine questions above. An hour of honest writing beats a copied page.

Date it and link it from every page. Put the link in your footer so it appears site-wide. An unlinked policy is barely a policy.

Audit your own forms. Every field on your contact form is a promise you should be able to explain. If you ask for a phone number you never call, remove the field or explain why you keep it.

Update it when you change tools. Add a chat widget, a new mailing list, a booking system, and the policy is out of date. Tie the update to the change: new tool, updated policy.

The link at the bottom of the page is a small thing. What it says about how you treat people's information is not. Write it like you mean it, keep it true, and it becomes one more reason customers trust you with their business.
